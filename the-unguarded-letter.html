What They Wrote to Each Other

The unguarded letter — and how we know it was different

A reading in three faces

A soldier wears a different face for each reader. The Hubbell brothers left enough letters — to their mother, to their sister, and to one another — that we can watch each face change, name by name, and prove the change is real.

One Man, Three Letters

April 1862 — the Virginia Peninsula

Consider a single week in April of 1862. Henry Hubbell, the eldest brother, is somewhere on the Virginia Peninsula with the 34th New York, the siege of Yorktown grinding on a few miles off. That month he writes eight letters that still survive — four to his mother in Champlain, four to his brothers already in uniform. They leave his hand within days of one another. They cross the same picket lines, ride the same mail. And they do not say the same things.

To his mother he is careful. He is well; he has taken no cold; here is money; please do not worry. To his brothers he is another man — longer-winded, blunter about the fighting, freer with the fear and freer with the swagger. The war is the same war in every letter. The voice is not the same voice.

This is an old and human fact — that we speak differently to the people we are trying to protect than to the people standing beside us. What is unusual is that the Hubbell letters let us watch it happen, and that the collection has been instrumented finely enough to measure it. Every letter carries small structured markers — whether it speaks of battle, of a wound, of death; the temperature of its feeling. Sort the letters by who was meant to read them, and the difference stops being an impression. It becomes a number.

A note on the eight-fold gap

Of every letter the brothers sent one another, nearly a quarter carry the rawest emotional register our reading recognizes. Of the letters they sent their mother, three in a hundred do. That is not a subtle lean — it is roughly eight to one in where a soldier put his unguarded feeling. The counts per writer are small, a caution we keep in view; but the direction holds for all four brothers independently, and that is where the confidence lives.

The Appetite for Battle

April 1862 — Henry, before Yorktown

In the same days of that April, Henry wrote to his mother and to his brother Charles. To his mother he made himself small and safe, and let the homesickness show. To Charles he let the other thing show — the strange exhilaration a young man feels near the guns, the appetite he could not admit to the woman who had already lost her husband and was about to lose him.

To his mother, the guns are absent and the farm is near. To his brother, the guns are a tonic — and he half-wishes Charles were in it with him. Neither letter is a lie. They are the same week, told to two people he loved differently.

What He Would Not Tell His Mother

December 1861 — Alexander, near Baltimore

Alexander was seventeen, a corporal near Baltimore, and had just been paid for the first time. To his mother he sent almost all of it, and the tenderest letter he would ever write. To his brother Charles, three weeks later, he sent something he could not have put in his mother's envelope: a young man's dark arithmetic about whether he would come home at all — and, in the same breath, a summons for his brothers to follow him into the army.

The letter to his mother is the shortest kind of brave — a boy making his mother a gift and hiding his fear inside a psalm. The letter to his brother is the other kind — the fear said plainly, and turned outward into a challenge. It is more than twice as long. He could afford the length, and the darkness, because Charles was a soldier too.

“I hardly ever expect to see home.”
Alexander, to his brother — December 29, 1861

The Same Day, Two Letters

September 30, 1862 — Charles, Plattsburgh Barracks

The clearest proof is a single day. On September 30, 1862 — not yet three weeks a soldier, still at the Plattsburgh barracks — Charles learned that his brother Henry had been wounded at Antietam. He sat down and wrote two letters. One to his mother. One to his brother Alexander. They are dated the same day, about the same news. They are not the same letter.

To his mother, Charles reaches for comfort — a prisoner is safer than a soldier; it may be all for the best. To his brother he drops the consolation and says the true thing: we must take our chances — and turns at once to the practical work only a brother could be asked to do, to go and make enquiries in Henry's company. The dread and the errand went to Alexander. Only the reassurance went home.

Three Faces at Once

February 1862 — Henry, at Camp McClellan

Once, the record is complete enough to see all three faces in a single stretch of days. In February 1862 Henry wrote to his mother, to his sister Fannie, and to his brother Charles within a week. Read together, they are almost a demonstration — the good son, the cheerful correspondent, and the blunt soldier, all the same man.

To his mother, a boy proving his virtue — he keeps away from the cards, the drink, the lottery. To his sister, a cheerful bearer of good war news. To his brother, the unglamorous truth of how the men actually lived: eating dog fashion, a pound of dirt with every meal, good enough for soldiers. Three readers, three Henrys, one week.

How We Know

None of this required a hunch. It required an archive that had been turned into something a question could be asked of.

A preserved collection, instrumented

These 272 letters were read closely and encoded — each one tagged with who wrote it, who received it, when, and whether it spoke of battle, a wound, a death, of money, of politics, and with a reading of its emotional weather. Nothing was invented; the letters were simply recorded in a form a machine can also read.

Which turns a question that would take weeks by hand — did these brothers write differently to each other than home? — into a few seconds of counting. A short script, _sibling_voice_analysis.py, sorts every letter by its reader and tallies the difference. It found what the diptychs above show: to a brother, the letters run longer, name danger far more often, and carry the rawest feeling by a factor of eight.

And it found the honest complication — that the brothers did not simply get “the truth” while their mother got a softened version. The careful, for-the-record reporting often went home. What went sideways, brother to brother, was length, danger, and raw feeling. The right way to say it is not candor versus concealment. It is an asymmetry of register — the same war, carried in a different voice, calibrated to who was reading. The data corrected the easy story into a truer one.

That is the point of instrumenting an archive: it lets a small, faithfully preserved collection answer questions no single reading could, and answer them in a way anyone can check. Every number on this page regenerates from one script over open data. This is not trust the historian’s ear. It is interpretation you can audit.
