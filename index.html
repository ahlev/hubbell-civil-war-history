The Hubbell BrothersCivil War Letters, 1861–1865

A widowed mother in upstate New York sent four sons to the Union Army. They wrote home about marches, money, sickness, and fear. Three did not survive the war. Their letters did.

The LettersWhat They Wrote Home

Their father, Frederick Augustus Hubbell, had died in 1853 when Alexander was nine years old. Frances McNeil Hubbell raised four sons and a daughter alone in Champlain, on the Canadian border of New York. When the call came for volunteers in 1861, she sent all four.

What followed was not the heroic correspondence of myth. The brothers wrote about diarrhea, back pay, the cost of getting a shirt washed. They wrote about men dying of fever in hospitals where the surgeons had no medicine. They wrote about money — eight dollars sent home, five dollars begged from their mother when the army failed to pay them for months. They wrote, above all, to stay connected to a home they were no longer sure they would see again.

You know, Mother, what it is to be away from home, sick among strangers and to be without money. I will not try to describe my feelings when I ask for this... Do you suppose you could spare me five dollars, if not, less will do.— Alexander, Hospital at Harpers Ferry, September 27, 1862

Henry stopped writing in July 1862. The family learned his name had appeared on a casualty list after Antietam. Conflicting reports followed — wounded in the leg, or killed outright. A soldier eventually reported seeing his name on a headboard at a grave. Charles would later write: "I think there is not the least doubt that Henry was killed."

In August 1864, all three surviving brothers were hospitalized simultaneously — Alexander with scurvy near Atlanta, James with exhaustion at Frederick City, Charles in a convalescent department near Harper's Ferry. Alexander found this darkly amusing.

Please excuse me, Mother, but I did have a hearty laugh when I took in the idea of your three hopeful sons being in the hospital at the one and same time.— Alexander, Hospital at Chattanooga, August 30, 1864

The PreservationFive Generations, One Story

The letters survived in a trunk in a basement for eighty years. Water-stained and fading, they might have been lost entirely. Instead, each generation chose to carry them forward.

1861
The Brothers
Henry, Alexander, James, and Charles write home from camps, marches, hospitals, and battlefields across Virginia, Maryland, Tennessee, Georgia, and Louisiana.
1947
Gladys Sands Hubbell
Alexander's daughter-in-law spends two and a half years transcribing deteriorating originals by typewriter — preserving every misspelling, every grammatical choice, noting every passage too damaged to read. A true labor of love.
1996
Fred Alexander Hubbell Jr.
Alexander's grandson writes an introduction identifying the four brothers, their regiments, and their fates — placing the story in context for future generations.
2025
Bruce Levitt
Scans the typewritten collection for digital preservation, ensuring the physical pages are no longer the only record.
2025–26
The Descendants
Modern technology meets handwritten history. 274 letters are transcribed into structured data, text-mined for health patterns, financial references, emotional arcs, and the web of people and places that shaped these soldiers' lives. What emerges are patterns no single reader could see — and a family made vivid again.
The following letters were transcribed by my mother, from the original hand written pages, in what was a true labor of love. The originals had been stored in a trunk in a basement, and many of them were somewhat water stained.— Fred Alexander Hubbell Jr., Atlanta, October 28, 1996
