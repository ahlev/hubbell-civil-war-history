The Hubbell BrothersCivil War Letters, 1861–1865

The Letters
Four brothers wrote home from the Civil War. Their words survived. Between 1861 and 1865, Henry, Alexander, James, and Charles Hubbell — sons of a widowed mother in upstate New York — wrote letters from camps, hospitals, marches, and battlefields. They are not memoirs or histories. They are unfiltered, unedited, and unaware of how the story ends. Read together, they paint a portrait of the war as it unfolded, one family at a time.
The Preservation
A handwritten record of the war, passed down across five generations. One brother was killed at Antietam. Another died on the journey home after the war had already ended. A third survived for years before his death was attributed to wartime disease. Only Alexander lived to old age. The letters outlasted them all — forgotten in a trunk in a basement for eighty years, water-stained and fading. A daughter-in-law rescued them by typewriter. A grandson placed them in context. A son-in-law digitized them. Now, Alexander Hubbell Levitt — Alexander's great-great-grandson and namesake — visited Gettysburg to trace ancestral footsteps and turned to modern technology to reconnect with their words.

The LettersWhat They Wrote Home

Their father, Frederick Augustus Hubbell, had died in 1853 when Alexander was nine years old. Frances McNeil Hubbell raised four sons and a daughter alone in Champlain, on the Canadian border of New York. When the call came for volunteers in 1861, she sent all four.

What followed was not the heroic correspondence of myth. These were young men writing to their mother. They wrote about being sick among strangers, about the ache of months without a letter from home. They worried about money — eight dollars sent home from a month's pay, five dollars begged back when the army failed to pay them at all. They described not just battles but the long, grinding weeks between them — the monotony, the mud, the waiting.

What makes these letters extraordinary is not any single revelation but the accumulation of ordinary detail across four years. Read together, they become something larger — an unedited record of what the Civil War felt like from inside it, written by men who did not yet know how their own story would end.

Henry stopped writing in July 1862. The family learned his name had appeared on a casualty list after Antietam. Conflicting reports followed — wounded in the leg, or killed outright. A soldier eventually reported seeing his name on a headboard at a grave. Charles would later write: "I think there is not the least doubt that Henry was killed."

In August 1864, all three surviving brothers were hospitalized simultaneously — Alexander with scurvy near Atlanta, James with exhaustion at Frederick City, Charles in a convalescent camp near Harper's Ferry. Their mother, who had already lost one son and spent two years fearing for the others, received this news. Alexander wrote to reassure her — in his own way.

You know, Mother, what it is to be away from home, sick among strangers and to be without money. I will not try to describe my feelings when I ask for this... Do you suppose you could spare me five dollars, if not, less will do.— Alexander, Hospital at Harpers Ferry, September 27, 1862
Please excuse me, Mother, but I did have a hearty laugh when I took in the idea of your three hopeful sons being in the hospital at the one and same time.— Alexander, Hospital at Chattanooga, August 30, 1864

The PreservationA Trunk in a Basement

After the war, the letters were bundled and put away. They sat in a trunk in a basement for eighty years — water-stained, fading, and largely forgotten. They might easily have been lost entirely. Instead, a remarkable chain of stewardship carried them forward.
