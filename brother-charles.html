Born 1843 in Champlain, New York. Enlisted September 1862. Served through the Shenandoah Valley campaign and the battles of Winchester and Cedar Creek. Survived the war, but died in 1875 — his death attributed to disease contracted during service.

Wartime Footprint

Too Honest

September 1862 — Plattsburgh, New York

Charles was the third Hubbell brother to enter the Army. By September 1862, Henry was already dead at Antietam — though the family didn't yet know it — and Alexander had been in the field for a year. Charles was nineteen. He went to Plattsburgh to enlist in the 153rd New York Volunteers, and in the very first minutes of his military career, he nearly ended it.

The Army required perfect vision. Charles was near-sighted. When Dr. Foote examined him, Charles admitted the deficiency outright. A family acquaintance named Mr. Averill, who had helped arrange his enlistment, intervened — telling the doctor that Charles had two brothers in the Army and one at West Point. The doctor relented and signed the papers.

Mr. Averill told me I was too honest, to tell the Dr. that I was near sighted. September 9, 1862 — Plattsburgh, NY

Averill meant it as a mild rebuke. But it reads now as a summary of Charles's character: precise, straightforward, inclined to state things as they were rather than as they needed to be. He reported his barracks in clinical detail — stone building, bunks four high, straw mattresses. The diet: pork, beef, potatoes, bread. He was made 1st Corporal almost immediately, a sign that someone recognized competence even in a near-sighted teenager.

Mother's Voice

He seems to be quite contented so far — of course he knows nothing of hardships.

Frances Hubbell, September 21, 1862

Searching for Henry

September – December 1862

Charles had barely settled into barracks when the news from Antietam arrived. Henry — the eldest, the first to enlist, the one who had counseled Alexander to stay home — was reported wounded. No one knew where he was. The family grasped at fragments: a man named "Big Shaw" said Henry had been shot in the thigh. A rumor circulated that he was a prisoner at Richmond.

Charles, characteristically, began investigating. In a letter to Alexander on September 30, he relayed what he had gathered and then reached for a theory that might offer comfort: if Henry was wounded, he couldn't run, so he was probably captured. And prisoners, Charles reasoned, were safer than soldiers in the field.

It may be all for the best. September 30, 1862 — Plattsburgh, NY

It was wishful thinking disguised as logic. Over the next three months, Charles continued to trace leads. By December, he had tracked down the source of the McDonald story — the rumor that Henry had been seen alive after the battle — and performed what amounted to a forensic analysis: "McDonald's report might have started at Champlain. Somebody might have wrote to their friends in the army about him." The trail was circular. Nobody had actually seen Henry alive. Charles did not say so directly, but the implication was clear.

Mother's Voice

I expect Charles tomorrow. It is heart breaking to think that I must so soon part with him.

Frances Hubbell, September 28, 1862

Camp Near Alexandria

November 1862 – Spring 1863

By November, Charles had deployed to Virginia. His regiment camped near Alexandria, within sight of Washington, and Charles entered the long grind of garrison life. His letters from this period are meticulous inventories: the cost of boots ($4.50), the allotment sent home ($10 a month), the number of men on guard duty (21 men, 2 corporals, 2 sergeants), the quality of food, the temperature of the tents.

He observed the soldiers around him without moralizing. "Nearly every man swears," he wrote to his mother. "There are a good many hard cases here. There are quite a number of decent men here also." He noted that James Beaumont had died of fever. He reported that rubber blankets were essential. He described the convalescent camp near Alexandria — ten to twelve thousand men, sick and wounded, living in a separate city of misery adjacent to his own. Two men in his company shot their own fingers off — "accidentally," he wrote, though the army had another word for it. Another shot off two fingers and a thumb.

What distinguished Charles from the other letter writers in the family was his eye for institutional detail. He saw the systems, not just the suffering. In a December letter, he reported that of the twelve men in his tent, only three could write. He and the other two literate soldiers ghostwrote letters for the rest — "They dont tell you anything to write so you write what you have a mind to." He described the soldiers' cemetery near camp: bodies buried as close as possible in straight rows, each with a white-painted headboard bearing name, company, and regiment. Alexander visited at Christmas, and Charles noted the contrast without sentiment: "Alek says he does not have enough to eat always — lives on fat pork & hard crackers. Alek said it made him feel bad to see how comfortable we were."

His longest letter, written on December 8, 1862, captures the texture of his world in extraordinary detail: bounty fraud, chaplain character studies, the price of stamps, the hatred of emancipation among the rank and file. Charles reported this last without commentary — "The soldiers here all seem to hate the niger" — neither endorsing nor condemning, simply recording what he saw. His regiment would prove overwhelmingly pro-McClellan in the 1864 election, with Charles one of only two Lincoln voters in his company.

Historical Context

The 153rd New York was recruited from Clinton and Essex Counties in upstate New York — border country along the Canadian line, far from abolitionist centers. Many soldiers viewed the war as a fight to preserve the Union, not to end slavery. Lincoln's preliminary Emancipation Proclamation (September 1862) and the final version (January 1, 1863) caused widespread resentment in regiments like the 153rd, where enlisted men saw themselves fighting for the Constitution, not for Black freedom.

A Name on a Headboard

April 27, 1863

For seven months, the Hubbell family had lived in a state of suspended grief — Henry wounded, Henry missing, Henry possibly a prisoner, possibly alive, possibly dead. No definitive word. Charles had traced every lead to its dead end.

Then, in late April 1863, Abram Fry of the New York artillery told Charles he had seen Henry's name written on a headboard at Antietam. A grave marker. Physical evidence. Charles reported it to his mother with the bluntest statement he ever committed to paper:

I think there is not the least doubt that Henry was killed. We would have heard from him long before this if he had been living. April 27, 1863 — Alexandria, VA

Then the letter breaks. Somewhere in the text — possibly Charles quoting his mother's letter back to her, possibly the grief he had been holding for seven months finally erupting — a voice cries out:

Dear, dear Henry, my darling boy. Oh, what a pall is over my heart, and must be while I am in this vale of tears. April 27, 1863

Whether these are Mother's words quoted, or Charles's own anguish finally surfacing in language he would never otherwise use, it is the rawest passage in the entire collection. The methodical brother, the man who counts casualties and itemizes expenses, suddenly sounds like someone drowning.

Historical Context

After Antietam, burial details worked for days to inter the dead. Union soldiers were typically buried in shallow graves near where they fell, sometimes with a crude wooden headboard bearing a name — if the body could be identified. Many could not. Of the approximately 2,100 Union soldiers killed outright, hundreds were buried as unknown. When the Antietam National Cemetery was established in 1867, remains were gathered from the surrounding fields; 1,836 were reinterred, of whom 578 could not be identified. The headboard Abram Fry reported seeing may have been lost or misattributed during this process. Henry Hubbell has no known grave.

Chancellorsville

May 1863

In early May 1863, the 153rd New York was thrown into the Battle of Chancellorsville. The regiment's losses were severe: 73 total — 9 killed, 43 wounded, 21 missing. Company H alone lost 6 men. Lester Bond was shot through the forehead during a bayonet charge. The orderly was blown into the air by a rebel shell. The 11th Corps, on their flank, routed entirely.

Charles's account of the battle reads like an after-action report: three charges made, positions held and lost, casualties tallied by company and regiment. He describes the fighting with the same precision he brought to barracks layouts and guard schedules. But the numbers tell their own story. A regiment that began with hundreds was being whittled down engagement by engagement.

Historical Context

Chancellorsville (May 1–3, 1863) was Lee's most audacious victory. Stonewall Jackson's flank attack on the 11th Corps triggered a rout that collapsed the entire Union right. The 153rd NY, positioned nearby, fought through the chaos. Confederate losses included Jackson himself, mortally wounded by friendly fire. Union casualties exceeded 17,000.

Capitol Hill

July 1863 – February 1864 — Washington, D.C.

After Chancellorsville, Charles was transferred to Washington. For the next seven months, he served as a guard at the Capitol Hill Barracks — Camp Davis — while the war raged elsewhere. Gettysburg happened in July 1863. The fall of Vicksburg. The New York Draft Riots. Chickamauga. Charles guarded prisoners and wrote home about draft dodgers and Congressional debates.

The work was not dramatic, but it was revealing. His regiment guarded two prisons and the railroad depot. Every night, men cleared by the examining board were marched under heavy guard to barracks. At the depot, soldiers caught citizens trying to flee the draft — including, one day, a boy of about nineteen dressed as a captain, only his coat too large for him. When the New York Draft Riots erupted in July, Charles's reaction was dry: "Don't you think New York has been a pleasant place to live in the last few days?" He added that nothing had pleased the soldiers so much in a long time as the draft itself — the only objection being that men with money could buy their way out. "$300 doesn't grow on every bush."

In February 1864, he escorted four hundred rebel prisoners to Point Lookout, the sprawling camp at the mouth of the Potomac — eight thousand men behind a board fence eight feet high, sentinels walking the top. A private in the regiment had accepted two hundred dollars to let a prisoner escape; the rumor was he would be shot. Charles described the military punishment called bucking and gagging with the clinical precision of a field manual: stick in the mouth, hands tied under the knees, the man unable to move. "Niggers are punished in this way in the South," he noted — drawing the connection without stating its implications.

He attended a session of Congress and heard a debate on confiscation — one long speech against it by a Democrat, part of one in favor by a Republican. He watched a false-muster fraud exposed in his own regiment: men had been counted in multiple companies, bounty drawn for soldiers who did not exist. He noted that the most profane man in the company took communion, then said it was for the congregation. "I know three of our Co. who attend church regularly for the purpose of seeing some ladies."

When word arrived that Alexander had survived Gettysburg, Charles wrote home with relief: "Am glad that Alex came safely out of that Gettysburg fight. Until I rec'd your letter did not know anything about him." The brothers were scattered across three theaters now. Information traveled slowly, and each lived in ignorance of the others' fate for weeks at a time.

Mother's Voice

I rejoice that Charles was not in the last battle in Virginia. His reg't., fortunately was not among those sent out from Washington to the front at that time.

Frances Hubbell, November 15, 1863

Mother was not merely grateful. She was working behind the scenes to keep Charles safe. Letters to the regimental surgeon. Correspondence with Mrs. Strain, wife of the regiment's lieutenant colonel. A quiet campaign conducted through the network of officers' wives and family connections that ran parallel to the Army's chain of command.

Historical Context

Frances Hubbell's campaign to protect Charles through Mrs. Strain was not unusual — it was how the system worked. Officers' wives formed an informal network that paralleled the Army's official chain of command, and families who could access it gained real advantages: favorable assignments, transfers to safer duties, early information about movements. The Union Army had no formal mechanism for compassionate reassignment; a mother who had already lost one son could not petition the War Department to spare another. Instead, she worked through personal connections — the regimental surgeon, the lieutenant colonel's wife, the community notable who had arranged the enlistment. These networks were invisible in official records but decisive in individual lives. Charles's transfer to a clerk position at headquarters was the direct result of his mother's sustained correspondence, conducted across hundreds of miles, over months, while she was simultaneously managing a farm, reading casualty lists, and grieving for Henry.

The Clerk at General Banks's Headquarters

March 1864 — New Orleans, Louisiana

In March 1864, Mother's campaign bore fruit. Mrs. Strain had interceded with her husband, Lt. Col. Strain, who arranged for Charles to be permanently detached from the regiment and assigned as a clerk at General Nathaniel Banks's headquarters in New Orleans. Extra pay: forty cents a day. Third-story office with fresh air off the Gulf. His job: copying special orders.

You are at the bottom of this matter. It is a pleasant place here, a good deal better than being in the field. March 6, 1864 — New Orleans, LA

Charles acknowledged the arrangement without shame. His mother had found a way to keep one more son alive, and he accepted it. The office became his home — "in one corner I keep my knapsack and when night comes spread my blankets on the floor and sleep soundly." A carpeted room with a marble mantle and a spigot of water. After eighteen months of tents and guard posts, it must have felt like a different war.

His letters from New Orleans are the most vivid in the collection — not for combat, but for observation. He described the journey south: entering the Mississippi at daylight on a Sunday morning, sugar plantations lining both banks, negro quarters in neat rows. New Orleans itself was a fine city doing almost no business — the cotton presses idle and empty, used to quarter soldiers. He catalogued its strangeness with a Northerner's wonder: cisterns above ground, sidewalks called banquettes, streetcars drawn by mules with no conductor — "trusting to the honesty of the passengers to put their fare in the box." The cemeteries were different, too: "most of the people are buried above ground, either in tombs, or in a wall that has holes in it that look like ovens."

On March 4, he watched Michael Hahn's inauguration as the Free State Governor of Louisiana — five thousand school children singing, cannon fire, fireworks. Reconstruction beginning while the war still burned.

Historical Context

The inauguration Charles witnessed was Lincoln's test case for Reconstruction — an experiment in rebuilding a state government while the war still raged. Under Lincoln's Ten Percent Plan, Louisiana could form a new government once ten percent of its 1860 voters swore loyalty to the Union. Michael Hahn, a German-born Unionist, won the governorship in February 1864 with the support of Union soldiers and loyalist civilians. The ceremony Charles described — five thousand school children, cannon salutes, fireworks — was staged to project normalcy in a city under military occupation. Lincoln privately urged Hahn to consider extending the vote to "some of the colored people," making Louisiana a proving ground for Black suffrage. The experiment ultimately failed: after Lincoln's assassination, Andrew Johnson's lenient policies allowed former Confederates to reclaim power, and Louisiana's Free State constitution was effectively dismantled. But on that March day in 1864, Charles watched something unprecedented — democracy being rebuilt in real time, in a war zone, with the outcome still in doubt.

But the safety was not absolute. One night, a clerk fell drunk from the third-story gallery onto the stone pavement below and was killed. The war took lives in every possible way, even in offices far behind the lines.

Bullets on the Red River

April – May 1864 — Louisiana

In April 1864, General Banks launched the Red River Campaign — an ambitious push into western Louisiana that quickly turned disastrous. Charles's regiment was in the thick of it, fighting at Sabine Crossroads and Pleasant Hill while Charles remained at headquarters in New Orleans. He reported terrible fighting with detached anxiety — the losses were horrific, but they were happening to other men.

Then the posting ended. On April 21, Charles was relieved by special order and told to rejoin his regiment at once. The same handwriting the Colonel had inspected in March and judged adequate — headquarters did not agree. "My penmanship is not good enough is the reason of my going back to the regt," he wrote matter-of-factly. "But I feel well enough about going back as others have failed to suit as well as me. Just before I came two men were relieved and now another new clerk that has come, I think, will be relieved as he does not write very well." The safe office, the marble mantle, the carpeted floor — all of it gone in a single order.

Carrying the mail to General Banks's field headquarters, he boarded a steamer on the Red River. The boat was fired on from the banks. One soldier killed. One wounded. Charles fired his gun into the woods without seeing a target.

As I had my gun I fired it into the woods but did not see any rebs. The bullets came very thick near where I was. Three soldiers that went out after cattle have not been heard from since — supposed to have been captured or killed by guerrillas. May 3, 1864 — Alexandria, LA

It was his first real combat — blind, chaotic, impersonal. He fired without seeing the enemy. Bullets came near him. Three men walked into the Louisiana landscape and never came back. The river itself was "reddish as if mixed with red clay." Charles noted the color the way he noted everything else: precisely, without interpretation.

Historical Context

The Red River Campaign (March–May 1864) was a strategic failure. Banks's army was repulsed at Sabine Crossroads (April 8) and fought a costly rearguard action at Pleasant Hill before retreating. Guerrilla attacks on supply lines and river transports made the withdrawal dangerous. The campaign consumed thousands of lives for no territorial gain.

The Shenandoah Valley

August – September 1864 — Virginia

By late summer 1864, Charles had been transferred to the Shenandoah Valley, where Sheridan's army was pressing to destroy Early's Confederate force and close the breadbasket that fed Lee's army. Charles was assigned to the Brigade Commissary — again behind the fighting line, counting rations instead of carrying a rifle. He wrote home that he was "getting fat, the result of good living."

James had arrived in May, walking into Charles's tent unannounced. "I was much surprised yesterday to see James," Charles wrote. "It will be pleasant for us both to be in the same regt." He noted, with an older brother's eye, that James had not shaved since West Point and had quite a pair of whiskers. They would tent and draw rations together — the first time any two Hubbell brothers had served side by side.

The Valley was rich farming country, and Sheridan intended to destroy it. Charles described the burning with his usual precision: the cavalry had been as far as Staunton, burning barns and grist mills. Troops foraged freely — pigs, sheep, chickens, apples, butter. "There is a Provost Marshall to stop such doings but it is done for all that." Oak fence rails made good fires. A recruit from Freeseville had received $1,202 bounty for one year's service — a fortune that reflected how desperate the army was for men. Charles, who had enlisted for nothing, noted the figure without comment.

On September 19, Sheridan attacked at the Third Battle of Winchester. The fighting was savage. Charles, from his position with the commissary, could see the results: Company H lost four killed and twelve wounded. The regiment lost sixty-five total. James came through unhurt.

James says he was not frightened, but I think he would own up if he was. It was a pretty warm place for green troops. September 21, 1864 — Near Strasburg, VA

It is one of the few moments of dry humor in Charles's correspondence — the older brother gently calling out the younger's bravado. James had never been in a real fight before Winchester. He would be in one more.

“The Rebs Are Here!”

October 19, 1864 — Cedar Creek, Virginia

At dawn on October 19, 1864, Jubal Early's Confederates launched a surprise attack on the Union camp at Cedar Creek. The assault came before the men were fully awake. Charles and James were together when the first shots broke the silence.

James exclaimed, "The Rebs are here!" We jumped up, put on our accoutrements and fell in behind the breastworks. We were ordered to pack up. Had our tents down in a twinkling. The Rebs got behind the breastworks before we knew it. Our brigade was ordered to leave the breastworks and form on the other side of a deep ravine. We jumped over the breastworks very quick as the Rebs were firing into us and we were moving up the hill the bullets came very thick. October 22, 1864 — Near Strasburg, VA

In the chaos of the retreat across the ravine, James was hit. A flesh wound in the thigh or hip. He walked five miles to the field hospital, fashioning a bandage from his own underclothes. James Lucas was shot through the head and arm. Sergeant Pelky from Corbeau was shot through the leg. Alex Douglas, who had been a friend since training, was missing — captured or killed by guerrillas, never heard from again. Lt. Col. Strain — the officer whose wife had helped Mother secure Charles's clerk position — was wounded in the arm.

Company H: seven wounded, two killed. The regiment: fifty wounded, eight killed. The Union line broke, then rallied when Sheridan arrived from Winchester, and drove the Confederates back beyond Fisher's Hill. Charles survived without a scratch.

I am very thankful that I did not get scratched. October 22, 1864
Historical Context

The Battle of Cedar Creek (October 19, 1864) nearly ended in disaster. Early's dawn attack overran the Union camp and drove three corps from the field. Sheridan's dramatic ride from Winchester — later immortalized in poetry — rallied the retreating army. The counterattack destroyed Early's force and effectively ended the Confederate presence in the Shenandoah Valley. Union casualties: 5,665. Confederate: 2,910.

Twenty-Two Men

November 1864

Two weeks after Cedar Creek, Charles wrote again. The numbers told the story. The company — which had started at full strength — was down to twenty-two men. In two fights, Winchester and Cedar Creek, they had lost twenty-three killed and wounded. More than half the company, gone in five weeks.

In the fight bullets flew all around me but I came out without a scratch. Our Co. lost 23 in the two fights out of about 45 men. There are but 22 men in Co. now. November 4, 1864 — Camp at Cedar Creek, VA

In the same letter, Charles reported that his grandmother had died. He noted that James was doing well at the hospital, according to the chaplain. He mentioned that James Lucas — shot through the head and arm at Cedar Creek — would probably not recover. And then, almost as an afterthought, he recorded how he voted:

I sent my vote to Silas, voted for Lincoln. Only two votes for Lincoln in our Co. November 4, 1864

Two votes for Lincoln out of twenty-two men. The 153rd was overwhelmingly McClellan — border-county men who distrusted abolition and wanted the old general back. Charles voted his conscience anyway, quietly, one of two. He didn't explain why. He didn't argue the case. He simply reported it, the same way he reported everything else: as a fact.

Historical Context

The 1864 presidential election was conducted in the field. Soldiers voted by absentee ballot, and the results varied dramatically by regiment. Lincoln won the overall soldier vote roughly 78% to 22%, but individual units — especially those from Democratic border counties — sometimes went heavily for McClellan. Charles's two-of-twenty-two Lincoln votes in Company H represents the extreme end of this divide.

Winter

November 1864 – April 1865 — Camp Russell, Virginia

The last months of the war passed quietly for Charles. The company was too depleted for major action — twenty-two men where there had once been a full complement. They built cabins with stone fireplaces and chimneys. On Thanksgiving, the ration of turkey for three men was so small they traded it for four big hardtacks. "Hard tack better," Charles wrote.

He described himself without vanity: mustache and chin whiskers, round-shouldered as ever, shaving the sides of his face every week or two when he could borrow a razor. He worried about Alexander — "I wish he could get into the Invalid Corps. I think that is the best place for him" — and about whether the war would last long enough to swallow James too. "I hore the war is nearly over," he wrote in December, "but have not much faith that it is."

His mother had been anxious, and his reassurance carried the weary tone of a man who had been writing the same comfort for two years: "Am very, very sorry that you have been so anxious about me, but bad news travels fast."

Savannah

June – July 1865 — Georgia

The war ended in April 1865, but Charles's service did not. In June, the regiment was shipped south for occupation duty in Savannah, Georgia. Charles arrived on June 12 and wrote home in haste — the steamer was leaving that night. The river was obstructed with pilings and forts. A man swimming in the harbor was caught by the tide and nearly carried out to sea before marines rescued him.

The city looks deserted. Three years up Oct 17th. James says he will write next. June 12, 1865 — Savannah, GA

The city looks deserted. Three years up October 17th. James will write next. These three sentences, placed side by side, contain everything: the ruined South, the countdown to freedom, the brother who survived Cedar Creek and was still with him.

A month later, Charles watched Savannah's first Fourth of July since the war. All the fire companies paraded — all Black but one. "The darkeys young and old all turned out." He noted that James was having trouble seeing at night, though his daytime vision was fine. It was a small detail — the kind Charles always recorded — but it hinted at the damage the war had done to bodies that appeared whole. Charles was still counting. Still reporting. Still alive.

What Remained

After the War

Charles came home. He had survived the entire war without a serious wound. The near-sighted teenager who almost failed the eye exam in Plattsburgh had endured three years of service — garrison duty and clerk work and the Shenandoah Valley — and walked away physically whole.

But the war had not finished with him. In 1875, ten years after mustering out, Charles died. His death was attributed to disease contracted during military service — the slow poison that killed more Civil War soldiers than bullets ever did. The precise nature of his illness, like so many details in his story, went unrecorded.

He was the man who counted everything. Casualties, rations, allotments, days to discharge. He counted his company down from forty-five to twenty-two. He counted two Lincoln votes out of twenty-two. He counted three years to the day. What he could not count, and could not outrun, was the cost the war extracted from his body long after the fighting stopped.

Historical Context

Disease killed roughly twice as many Civil War soldiers as combat. Chronic diarrhea, typhoid, malaria, and infections contracted in camp persisted long after discharge. The federal government recognized this through the pension system: veterans or their widows could file claims attributing death to wartime service, even years later. Charles's death in 1875 — ten years after mustering out — followed a pattern repeated across hundreds of thousands of households. Of the Union's 2.1 million soldiers, an estimated 400,000 carried chronic illness home from the war. Many, like Charles, died young without the cause ever being precisely recorded.
