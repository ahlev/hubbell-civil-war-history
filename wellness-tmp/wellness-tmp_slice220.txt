TRANS: May 3rd, 1864
Alexandria, La.

Dear Mother:

I arrived here yesterday and am now back with the regt. Went on board of a steamer, Monday, April 25th at N. Orleans, but after staying some time on her she was ordered not to leave so I went on board of another steamer which left Wednesday afternoon. I carried despatches for Gen. Banks. I occupied a state room with two mail carriers, and we ate at the table with those that paid for a meal. The soldiers on the boat drew their rations and cooked them themselves. The living at the table was nothing extra. We stopped at Port Hudson a short time. The boat was a slow one and it was on the 4th day after we left N. Orleans that we arrived at this place. We ought to have gotten here in two or three days. There were two or three hundred soldiers on board all armed to be prepared for the guerrillas. Only a short time before a steamer on the Mississippi was fired into and two men were killed and 11 wounded. After getting into Red River we were told to report to the gunboat at the mouth of Black River and if necessary a convoy would go with us. On arriving at the mouth of Black River we were told to wait for a convoy which we did one day and night and then as no gunboat was ready to go with us we started without any. There was a great difference in the color of the Red & Black rivers where the Black flows into the Red. The water of the Red is reddish as if mixed with red clay. We passed by some cattle on the shore when the Lieut. Col. commanding ordered the boat to be run ashore and a beef to be shot and dragged on board. One beef was shot and dragged on board with a rope. But three soldiers that went into the woods chasing the cattle after another beef did not come back. The boat whistled and waited for them, but as they did not come back we finally came off without them. It is supposed they were shot or taken prisoners by the guerrillas. This was on the bank of the Red River.

We passed the night at Fort DeRussy and the next morning in convoy with a transport that had three cannon and with a gunboat in the rear we started for this place. When about thirty-five miles from here we had a fight. There was one man shot dead and one wounded on our boat. The rebs had one cannon and the gunboat soon dismantled it. The guerrillas kept up a musketry fire on us some time. As I had my gun I fired it into the woods but did not see any rebs except one who came out of the woods and fired at the gunboat. He had a breastwork on the boat made of boxes of hardtack. As the banks of the river here high we could not see much but those on the hurricane deck had a good view of the rebs. It was there the men were shot on our boat. Two or three bullets came near where I was.

I found four letters for me with the Regt., one from Alex. Alex Douglass sent three to N. Orleans, but as he directed them to the "Provost Marshall" of course I did not receive them. Our Captain thought I was there, and as I did not write to the regt. it was partly on one fault. The regt. look thin in flesh. A. Douglass is not very well. I never saw so much dust as there is here. Then I arrived the regt. were out on a foraging expedition and I never saw such looking men. They were covered with dust half an inch thick when they came in.

I write this sitting on the ground so you must excuse the penmanship. Please write soon. I wish you would send me a newspaper more in a while as there is nothing to read here. Direct your letters to N. Orleans, La., Co. and Regt., of course. The reason why I did not receive the letters you directed to Hd. Qtrs. was because the Co. and Regt. were on the letters.

From your aff. son,

C. F. Hubbell

Dear Mother:

I now hasten to write you a few lines to let you know of my welfare. This is my first opportunity of writing you since I left Stevenson. We have been marching hard — very hard for the last few days.

I rec'd the boots when I reached Bridgeport, Ala. I was very much pleased with them, but most unfortunately they proved a little too small for me (as my used up feet will testify) and I have sold them to yours Lafountain from Champlain for $6.50. I have now an excellent pair of Govt. bootees and I find them much better for marching than boots.

The sugar was most excellent and you can well imagine how much I enjoyed it. I am very much obliged to you for sending it. The stripes are very nice indeed.

We are close upon the enemy. Our advance came upon them this morning but they fell back. I hardly think they are in much force. We have between thirty-five and forty thousand men here under Hooker. We are I believe on the extreme right of the army.

I will write you as often as possible. Culver Bostwick is here. He reached the regt. yesterday morning and he wishes me to tell his friends that he would write with me but that he has drawn a new gun and is too busy cleaning it. The mail is going out in a moment and I must close.

Your aff. son,

Alex F. Hubbell

TRANS: May 10th, 1864
Camp near Buzzard's Roost, Ga.

Dear Mother:

I wrote you a few days since but will not write you again that you may not be over anxious about me in these perilous times. The 1st & 2nd brigades of our Div. were engaged with the enemy at this place Sunday. We should have been in but early that morning we (3rd Brig.) were sent out as a support to a Div. of Cavalry on a reconnaissance and did not rejoin our Div. until late in the evening. The firing had just ceased when we came up. Buzzard's Roost is a high rocky ridge and where we are is only accessible by a narrow gap through the high rocky ledge with which its sides are covered. Our men drove the rebels nearly to the crest of the hill when they came upon them in great force posted behind rocks and trees and in the ledges from which they fired upon our men such a withering fire that they were obliged to halt, but managed to hold their ground until all our wounded were removed and, I believe, left the dead, when they fell back to their original position. The Third Div. of our Corps made a similar attempt lower down with like effect. I think both the attacks were but feints to attract the attention of the enemy from other portions of the army. A large force of our army is now in rear of the enemy. I think the Johnnies will have to "up and git" before a great while. Quite a heavy cannonade has been going on this afternoon on the left of our line. We were working all last night building breastworks, as it was feared the enemy might make a break this way and thus get between the two main portions of our army. I think you will hear glorious news from this army before a great while — even before this letter reaches you.

A dispatch was read to the troops today stating that the Army of the Potomac had thrashed the rebs and driven them ten miles and Gen. Sherman added "let us do likewise." I vote and trust we shall drive them ten times ten miles.

Do not be over anxious about me, dear Mother. I am in God's hands. I hope I shall come out of these battles all right. All the boys from our way are well. Culver Bostwick stands the marches like a little hero. The first day he joined us we marched twenty odd miles. It was a pretty good beginning, I thought. Those new boots used up my feet so that I was obliged to march two days in rear of the column and one day was carried in an ambulance. I am happy to say that I am all right now. Our men are busy today strengthening the breastworks. The rebs would find it hard if they attacked us here. We are somewhat isolated from the rest of the army and this is a very important position. That is the reason of our strengthening it so. I will write you all the news as soon as I can find out anything definite.

Please write often. Give my regards to Grandmother and Silas and all friends. Tell Miss Fannie I am patiently awaiting a letter from her. I will write her as soon as I can find opportunity. The cannonade is quite brisk on the left. I expect they are having a big fight. God grant that we have the victory. Love to all.

Your aff. son,

Alex F. Hubbell

P.S. Have you heard from James and is Charley still at New Orleans? I have two postage stamps left.

TRANS: May 20th, 1864
Camp near Kingston, Ga.

Dear Fannie:

"At length," I can hear you say, "at length he has found time to write me a letter. Right then, indeed he should, I should think. Not a word has he written since he left on furlough." Now, Miss Fannie, haven't I nursed a right? I will plead guilty to all charges and beg ten thousand pardons, but cannot under existing conditions promise to do any better in future, but will promise to do my best. Now won't you accept my poor apologies and write me a good long letter? You see your silence has brought me to terms.

We are, I suppose you are aware, in close pursuit of Johnson's army. Our advance has had several little brush brushes with the enemy's rear since we started. Yesterday the rebs made a stand where I am now writing. We moved upon them about three P.M. in line of battle, but when we reached here the enemy were found wanting, having again fallen back. Their only object was, I suppose, to check our advance as long as possible. We are resting today awaiting the construction of a bridge which the rebs burned yesterday. You can have little idea how grateful this little rest is to us after being so constantly on the move for the last three weeks. We came late and rise while the stars are shining and march all day under a scorching sun with a load you would think would kill a mule. But then we do not always have such rough times. T

The name of the late battle which we fought, our loss, the loss of the enemy, and all the little particulars I am entirely ignorant of having seen no paper of a date later than the battle. Rumors are in abundance but I have learned not to put much confidence in them. Of the battle all I know is what transpired immediately around me and that is a very small part of a battle. The line of battle was very long and I am thinking it will be a hard thing to settle upon a name for it. The "eastern men are learning to respect the Star Corps (20th)."

I was astonished — shocked yesterday to read in a paper Mr. Hoyle sent that Mr. Bass was shot and probably fatally. Now sad! I think Dodds must have been deranged. Who will now teach the old Academy? Nor Mrs. Bass! His wound I have but little doubt is fatal. I have known men shot through the right lung to liver, but do not recollect of one surviving shot through the left lung. Mr. Bass will have one advantage that we poor fellows do not have out here — that is good nursing and the best medical treatment. One of our men the other day, a brutal fellow, when we were casting over the battle field picked up a rebel leg and foot and shook it in our faces with some disgusting remark. I wanted to knock the fellow down with the butt of my gun.

My paper says halt. I send this letter with my last stamp. The 153rd has been engaged, I see. Charley, I believe, was not there. I rec'd a letter from him a few days since. He was then awaiting the boat to join his regt. Tell Mother to keep a stout heart. All our lives are in God's hands. Love to all.

Your aff. Brother,

Alexander

P.S.— Bostwick is a bully soldier. Takes a battle very philosophically and stands the marches first rate. He is a great comfort to me. We mess together. He was somewhat astonished at the mode of proceeding in the late battle. Said he thought they came out in an open field and fought it out. The late battle ground was a perfect wilderness.

Dear Mother:

By the kind providence of God I am permitted to write you again, and inform you that I am still uninjured. We have been fighting nearly every day since I last wrote you and working the greater portion of every night (and sometimes the whole) entrenching ourselves in positions gained the previous day. I shall not attempt to give you any detailed acct. of our proceedings. Suffice it to say that everything thus far is proceeding favorably. During the last week we have driven the enemy from two strong lines of entrenchments and are now burrowing away at him in his third. Yesterday was the Holy Sabbath, and I suppose, was passed by you in quiet and meditation. But far different was it with the 60th N.Y. Vets. We were the advance regt. of the division yesterday, being deployed as skirmishers, and were kept engaged with the enemy from two P.M. until after ten o'clock last night — when we were relieved.

The list of killed and wounded in our regt. is beginning to pile up fearfully. None of the boys from Champlain have been injured. Our Regt. was relieved from the front line this morning by the 2nd, and we now rest a little in the rear of the line of battle but not entirely out of range as the whistle of an occasional bullet plainly tells us. Our batteries are keeping up a pretty steady fire. The skirmish lines are also pretty hotly engaged. The rebel sharpshooters are at present keeping up a pretty lively fire on one of our batteries on the hill just above us, but with not much effect.

Please write often. I will write as often as possible. Keep a stout heart. We are all in God's hands. May He grant us all submission to His will.

Have you heard from James yet? His silence is singular and I think very wrong. The reason of it I think is that he feels his failure keenly and he wishes to make something of a mark in the world before he writes you, so that he may be able to compensate you for your disappointment in his failure. I received your letter of May 21st a few days since, also a N.Y. Tribune and papers from Mrs. Boyle. Please write upon the margin of your papers how you all are as I receive the papers oftener than the letters. The mail seem to be delayed at Chattanooga, I suppose from the difficulty of transportation. Love to all. I hope I will come out of this campaign all right, but if God orders it otherwise may we all meet in heaven. My home and trust is in Jesus our Saviour. Much love to Frances and tell her I shall if possible write her soon.

Your aff. son,

Alex F. Hubbell

We are still encamped on the Mississippi; how long we shall remain here I cannot tell. I heard this morning that it was probable that we should not remain here long. The location is a good one, and if we are to remain in camp this summer I should not object to remaining here.

I have had but one trial of marching. That was out on a foraging expedition for corn. At night I sprawled out on my tent floor feeling that I had done a pretty hard day's work. I stood it pretty well considering I had used my legs so little since I have entered the service. We went to a small town at two great roads and filled our wagons from a French woman's barn. The men are nearly all off to war. Four barns, I think, filled our train of one hundred wagons. They talk about the rebels starving. It cannot be so here. One of our company called at a house and was hospitably entertained with cornbread and syrup, part of which he brought out in his hands. They said they had not seen wheat flour for about two years. There is considerable sugar in the mills along the river but it does not benefit us any. Our allowance is one spoonful per day. Negroes were quite plenty in the town. All spoke French like their masters. In front of some houses there were squads of eight or ten all sitting as radiantly as a negro. We saw numbers of great and little pigs, chickens and vegetable gardens, pretty well filled, all of which tempted our soldiers very much, but foraging was strictly forbidden.

The houses are not built with much style, though they may be very comfortable; they are mostly low wooden buildings. I have seen some very handsome brick houses with beautiful grounds in front. Nearly all have pretty yards in front which seem to be spontaneous. I do not see why brick buildings are not more common. Clay is so abundant on the river and the Mississippi so handy to float the material down. None of the settlements are far back from the river.

James Sweeney has lately come into our regt. as Surgeon. On Friday and I went over to see him. He was such a little faller when he left our place that I hardly remembered him. I think that if either of us are sick he will be a friend worth having. You know in the army a person does not get much care.

Well, I have scribbled over this paper and not said much. Comment vous portez ma vous! What do you think of this war? Do you watch the Army of the Potomac with interest? O, Grant, go ahead and whip the rebels, is the feeling here. An inconvenient place to write has made me scribble and soil this paper. Will you please excuse it.

Your aff. brother,

James

Dear Mother:

Must try and write you a word at least though I am pretty tired as I was up all night working with pick and shovel in the trenches. Our batteries along the whole line opened up on the rebs this morning but everything has settled down again to its usual opening — i.e. — sharp skirmishing and a shot now and then from the batteries. Movements are now being made that will I think, bring matters to a decided issue soon. I hope we shall be able to bring this campaign to a close soon.

Luther was wounded this morning while we were after a log for the top of the breast works. We had just gone up with several men to an old house from which we intended to get the log. When the Capt. (I was at work with the shovels) ordered me with several others to go on and give them a lift in bringing it down, I had just started when a ball whistled close by and was in succession followed by a yell of agony. I ran forward and had gone but a little ways when I met Luther coming back leaning upon one of the men. You may be sure I felt a little queer. To my anxious inquiries Luther replied that he thought he was not much hurt — that he thought that it was only a flesh wound. And so it proved though it was a pretty bad hurt, but not dangerous. The ball tore a furrow in his right side and passed through the fleshy part of his right arm a little above the elbow. He will probably be laid up two or three months. God in his kindness great kindness has as yet preserved me from all harm. Culver Bostwick is also well. (He have just drawn a ration of whiskey. I gave mine to the boys for two hard crackers. I believe Bostwick did the same. We do not draw whiskey every day — only when we have had an unusually hard time. We were up last night, and the night before and — well, I may say we hardly know what an uninterrupted night's rest is. He will know how to appreciate rest if ever we get it. Culver and I are getting worn down bully thin. We suffer terribly from the heat — and — and terrain — if I must say it.

Of army movements I will not attempt to tell you. You can find out pretty much from the papers. James has not yet written me. I hope he has not forgotten his brother Alex. How do all things flourish in Champlain! and what were to be the doings on the Fourth? There is a likelihood of there being considerable powder burned here, though if burning powder makes Fourth of July we have had fourth of July ever since we started. Love to all. Dear Mother:

I received your letter of June 20th yesterday and the handkerchief enclosed, for which I am very, very much obliged. I can now wipe the perspiration from my brow with some degree of comfort — before I received the handkerchief I was reduced to the necessity of using a portion of an old rebel meal bag.

Your letter gave me a feeling something akin, I suppose, felt by the Israelites of old when they were described as longing for "the flesh pots of Egypt." I hardly think I shall be at home in time to enjoy the cherries and plums with you, but hope to be before another season rolls around. The fighting will all be done this summer, I think. The eyes of the 60th Vets. have been blest with a sight of the goal (as we suppose) of the campaign — Atlanta. Day before yesterday we were upon a high ridge from which by climbing a tree we could see the city with its tall spires and chimneys — and a great smoke going up — from its numerous furnaces, I suppose. With a glass you could see the windows of the houses and the streets plainly. We could see the forts around the city and lines of rifle pits. The rebs have made a stand again, this side of the river. We are not fighting much at present, only skirmishing and an occasional shot from the batteries. Last night about dusk there was a furious cannonade to the fight of the line, but whether it was accompanied with musketry, we were too distant to tell. I have not heard the cause of it. About midnight the firing of the skirmishers was very sharp, and I expected we should be called up but it settled down again to its usual opening. We are, I understand, about a mile and a halfrom the river. The rebel works are on a ridge close to its banks. They are very strong, I should judge. They have several large earthen forts in the line. We are making the most of our little rest. The officers are hard at work today upon the pay rolls. I suppose we shall not be paid until the campaign is over. It has lasted now over two months. I heard from Luther yesterday. He is doing well.

Is there much excitement up there relative to the Presidential campaign? It occasions very little remark down here. Fremont, I think, will meet little favor with the army. All are awaiting the action of the Democratic convention at Chicago. I see they have postponed the meeting until Aug. 29th. They have shown the right spirit and put the Republicans to shame. Every nerve of the Nation should now be used to strengthen her armies and uphold the administration. It will be time enough to talk politics after the summer's work is done. It will be hard to tell, I think, before that is done who is the right man for President.

I suppose I saw the cannon fired that killed Bishop Polk as our Regt. was close to the batteries at the time. The shot or shell struck him in the breast. If the shell had burst, old Johnston and Hardee would probably ave shared the same fate, as they were close beside him. Please write often. Love to all.

Your aff. son,

Alex F. Hubbell

(There goes another shot.) "We had a most terrific battle the 20th inst. (Another shell made us jump a little just now.) I will not try to describe the battle to you. I could not do it justice. Captain Delaney was wounded in the arm, a pretty bad wound but not dangerous. He will not be able to be with us probably for two or three months. The 60th Vets covered themselves with glory. We have the credit of saving the 13th N.Y. Independent Battery. (The rebs are shooting uncomfortably close. I must hurry up and say what I have to say as it is a little dangerous writing out here.) Love to all. You see the rebs have spoiled your letter. Please write often.

Your aff. son,

Alex F. Hubbell

P.S. The rebs are shooting very close. We are within 1¼ miles of Atlanta and our long range guns are shelling the city. How good God has been to me.

Our batteries were unusually silent this day, and I understand that it was by special order of Gen'l. Sherman, but about 10 P.M. they opened lustily. The battery we call "the Atlanta Express" sent a shell into the city every five minutes during the night.

We have changed our position since then and are now further to the left of the line. Day before yesterday the 15th, 16th and 17th army corps moved to the right of the line. The cannonading there was very heavy yesterday and I think they had a heavy fight. Our regt. was sent out last night to make a feint as if we intended to attack them here, in order, I understand, to prevent their sending troops to the right. There was "right smart" of shooting done. The rebs opened a heavy fire upon us from their batteries. I am confident they expected a heavy attack. We lost two men. It is tolerably quiet today, a little skirmishing and an occasional shot from the batteries. Please write often.

You asked me how my health is. It is very good and has been generally during the campaign. Never been slightly indisposed only once or twice.
