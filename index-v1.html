Using modern data science and interactive visualizations, this project brings their words, and the humanity of living through the Civil War, to life in ways the letters alone never could.

Between 1861 and 1865, Henry, Alexander, James, and Charles Hubbell — sons of a widowed mother in upstate New York — wrote 273 letters from camps, hospitals, marches, and battlefields.

This rare collection of family and American history, cared for by generations of descendants, has never been published — until now. This project maps their movements, traces their relationships, and reveals patterns hidden within hundreds of handwritten pages.

“Way down South in Dixie.” That is where I am. And a beautiful country too if it were not cursed by the existence of slavery. I am encamped on the summit of a high hill or rather a mountain from which I can see a long distance and the view is very fine. Green fields, meadows, large farms, patches of woods, roads winding here and there, little villages in the distance, and all of this spread out before me like a beautiful painting.

I was sitting on the hill just now watching the sun as it was setting behind the Blue Ridge. The soft notes of the bugle were floating through the air when I almost forgot I was a soldier. I seemed for a moment to be transported to the green fields and quiet scenes of old Clinton County.

But then again I thought if the drum should beat to arms tomorrow morning, there will be a different scene. Troops will be forming in line of battle. The sun will be glistening on the bright steel of ten thousand breastplates. The artillery will be wheeled into position and the skirmishers will be thrown out, and then the earth will fairly rock.

Alexander Hubbell Corporal, Co. H, 60th New York · Age 18
Pine Mountain, Virginia · July 14, 1862

The Letters

What they wrote home.

Their father, Frederick Augustus Hubbell, had died in 1853 when Alexander was nine years old. Frances McNeil Hubbell raised four sons and a daughter alone in Champlain, on the Canadian border of New York. When the call came for volunteers in 1861, she sent all four.

What followed was not the heroic correspondence of myth. These were young men writing to their mother — about being sick among strangers, about the ache of months without a letter from home. They worried about money: eight dollars sent home from a month’s pay, five dollars begged back when the army failed to pay them at all. They described not just battles but the long, grinding weeks between them.

What makes these letters extraordinary is not any single revelation but the accumulation of ordinary detail across four years. Read together, they become something larger — an unedited record of what the Civil War felt like from inside it.

You know, Mother, what it is to be away from home, sick among strangers and to be without money. I will not try to describe my feelings when I ask for this… Do you suppose you could spare me five dollars, if not, less will do. Alexander · Hospital at Harper’s Ferry · Sept 27, 1862

The Preservation

A trunk in a basement. Five generations of care.

After the war the letters were bundled and put away. They sat in a trunk for eighty years — water-stained, fading, and largely forgotten. They might easily have been lost. Instead, a remarkable chain of stewardship carried them forward: transcribed, introduced, digitized, and finally analyzed — each by a different family member, each in a different decade. The latest of them was named for the first soldier — a name his parents chose while standing on the battlefield at Gettysburg.
