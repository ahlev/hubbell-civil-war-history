The project in one paragraph

The Hubbell Civil War Letters is a free interactive archive of 272 letters written between 1861 and 1870 by four soldier-brothers from Champlain, New York — Henry (34th NY), Alexander (60th NY), James and Charles (153rd NY) — and their mother, Frances. Preserved by five generations of the family and typed in full on a typewriter in 1947–49, the collection was transcribed, structured, and published in 2026 by Alexander Hubbell Levitt, the great-great-grandson and namesake of the one brother who survived the war, using AI held to a published rule: the model is a filter, never a source.

Story starters (all quotes verbatim, with letter IDs)

The mother who wrote to a dead son

Henry Hubbell, 34th New York, was killed in the West Woods at Antietam on September 17, 1862; his body was never recovered. Four days later — not knowing — his mother wrote to him from Champlain:

“Language is too weak, to express my anxiety about you since the commencement of the terrible battle… I read the sad record day after day as it is published, with trembling.”
Frances Hubbell to Henry, Sept 21, 1862 · LTR-1862-09-21-002. Two days earlier his brother Alexander, on the same field, had reported him missing (LTR-1862-09-19-001); the family’s letters trace missing → hope → certainty across months.

A letter written on the Gettysburg battlefield

Alexander’s 60th New York held Culp’s Hill. On July 4, 1863, from “Near Gettysburg, Penna,” he counted his regiment’s 52 casualties, named his company’s dead and wounded, and reported:

“We are burying our and the rebel’s dead today.”
Alexander F. Hubbell to his mother, July 4, 1863 · LTR-1863-07-04-001 — an essentially unpublished eyewitness account, written before the armies left the field.

The family remembered the wrong date for 160 years

James, wounded at Cedar Creek in 1864 (“I walked several miles after being hit in order not to fall into the Johnnies hands” — LTR-1864-10-29-001), survived the war and died in Albany on his way home. Family history said October 19, 1865. Cross-referencing muster rolls, the brothers’ cemetery monument, and period newspapers proved it was October 12 — the family’s memory had fused his death with the anniversary of his wound. The archive corrected its own founding story, and documented the fix.

Five generations, one archive

Frances saved the letters. Gladys Sands Hubbell typed all 272 on a typewriter, 1947–49. Fred Alexander Hubbell Jr. wrote the introduction, 1996. Bruce Levitt digitized them in the 2010s. Alexander Hubbell Levitt structured, verified, and published them in 2026 — with AI doing the labor a research team once would have, under a documented rule that the model is a filter, never a source.

About the creator

Alexander Hubbell Levitt is a technology researcher in Athens, Georgia, and the great-great-grandson and namesake of Alexander F. Hubbell of the 60th New York. He is available for interviews, podcasts, demonstrations of the archive, and talks. More: About the Project.
