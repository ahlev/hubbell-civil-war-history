The youngest Hubbell brother. A West Point cadet who traded classroom Latin for rifle pits. Wounded at the Battle of Cedar Creek on October 19, 1864. Died exactly one year later, on October 19, 1865, before reaching home.

Wartime Footprint

The Cadet

Summer 1862 — West Point Military Academy

James was the youngest of the four brothers and the most scholarly. While Henry fought on the Peninsula and Alexander drilled in Virginia, James entered the United States Military Academy at West Point — a path that set him apart. He was not a volunteer marching toward a battlefield. He was a student preparing for a career. His brothers expected great things.

Henry's Voice

I have no doubt but what James will succeed for he is a good scholar.

Henry Hubbell, July 8, 1862

James's early letters are those of a boy discovering an institution. He described the lockers — five feet long, one foot high, one foot broad, shared among three cadets. He described the hazing: older cadets dragging new arrivals around the grounds at night, knocking off bayonets, trying to steal their guns. James held his.

Sometimes the Cadets would come out with half a dozen wheelbarrows, and sometimes with an old cannon. They would knock off our bayonets and get our guns away if they could. They dragged me around one night but could not get my gun away. July 27, 1862 — West Point

He stood in the third section of six — a strong academic position. The lowest section was called the "Immortal Section," a name that carried no glory. James attended Bible class with Professor French, where prayer books were distributed and cadets held prayer meetings twice a week. He was earnest, devout, and competitive: "I shall try to go up higher, and I think, can."

Historical Context

West Point during the Civil War operated under extraordinary pressure. The regular four-year curriculum was compressed, and classes graduated early to fill the Union's desperate need for trained officers. The academy had been riven by secession: of the 65 cadets from Southern states in 1861, nearly all resigned to join the Confederacy. Robert E. Lee had been its superintendent just a decade earlier. James entered a campus that was simultaneously an elite engineering school and a wartime officer factory, where classmates might be commanding troops within months of graduation.

What He Didn't Know

September 21, 1862 — West Point

On September 21, 1862, James sat down at West Point and wrote to his mother. He reported that Charles had enlisted and that Mother must be "very lonely." He described his academic standing, his Bible class, his near-miss at being marked late to parade because his shoes were unblackened. Then, at the end, he asked a simple question:

Have you heard from Henry and Alex yet? September 21, 1862 — West Point

He did not know. Four days earlier, on September 17, Henry had been killed at the Battle of Antietam. Alexander had been wounded. The family was in crisis. And James, in his classroom on the Hudson, was asking about his shoes.

It is one of the most quietly devastating moments in the collection — not for what it says, but for the gap between what the writer knows and what the reader knows. James would learn soon enough. But on September 21, he was still the youngest brother asking after the others, certain they were fine.

Historical Context

The Battle of Antietam (September 17, 1862) was the bloodiest single day in American history: 22,717 casualties. News traveled slowly. Casualty lists appeared in newspapers days or weeks later, often incomplete. Families frequently learned of a soldier's fate through word of mouth, delayed letters, or rumor. James's innocent question — written four days after the battle — illustrates how the war's worst news arrived on its own timetable.

Two Years of Silence

James's letters stop after September 1862. No surviving correspondence until June 1864. His brothers' letters fill in fragments: Charles reported James still at West Point as late as September 1863, receiving demerits and hosting sixty-five new cadets. By early 1864, James had left the academy — the circumstances unclear — and enlisted as a private in the 153rd New York Volunteers, Charles's regiment. He listed his occupation as "farmer." He received $860 in bounty. The West Point cadet who had studied French and drilled with artillery was now a private carrying a rifle. When he walked into Charles's tent in Louisiana in May 1864, unannounced, Charles barely recognized him: "James has not shaved since he left West Point and has quite a pair of whiskers."

Morganza

June 1864 — Louisiana

James's first letter from the field is that of a young man newly arrived in a strange country. He was encamped on the Mississippi at Morganza, Louisiana. He had been on a foraging expedition — a hundred-wagon train that filled up from a French woman's barn while her men were off at war. The landscape amazed him: French-speaking Black workers, sugar mills rationing one spoonful per day, handsome brick houses standing empty.

One of our company called at a house and was hospitably entertained with cornbread and syrup, part of which he brought out in his hands. They said they had not seen wheat flour for about two years. June 21, 1864 — Morganza, LA

He noted that Dr. James Sweeney, whom he remembered as a boy from back home, was now the regimental surgeon — "such a little faller when he left our place that I hardly remembered him. I think that if either of us are sick he will be a friend worth having." The West Point education surfaced in his French: "Comment vous portez ma vous!" And his politics were already clear: "O, Grant, go ahead and whip the rebels, is the feeling here."

But beneath the curiosity was the physical reality the scholarly boy had not anticipated. He had spent years sitting in classrooms and now his legs could barely carry him on a march: "I stood it pretty well considering I had used my legs so little since I have entered the service."

Historical Context

James's foraging expedition — a hundred-wagon train filling up from a French woman's barn — illustrates the Union Army's evolving policy toward civilian property in occupied territory. Early in the war, commanders tried to protect Southern property and maintain goodwill. By 1864, that restraint had collapsed. General Orders No. 100 (the Lieber Code of 1863) authorized armies to seize food and supplies from civilians in occupied areas, with or without payment. In Louisiana, where the population was deeply divided between Unionists and Confederates, foraging was both a military necessity and a tool of coercion. The French-speaking families James described — women running plantations while their men were off fighting, offering cornbread to Union soldiers because they hadn't seen wheat flour in two years — were living under a regime of organized deprivation. What James observed as picturesque was, for the people he was taking from, the slow grinding of occupation.

“Generally Unwell and Played Out”

August 1864 — Frederick City, Maryland

In late June, the regiment was shipped north to defend Washington against Jubal Early's raid. James was thrown into rifle pits around the capital, then sent in pursuit of the retreating Confederates into the Shenandoah Valley. The forced marches broke him. His feet blistered. He fell out of the column, rode a wagon train, and ended up in a hospital at Frederick City, Maryland.

My sickness was generally unwell and played out. I have been in the hospital about two weeks now and am nearly ready for duty. August 15, 1864 — Frederick City, MD

From his hospital on a hill overlooking the city, James offered one of the collection's most vivid definitions of Army food:

Bake a flour and water cake with no salt or saleratus on a griddle till it is hard — throw it on a shelf for three or four days and see what kind of a meal it will make. August 15, 1864

He described Charles with a brother's clear-eyed honesty: "He was quite thin and used up when I left; he finds the marches very hard. Charlie is one of the kind that'll go until he cannot go. I do not think that is the way." James believed in pacing yourself. Charles believed in endurance. Both survived. But James, the younger and physically weaker, was already learning that this war would demand more from his body than his mind.

He was also angry. Early's raid had brought a Confederate army to the outskirts of Washington with twenty thousand Union troops nearby, and the rebels had escaped with a three-mile wagon train of plunder. "The fact that the army came up here with that force, harvested, plundered and got off with a wagon train three miles in length is rather irritating to us."

Historical Context

Jubal Early's Raid on Washington (July 1864) was the Confederacy's most audacious gamble of the war's final year. Early led 15,000 troops down the Shenandoah Valley, crossed the Potomac, defeated a Union force at the Battle of the Monocacy near Frederick (July 9), and reached the outskirts of Washington on July 11 — close enough for Lincoln to come under fire while watching from Fort Stevens. Hastily redeployed troops from the Army of the Potomac, including James's regiment, arrived just in time to reinforce the capital. Early withdrew with his plunder intact, humiliating the Union and prompting Grant to send Sheridan to close the Valley permanently.

Winchester

September 19, 1864 — Shenandoah Valley, Virginia

James returned from the hospital at Frederick City and rejoined the regiment in the Shenandoah Valley. On September 19, Sheridan attacked Jubal Early's Confederates at the Third Battle of Winchester. It was James's first major engagement. Company H lost four killed and twelve wounded. The regiment lost sixty-five. James came through without a scratch.

Charles, writing to their mother two days later, allowed himself a moment of cautious relief: "James says he was not frightened, but I think he would own up if he was. It was a pretty warm place for green troops." The boy who had studied artillery drill at West Point had now faced the real thing. The company that had once mustered at full strength was being cut in half, fight by fight.

The Valley

October 1864 — Shenandoah Valley, Virginia

By October, James was back on the march. Sheridan's army was burning its way south — destroying barns, forage, anything that could feed Lee's army. James wrote to Alexander with a mixture of battlefield reporting and dark humor. "You have travelled this road once, I think" — Alexander had served in this same valley in 1862.

While he was in one house one of the children said, "Ma, there is a Yankee down cellar taking all our preserves and bacon." "Ma, see that Yankee looking in the oven." October 4, 1864 — Harrisonburg, VA

He reported that Alex Douglas — the same man Charles would later report missing from Cedar Creek — had already been "gobbled by guerrillas" after falling behind on a march. He noted that the boys had a habit of running through Southern houses and taking whatever they chose, "which I think exasperates the rebels very much."

And then, in a letter to his brother, he stated his politics with the directness of a man who might not get another chance to say it:

I'm a voter; I go for old Abe; he is the man, I think. Little Mac will probably run strong. This war has lasted nearly four years. I would like to see it ended, and believe it will be soon. October 4, 1864

He would cast his vote for Lincoln from a hospital bed.

Cedar Creek

October 19, 1864

On the night of October 18, Charles and James received a bundle of newspapers and looked forward to reading them the next day. They never got the chance.

Before dawn on October 19, Early's Confederates launched a surprise attack on the Union camp at Cedar Creek. Charles told the story from his perspective — James exclaiming "The Rebs are here!", the scramble for accoutrements, the breastworks overrun, the retreat across a deep ravine with bullets flying thick. But James's own account, written ten days later from a hospital bed, is spare and soldier-plain:

I was wounded in the left thigh at Cedar Run. It is nothing but a flesh wound and is doing well. I walked several miles after being hit in order not to fall into the Johnnies' hands. October 29, 1864 — Patterson Park Hospital, Baltimore
Charles's Account

A Sergeant of our Co. says that as he was going to the rear with a wounded man he saw James; that James rec'd. a flesh wound in the thigh or hip and walked about five miles to the hospital. Says James had made a bandage of his drawers.

Charles Hubbell, October 22, 1864

Five miles on a wounded leg, a bandage improvised from his own underclothes, to avoid capture. The West Point cadet, the third-section scholar, the boy who couldn't keep up on marches two months earlier, walked five miles with a bullet wound because the alternative was a Confederate prison.

The journey from the field to a hospital was its own ordeal. Thirty miles by army wagon over rutted Valley roads. Then 120 miles by freight car. "That journey was a hard one," James wrote, in what may be the greatest understatement in the collection.

Historical Context

The Battle of Cedar Creek (October 19, 1864) began as a Confederate rout of the Union camp and ended as one of Sheridan's greatest victories. The 153rd New York was overrun in the initial surprise attack. Company H lost 7 wounded and 2 killed. James was one of the wounded. Sheridan's famous ride from Winchester rallied the retreating army and drove Early's force from the field, effectively ending the war in the Shenandoah Valley.

The Hospital Chain

October – December 1864 — Baltimore to Philadelphia

James moved through the military hospital system like a package in the mail — first Frederick City, then Patterson Park in Baltimore, then Chestnut Hill Hospital in Philadelphia. At each stop he wrote home. His letters from the hospitals are the most detailed and articulate of his small collection, as though the enforced stillness gave the scholar in him permission to observe again.

At Patterson Park in Baltimore, he cast his vote for Lincoln, as he had promised. "I shall be able to vote here and shall cast my vote for Abraham Lincoln. I hope he will be elected." He assessed the war with a soldier's confidence: "I do not believe the Rebs will try it again in the Valley — three times terribly whipped."

Historical Context

James's hospital vote was made possible by one of the war's quiet revolutions: soldier suffrage. Before 1862, most states required citizens to vote in person at their home polling place — effectively disenfranchising hundreds of thousands of men in uniform. Between 1862 and 1864, nineteen states passed laws allowing soldiers to vote by absentee ballot or at field polling stations supervised by officers. The stakes were enormous. Lincoln's re-election was uncertain through the summer of 1864; if McClellan won on a peace platform, the war might end with the Confederacy intact. Soldiers voted overwhelmingly for Lincoln — roughly 78% across the army — though individual units varied wildly. James and his brother Charles, who also voted Lincoln, were minority voices in the 153rd New York. The soldier vote did not decide the election — Lincoln won the civilian vote as well — but it cemented the idea that men fighting the war had earned the right to shape its political outcome, even from a hospital bed in Baltimore.

By December, he had been transferred to Chestnut Hill Hospital in Philadelphia, where his wound was nearly healed. He could walk around. He felt ready to return to his regiment. And on December 9, he described the Thanksgiving dinner the hospital had served two weeks earlier:

The dinner was turkey and gravy, oyster soup (I could not eat that) boiled potatoes, bread, cranberry preserves and ice cream. All was cold. It could not be otherwise when the table was so large. Each man had his dinner piled on his plate. Our ward got their dinner about four o'clock, being among the last they were obliged to wait for the second table. December 9, 1864 — Chestnut Hill Hospital, Philadelphia

Turkey and ice cream, all cold, piled on a plate, served at four in the afternoon because the ward was among the last. It is a deeply American scene — the institutional attempt at celebration, the reality of mass feeding, the young man who could not eat the oyster soup. James reported it all with his usual precision, the way Charles would have counted the portions.

Historical Context

The military hospital system was the Civil War's largest medical enterprise. By 1864, the Union operated over 200 general hospitals with 137,000 beds. Wounded soldiers moved through a chain: field hospital near the battlefield, then transfer by wagon and rail to larger facilities in cities like Baltimore, Philadelphia, and Washington. Chestnut Hill Hospital in Philadelphia, where James spent his final weeks of recovery, was a sprawling complex of fifty wooden pavilion wards housing 3,600 patients. The U.S. Sanitary Commission — a civilian volunteer organization — supplemented Army care with supplies, nurses, and holiday meals like the Thanksgiving dinner James described.

The Ring

December 1864

In the same letter, folded inside the paper, James enclosed a ring. He had made several during the spring march from Louisiana to Washington, whittling or bending them while walking. Most broke in his pocket book. This one survived.

Enclosed you will find a ring. I made several while on my way last Spring to the regiment, but they got broke in my pocket book. December 9, 1864 — Chestnut Hill Hospital, Philadelphia

He was planning for the next campaign. He would carry writing paper folded into a book cover. He would roll his bundle across his shoulder instead of using a knapsack. He reassured his mother about Alexander: "You do not know how well a soldier boy can take care of himself." He asked about a furlough but expected none — too many were already out, and he was nearly well.

It is the last letter in the collection from James Hubbell.

October 19, 1865

The story does not end in the hospital at Philadelphia. In June 1865, Charles wrote from Savannah, Georgia, that James was with him — alive, serving occupation duty, planning to write home next. The wound had healed, or seemed to. The West Point cadet, the private who had walked five miles on a bullet wound, had recovered and gone back to his regiment. He was in the Deep South, watching freed people parade on the Fourth of July, complaining to Charles that his eyes troubled him at night.

James died on October 19, 1865 — exactly one year to the day after the bullet struck him at Cedar Creek. The flesh wound that was "doing well" in October, that was "nearly healed" in December, that had allowed him to march and serve through the spring and summer — it killed him anyway. No letter survives to explain how. No medical record in the family collection describes what went wrong.

The ring he made on the march from Louisiana arrived home before he did.

What Remains

James left fewer letters than any of his brothers. Nine in all, spanning three years. But in those nine letters he described the full arc of the Civil War experience: the academy and the battlefield, the march and the collapse, the wound and the hospital, the cold turkey and the handmade ring.

He was the youngest. He was the scholar. He held his gun when the older cadets tried to take it. He defined hardtack for posterity. He walked five miles on a wounded leg. He voted for Lincoln from a hospital bed. He made rings on the march and mailed the one that didn't break.

And on the anniversary of the day that ended his war, the war ended him.
