A widow managing a farm in northern New York, Frances Hubbell sent four sons to the Civil War. She read the casualty lists every morning with trembling hands. She conducted her own investigation into Henry's death. She never broke.

“If I Was a Man”

September 21, 1862 — Champlain, New York

Frances Hubbell's first surviving letter was written four days after the Battle of Antietam — though she did not yet know what had happened there. She knew only that the 34th New York had been in terrible fighting. She had been reading the casualty lists every morning, searching for names she recognized, finding none she loved. Not yet.

Language is too weak, to express my anxiety about you since the commencement of the terrible battle. Thank God your name has not yet appeared on the sad list, which has caused mourning and lamentation to so many homes. I read the sad record day after day as it is published, with trembling. September 21, 1862 — to Henry

She reported that Charles had been made first corporal at Plattsburgh. She noted that the war seemed nearly over. She expressed faith in McClellan. And then, in a single sentence, she revealed what this war had done to a woman who could only watch:

How I long to be near you. I am almost tempted at times to go down to the hospitals. If I was a man I am sure I should enlist. In reading the descriptions of the battle fields — my hair almost rises. September 21, 1862

She was writing to Henry. She did not know he was already dead.

Historical Context

Frances Hubbell was one of an estimated 200,000 Northern women who managed farms alone during the Civil War. With her husband dead since 1853 and all four sons in the Army, she bore full responsibility for the Champlain farm — livestock, crops, hired labor, mortgage, and the daily decisions that determined whether the family would eat. The federal government offered no support for soldiers' families; the allotment system that Henry and Alexander used to send money home was the only lifeline, and it was unreliable. Some counties and states established soldiers' aid societies that distributed food and fuel to families left behind, but coverage was uneven and rarely reached remote communities like Champlain. Frances's repeated concern about whether money had arrived was not anxiety — it was economic survival. The war created an invisible labor crisis on the Northern home front: women, children, and elderly men stepped into roles that had been held by the million-plus men now in uniform. Mechanization helped — the McCormick reaper allowed fewer hands to bring in the harvest — but the human cost of managing alone, while also managing grief, went largely unrecorded.

Historical Context

Casualty lists were published in Northern newspapers days or weeks after a battle, compiled from regimental returns forwarded through military channels. The lists were frequently incomplete and riddled with errors — misspelled names, wrong regiments, men listed as killed who were only wounded, and wounded men omitted entirely. For families like the Hubbells in remote Champlain, New York, the newspaper was often the only source of information. There was no official notification system for families. A mother learned her son's fate the same way she learned the price of wheat: by reading the paper.

The Wounded List

September 28, 1862

One week later, Henry's name appeared on the wounded list. The world Frances had been holding together — four sons deployed, a farm to run, casualty lists to read each morning — fractured.

She wrote to Alexander in a state of near-collapse. She had no money to travel to Washington. She had no contacts in the 34th New York. She did not know where Henry was — a field hospital, a prison, a grave. She asked Alexander to find him, to get a furlough, to bring him home. She offered to come herself, as a nurse, if anyone would let her.

Write me immediately & relieve me from the agony of suspense. O, the dreadful minutes are hours to me — I do not know what to do with myself — and shall be miserable until I hear from you. I wish you would interest some one to get a furlough for him, if he is able to travel. O, if I could only go down and take care of him. September 28, 1862 — to Alexander

Then, at the bottom of the page, six words that carry the weight of the entire collection:

My heart is aching to bursting, and I can write no more. September 28, 1862

In the same letter, she noted that Charles was expected the next day for a furlough before deploying. "It is heart breaking to think that I must so soon part with him." She was losing one son and sending another. She could not stop either.

The Investigation

October 1862

What Frances did next is remarkable. Unable to travel, unable to search the battlefield herself, she conducted an investigation from Champlain. Every returned soldier, every letter from the front, every community contact became a source. She cross-referenced testimony the way a detective builds a case.

Young Fiser reported that Henry was seen to fall, that the rebels were within two rods — thirty-three feet — when they charged, and that the men fled without looking back. Mr. Copeland read a letter stating that the field had been searched and Henry's body was not among the identified Union dead. A man named McDonald claimed he had seen Henry after the battle, limping to the rear, leaning on his gun. Scott reported seeing Henry on one knee.

Frances weighed each account. She identified contradictions. She demanded specifics: who exactly saw Henry? When? Where were they standing? She tracked down the McDonald story and would later discover, through McKee's letter, that McDonald had not actually seen Henry at all.

Where, where is the dear, dear boy? Echo answers — where? I feel that he somehow got mixed up with another Regt., fell, and was buried unrecognized. October 2, 1862 — to Alexander

She was not wrong. Henry was buried on the field at Antietam. His body was never recovered.

In the midst of this forensic grief, she found time for spiritual counsel. The 91st Psalm — the Soldier's Psalm, carried by Union soldiers across the war — she commanded Alexander to read daily:

Read the 91st Psalm daily — and live up to its promises. The Saviour is near, near you — and can hear the heart's faintest sigh. In the din of battle no artillery can drown that sigh if it is breathed in faith. October 6, 1862

At the end of that letter, she abandoned prose entirely:

Write, write, write, write, write, write, write, WRITE. October 6, 1862
Historical Context

Psalm 91 — "He that dwelleth in the secret place of the most High shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty" — was known throughout the Civil War as the Soldier's Psalm. Mothers sewed copies into uniforms. Chaplains distributed it before battles. Its promises of divine protection ("A thousand shall fall at thy side, and ten thousand at thy right hand; but it shall not come nigh thee") offered comfort that was both deeply felt and statistically improbable. Frances's command to Alexander to read it daily reflects a widespread practice among Union families: the psalm was not merely devotional but talismanic — a ward against the bullet that had already found Henry.

Hope Dies

October – December 1862

In late October, B. Misner returned from captivity in Richmond. He had seen every Union prisoner taken at Antietam. Henry was not among them. The prisoner theory — the last theory that allowed for a living Henry — collapsed.

I see B. Misner this morning and he has dashed any hopes with regard to Henry. He says that he and Albert are not incarcerated at all, and that they saw all the prisoners taken at Antietam, and Henry was not among them. October 29, 1862 — to Alexander

Frances began to name what had happened: "I fear that he was one of the 'unknown' buried, or one of the number found in the bushes, who had died of their wounds and could not be identified." She warned Alexander — "do not go in without your name upon your person" — a mother's terrible pragmatism, learned from losing a son whose body could not be found.

By December, she was unraveling. She went to the post office every morning, and every morning came home empty-handed. She could not look at Henry's letters or photographs. "I cannot endure to look at his letters or pictures — it is too much for my poor shattered nerves." And yet Henry appeared everywhere in the daily work of the farm. "Tomorrow I intend to get pony shod," she wrote. "Examples of this kind that I intend to, bring Henry painfully to mind. These are things that he always saw to." Every chore he had done was now hers, and every chore reminded her that he was gone.

In the same letter, she begged Alexander to come home: "Can you get a discharge and come home. I cant live alone — I must have one of you. In my old age it is hard to be left alone."

You know what Henry wrote you on New Year day. He says, "who knows Alex, whether you and I will be here at the close of this year." O, how my heart bleeds. It was almost a prophecy. December 9, 1862 — to Alexander

The Formal Inquiry

April 30, 1863

Around the same time, Frances composed the only letter in the collection addressed not to family but to authority. Written to an unknown official — likely a military hospital administrator or the Sanitary Commission — it is a formal request for information about her son, and it is the most controlled piece of writing she produced.

My son, Henry Hubbell, of Co. D, 34th N.Y. reg't., has been missing since the battle of Antietam. He fell as the enemy charged, and was not seen after. The surgeon assures me that he sent trusty men to search the field after the battle, that every union soldier killed was fully identified, and that my son certainly was not among those. His belief is that he was slightly wounded and taken prisoner. April 30, 1863 — to "Dear Sir"

She described Henry's health before the battle — feeble, nervous, much reduced. She mentioned, with the careful restraint of a woman who has imagined every possibility, that she had noticed in a newspaper that many soldiers were confined in an insane asylum at Williamsburg, Virginia. "I sometimes fear he may be among them."

It is signed: "That the Lord will direct and give you success in this matter, is the prayer of his afflicted mother, Frances M. Hubbell."

Historical Context

Tens of thousands of Union families spent years searching for missing soldiers. The federal government had no systematic process for tracking the wounded, captured, or dead. In 1865, Clara Barton established the Office of Correspondence with Friends of the Missing Men of the United States Army, which handled over 63,000 inquiries. The U.S. Sanitary Commission and the U.S. Christian Commission also served as clearinghouses for information. Frances's formal letter — addressed to an unknown official, methodically presenting the known facts — follows a pattern repeated by thousands of mothers, wives, and sisters who became, by necessity, their own investigators.

Reading His Letters

May 1863

Seven months after Antietam, Frances finally read Henry's letters. All of them. She had been unable to look at them. Now she sat down and went through every page her eldest son had written home.

I am feeling so sad today. I have just finished the perusal of all of dear Henry's letters. The reading of them brought him so vividly before me. I have wept until I am almost blind. I can see his sweet face and hear his sweet voice — it seems as if I have had a visit with him. May 4, 1863 — to Alexander

She quoted his words back to Alexander: "I often dream of being home; I think of you every night when I am alone; I long to see you all; at this time next year, I hope to be with you." Each promise he made — to come home, to see them, to be there next year — had been broken by a bullet in Maryland.

In the same letter, she resolved the McDonald mystery. McKee's letter, found among Henry's papers, stated plainly: "I did not see Henry after he was wounded." McDonald's story — the one the family had clung to for months — was false. Young Carter, recently discharged from the 34th with a thumb shot off at Antietam, explained why no one had seen Henry fall: "His position in the ranks was fourth and next to Ballerjy who was killed. Thinks the reason why so little is known of Henry is that those who were near him were killed."

The witnesses were dead. The evidence was silence.

My heart is breaking, breaking, breaking. I cannot dwell upon this painful, painful subject without being almost deranged. May 4, 1863

And then, acceptance — a sentence that reads like a door closing:

He has gone to his far home of bliss — yes, one of them, and I shall follow. May 4, 1863

The Campaign for Charles

Late 1863 – March 1864

Frances had lost one son and could not reach the others. But she was not helpless, and she was not passive. Sometime in late 1863, she began a quiet campaign to get Charles out of the field. She wrote to the regimental surgeon about Charles's health. She enlisted Mrs. Strain — wife of Lieutenant Colonel Strain of the 153rd New York — who wrote to her husband several times on Charles's behalf. She got Captain Ransom, also of the 153rd, to write across regimental lines to Captain Delaney of Alexander's 60th New York, asking about a discharge for Alexander. She was working every contact she had, across hundreds of miles, by mail alone. None of Frances's letters to Mrs. Strain or to the surgeon survive. We know the campaign happened because Strain himself told Charles the full story.

She could not save Henry. She could not bring Alexander home — the colonel wrote back that he "would do what he could to restore you to me, but feared the attempts would be futile." Alexander confirmed the dead end in a November letter: Captain Ransom had written to Captain Delaney on his behalf, but the surgeon and colonel said it was not in their power. "Secretary Stanton is the man to apply to, or else the President," Alexander wrote. Frances did not have access to either.

But she could try to protect Charles. And on the morning of March 3, 1864, Lieutenant Colonel Strain called Charles into his tent and told him to pack. He was being permanently detached from the regiment and reassigned as a clerk at General Banks's headquarters in New Orleans. Strain told Charles the full story: that his mother had written to the surgeon, that Mrs. Strain had written to him multiple times, that he had been waiting for an opportunity. The captain had wanted to send a healthier man, but Strain overruled him — saying he "could not spare so healthy a man," the delicate fiction that justified giving the less robust Charles the desk job.

Charles wrote home with the news. He did not pretend to be surprised.

Now, Mother, I am much obliged to you, for you are at the bottom of this matter. It is a pleasant place here, a good deal better than being in the field. Charles Hubbell, March 6, 1864 — New Orleans

The position lasted seven weeks. Charles was relieved on April 21 because his penmanship was not up to the Assistant Adjutant General's standards. "The A.A. General here is very particular about penmanship," Charles reported — an irony that would have amused his brothers, since Charles had spent months lecturing Alexander about improving his handwriting. He returned to the regiment. But for those seven weeks, Frances had managed from Champlain what the Army had no mechanism to provide: a mother's intervention on behalf of a son she had already nearly lost once. It was the only time in the war that her campaign produced a concrete result.

Historical Context

The Union Army had no formal policy for compassionate reassignment. A mother who had lost one son could not petition the War Department to spare another. Instead, families worked through personal networks — officers' wives, community connections, regimental surgeons — that operated parallel to the chain of command. Frances's campaign through Mrs. Strain was invisible in official records but decisive in Charles's life. The mechanism was simple: a wife wrote to her husband the colonel, the colonel waited for an opening, the opening came. It was how the system actually worked, for families who knew how to work it.

The Empty House

November – December 1863

By late 1863, all four sons were gone — Henry dead, Alexander in Tennessee, Charles at Capitol Hill in Washington, James at West Point. Frances sat in the Champlain farmhouse with her daughter Fannie and wrote letters into the void. When Alexander's correspondence stopped after the battles around Chattanooga, the Henry nightmare began again.

Where are you? Oh, where are you? Not a word have I heard from you since the late battles in Tennessee and I am in the deepest distress about you. December 9, 1863 — to Alexander

Henry's ghost was everywhere. Every silence from a living son summoned the dead one: "Dear, dear, precious Henry. I cannot dwell a moment upon his fate without an agony that is almost insupportable." She described the house as a great castle of silence. She refused to go out for Thanksgiving.

I hope you will get a Thanksgiving dinner somewhere. I wish I could send you one. I trust you will be with us next year. I shall not go out this year. How can I when all my boys are away! I cannot. November 15, 1863 — to Alexander

In the same breath, she counseled faith: "Go and tell Jesus all your trials. His ear is ever open." She reminded Alexander of his promise — a temperance pledge, the details unspecified but clearly sacred between them. She urged him to be a Christian soldier, like Havelock. She was sustaining her sons' souls from two hundred miles away while her own was in ruins.

Historical Context

The Battles of Chattanooga (November 23–25, 1863) broke the Confederate siege of the city and opened the gateway to Georgia. The fighting included the dramatic assault on Lookout Mountain — later romanticized as the "Battle Above the Clouds" — and the storming of Missionary Ridge, where Union troops charged uphill without orders and overran the Confederate line. Alexander's 60th New York was in the thick of it. When Frances wrote "Where are you? Oh, where are you?" in December, the mail from Chattanooga had been disrupted by weeks of fighting. Her terror was grounded in experience: the last time a son's letters stopped after a major battle, the son was dead.

“Among the Living”

January 1865 — Champlain, New York

In January 1865, after weeks of silence following the Battle of Nashville, two letters from Alexander arrived in the same mail. Frances responded with a passage that sounds like the Psalms she had been prescribing to her sons:

Praise the Lord — Bless the Lord O my soul — all within me bless His holy, holy name for preserving the precious life of my darling child. Your letters of the 24th & 25th are this moment received, the first which we have received from you since the battles before Nashville. … You are among the living while my fears had numbered you with the dead. January 11, 1865 — to Alexander

You are among the living while my fears had numbered you with the dead. Every silence, for three years, had meant the same thing to her: another Antietam. Another morning at the post office with nothing. Another son becoming Henry.

And Alexander was not the only son she feared for. James had gone silent too — no word from the hospital, no letters forwarded. She feared guerrillas had captured him on the road. "I very much fear poor James was among them," she wrote. "Some evil has befallen him. He would not cause me so much anxiety — he is too good a boy." Then, in a detail that cuts deeper than any battlefield account: "In his last letter he sent a very pretty ring — made of bone by himself." The ring was all she had of him. She did not yet know if the boy who made it was alive.

Champlain itself had changed. After the St. Albans Raid — Confederate agents crossing from Canada to rob Vermont banks — the border town was under military rule. One hundred fifty soldiers garrisoned in the village. Stables for a hundred cavalry horses. Reveille in the morning and tattoo at night. The war that Frances had experienced only through letters had finally arrived at her doorstep.

She counseled Alexander one last time, at length, with the accumulated moral authority of three years of suffering:

Rule your spirit. You know what Solomon says — that one who does not rule his spirit is like a city without a wall. … Let your Bible be your most intimate companion. I beseech you not to indulge in reading the vile literature which is circulated in the army. You had better take coals of fire to your bosom. I warn you against it. I charge you not to read it. January 1865
Historical Context

Frances's warning against "vile literature" in the army reflects a widespread moral panic among Northern families. Camp life exposed young men to gambling, alcohol, profanity, and a thriving trade in cheap sensational fiction and pornographic prints that circulated freely among the troops. The United States Christian Commission — a Protestant volunteer organization that operated alongside the more secular Sanitary Commission — distributed over 1.4 million Bibles and 13 million religious tracts during the war, explicitly to counter this influence. Mothers like Frances saw the moral danger of the army as real as the physical one: a son might survive the war and come home ruined in character. Her language — "you had better take coals of fire to your bosom" — echoes Proverbs 6:27, a passage about moral temptation. The temperance pledge she referenced, and Alexander's promise to "do nothing of which you would not approve," were part of a moral contract between mother and son that ran parallel to his military obligations. For Frances, keeping her sons' souls was as urgent as keeping their bodies alive.

Historical Context

The St. Albans Raid (October 19, 1864) was the northernmost engagement of the Civil War. Twenty-one Confederate agents crossed from Canada into St. Albans, Vermont — just thirty miles from Champlain — robbed three banks of over $200,000, shot one civilian, and attempted to burn the town before fleeing back across the border. The raid panicked the northern frontier. Military garrisons were established in border towns including Champlain, which Frances described in detail: soldiers quartered in the village, cavalry horses stabled nearby, reveille and tattoo marking the hours. For a woman who had experienced the war only through letters, the soldiers outside her window were a jarring arrival of the conflict at her own doorstep.

The War Ends

No letters survive from Frances between January 1865 and November 1870. The war ended. Alexander came home. Charles came home. James did not — he died on October 19, 1865, one year to the day after being wounded at Cedar Creek. Frances buried a second son.

The Cheese Factory

November 28, 1870 — Champlain, New York

The last letter in the collection is addressed to "My dear boys" — all of them, together. It is the only letter in which Frances writes to her surviving sons as adults, not as soldiers.

She had spent Thanksgiving at Uncle William's house with the extended family. The dinner was grand — "such as could not be got up anywhere except in New England." The ride home was terrifying: two miles in pitch darkness, muddy roads, rain. "I never rode in such fear. It was pitchy dark — it could be felt."

Your Uncle William built a cheese factory last summer, and he says it is better than bonds. There are ten stock holders. The factory cost five thousand dollars and they say it will pay for itself in five years. They intend to milk 25 cows next summer. … The cheeses make their weight eighty pounds and sold for fourteen cents. They are the best in market. November 28, 1870 — to "My dear boys"

Cheese factories and bond yields. Butter selling for forty cents. The boys' house needs insulating — refuse brick from the fort, available for nothing, would line the walls and make it comfortable. She was sending Webster's Masterpieces by mail if the postage wasn't more than the book was worth.

Eight years earlier, she had written "my heart is breaking, breaking, breaking." Now she was advising on dairy investments and home insulation. The distance between those two sentences is the distance the war put between who she was and who she became — or perhaps it is no distance at all. Perhaps the woman who wrote French love letters while reading casualty lists was always both: the one who broke, and the one who endured.
