Who They Were

A widowed mother, four soldier sons, and a sister who kept the home together. These are the people behind the letters.

The Hubbell family lived in Champlain, Clinton County, New York — a small farming town on the Canadian border near Lake Champlain. Frederick Augustus Hubbell died in 1853, leaving his wife Frances to raise five children alone. When the Civil War began, she sent every son she had.

Frances L. (McNeil) Hubbell

HomeChamplain, NY

Born in Charlotte, Vermont to a family of Scottish descent, Frances married Frederick Augustus Hubbell in 1833 and settled in Champlain, on the Canadian border of New York. When Frederick died in 1853, she was left to raise six children alone on a small farm. She was approximately 45 years old.

Her wartime letters reveal a woman of extraordinary strength, managing the home front alone while all four sons served simultaneously. She sent money she could not spare, shipped boxes of supplies, and endured long silences between letters. She was the emotional center of the family — the person every son wrote to first.

After the war, Frances moved to Fonda, Pocahontas County, Iowa around 1872, following Alexander and Charles who had settled there. She lived with or near her surviving children until her death at approximately age 82.

In Her Own Words

If I was a man I am sure I should enlist, but as it is I have sent all I had to send and hope they will do nobly. — Frances to a son, ~1862
I have wept until I am almost blind. I could not read Henry's letters without weeping. — Frances, after learning of Henry's death, 1862
Survived the war but lost three of her four sons — Henry at Antietam, James returning from Savannah, and Charles to war-contracted illness a decade later. She outlived them all, dying in Iowa in 1890.
H

Henry Hubbell

The Eldest Son — First to Enlist, First to Fall
b. November 1839, Champlain, NY • d. September 17, 1862, Antietam, MD (age ~22)
RegimentCo. D, 34th NY
EnlistedJune 15, 1861
Service15 months
Letters59 preserved
RankPrivate
TheaterEastern (VA, MD)

Henry was the eldest brother and the first to answer the call, enlisting in the 34th New York Volunteers in June 1861. His letters reveal a thoughtful young man with dry humor and a philosophical streak — noting that he could spell "diarrhea" but not "rheumatism," and reflecting that his entire military life felt dreamlike.

He served in the Eastern Theater through the Peninsula Campaign and Second Bull Run. His letters show someone who tried to avoid the quarreling and petty conflicts of camp life, preferring to write home about the landscape, the food, and the small absurdities of army routine. He was practical and observant, often reassuring his mother while giving unflinching accounts of conditions.

Henry's last known letter was written in July 1862. On September 17, 1862, he was killed at the Battle of Antietam — the single bloodiest day of the war. His body was never positively recovered. The family learned of his death through conflicting reports: a casualty list, then word he was wounded, then finally a soldier's account of seeing his name on a headboard marking a grave.

In His Own Words

It is all like a dream, my being here. I can hardly realize that I have been from home so long. — Henry, from camp in Virginia, 1861
I have learned to spell diarrhea but can't spell rheumatism yet. — Henry, on camp ailments, 1862
I try to avoid all quarrels and disputes, for they never do any good. — Henry, on camp life, 1862
Killed in action at the Battle of Antietam, September 17, 1862. He was approximately 22 years old. His body was never recovered by the family.
A

Alexander F. Hubbell

The Prolific Writer — 120 Letters Through Nearly Four Years of War
b. March 28, 1844, Champlain, NY • d. December 7, 1894, Fonda, Iowa (age 50)
RegimentCo. H, 60th NY
EnlistedSeptember 13, 1861
Service~3.5 years
Letters120 preserved
RankCpl → Sgt
TheaterEastern & Western

Alexander was just 17 when he enlisted — the youngest brother in uniform. Despite his age, he quickly became the family's most dedicated correspondent, writing 120 letters that form the backbone of the collection. He was promoted from Corporal to Sergeant and served in both the Eastern and Western Theaters, fighting at Antietam, Lookout Mountain, and through Sherman's Atlanta Campaign.

His letters reveal the most emotionally expressive of the brothers: fiercely patriotic, deeply homesick, and unafraid to show vulnerability. He wrote with passion about duty and country, but also begged his mother for five dollars when the army didn't pay for months. He was wounded at Lookout Mountain and suffered from scurvy during the Atlanta Campaign, spending time in hospitals at Chattanooga and elsewhere.

After the war, Alexander settled in Fonda, Pocahontas County, Iowa. He married Lois Wood in 1887 at age 43, had four children, and farmed until his death at age 50 in 1894 — likely from health complications rooted in his wartime service. His grandson Fred Alexander Hubbell Jr. would later write the introduction contextualizing the family letters, and his daughter-in-law Gladys Sands Hubbell spent two and a half years transcribing the collection by typewriter in 1947.

In His Own Words

I love my country ten times better… No, not ten times, but ten times ten times better than I ever did before. — Alexander, early in the war, 1861
You know, Mother, what it is to be away from home, sick among strangers and to be without money. I will not try to describe my feelings when I ask for this… Do you suppose you could spare me five dollars, if not, less will do. — Alexander, Hospital at Harpers Ferry, September 27, 1862
I feel old. I have begun life's battle for myself. I sometimes feel as though I was about 50 years old. — Alexander, age 20, 1864
Please excuse me, Mother, but I did have a hearty laugh when I took in the idea of your three hopeful sons being in the hospital at the one and same time. — Alexander, Hospital at Chattanooga, August 30, 1864
Survived the war. Settled in Iowa, married Lois Wood in 1887, had four children. Died December 7, 1894 at age 50. His descendants preserved the letters for five generations.

Charles F. Hubbell

The Practical One — Clear-Eyed and Steady Under Fire
b. April 1840, Champlain, NY • d. March 21, 1875, Iowa (age ~34)
RegimentCo. I, 153rd NY
EnlistedSeptember 1862
Service~3 years
Letters69 preserved
RankPrivate
TheaterEastern & Shenandoah

Charles was the most matter-of-fact of the brothers — his letters are practical, observant, and largely free of the sentimentality that marks Alexander's correspondence. He enlisted in the 153rd New York Volunteers alongside his younger brother James. While remarkably healthy throughout his service compared to his brothers, he kept a clear record of camp conditions and the realities of soldiering.

He served in the Shenandoah Valley Campaign under Sheridan, including the Battle of Cedar Creek. His letters show a man who expected nothing from army life and was therefore rarely disappointed. He was the one who confirmed Henry's death to the family, writing plainly: "I think there is not the least doubt that Henry was killed."

After the war, Charles followed Alexander to Iowa and settled as a farmer. He died on March 21, 1875 at approximately age 34 — from disease contracted during his military service. He had survived the war only to be killed by it a decade later.

In His Own Words

Did not enlist with the expectation of sleeping on a feather bed. — Charles, on camp conditions, 1863
I think there is not the least doubt that Henry was killed. — Charles, confirming his brother's death, 1862
Survived the fighting but died March 21, 1875 at age ~34 from disease contracted during his military service. The war claimed him a decade after Appomattox.

James Hubbell

The Youngest Soldier — Wounded at Cedar Creek, Lost on the Way Home
b. January 1843, Champlain, NY • d. October 19, 1865, Albany, NY (age ~22)
RegimentCo. I, 153rd NY
EnlistedSeptember 1862
Service~3 years
Letters9 preserved
RankPrivate
TheaterEastern & Shenandoah

James was the youngest of the four soldier brothers, enlisting at about age 19 alongside Charles in the 153rd New York Volunteers. His nine surviving letters are the fewest of any brother, but they reveal a lively personality — the kind of young man who apologized for getting hair oil on the writing paper and described hardtack with dark humor.

He served in the Shenandoah Valley alongside Charles and was wounded at the Battle of Cedar Creek on October 19, 1864. He spent months in military hospitals recovering. When the war ended, he was still hospitalized — too weakened to travel home under his own power.

James died on October 19, 1865 — exactly one year after being wounded at Cedar Creek — at a hospital in Albany, New York while being transported home from Savannah. The war was already six months over. He was approximately 22 years old.

In His Own Words

You will have to excuse me for getting hair oil on this paper. — James, in camp, 1863
Hardtack… is nothing more or less than flour and water mixed together and rolled out and cut in squares about three inches and baked or dried. And I tell you, you have got to have a pretty good set of grinders to eat them. — James, on army rations, 1863
Wounded at Cedar Creek, October 19, 1864. Died exactly one year later, October 19, 1865, at Albany Hospital while being transported home from Savannah. The war had been over for six months.

Frances “Fannie” Hubbell

The Sister — Anchor of the Home Front
b. April 1837, Champlain, NY • d. after 1872, Iowa
DescentScottish (McNeil)
OccupationTeacher
Letters To Her38+ received
HomeDover Twp, NY → Iowa

Frances, known as Fannie, was the eldest child of the Hubbell family and the only daughter. She was four years older than Henry and seven years older than Alexander. She worked as a teacher in Dover Township, likely helping support the family after their father's death.

Though none of Fannie's own letters survive in the collection, her presence is vivid in what the brothers wrote to her. She was the recipient of at least 38 letters — the brothers addressed her as "dear sister" and wrote to her separately from their mother, sometimes sharing different details or a more informal tone. Her role as the eldest sibling who stayed behind gave the brothers someone to confide in who was not their mother and would not worry the same way.

Fannie never married. Around 1872, she followed the family west to Iowa, where Alexander and Charles had settled after the war. The details of her later life are sparse — she appears in the family record primarily through the eyes of the brothers who loved her.
