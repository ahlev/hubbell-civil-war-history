Who They Were

A widowed mother. Four soldier sons. A sister who kept the home together.
These are the people behind the 274 letters.

The Hubbell family lived in Champlain, Clinton County, New York — a small farming town on the Canadian border near Lake Champlain. Frederick Augustus Hubbell died in 1853, leaving his wife Frances to raise five children alone. When the Civil War began eight years later, she sent every son she had.

What survives is not a family history written after the fact. It is 274 letters written during the war itself — by four brothers from camps, hospitals, marches, and battlefields, and by a mother who scanned the published casualty lists with trembling hands. They did not know how the story would end. Neither, reading them now, do we — until the letters stop.

Born in Charlotte, Vermont to a family of Scottish descent, Frances married Frederick Augustus Hubbell in 1833 and settled in Champlain. When Frederick died in 1853, she was left to raise six children alone. She was approximately 45 years old.

Her wartime letters reveal extraordinary strength — managing the home front alone while all four sons served simultaneously. She sent money she could not spare, shipped boxes of supplies, and endured long silences. She was the emotional center of the family, the person every son wrote to first.

After the war, Frances moved to Fonda, Iowa around 1872, following her surviving sons. She outlived three of her four soldier sons, dying at approximately age 82.

If I was a man I am sure I should enlist, but as it is I have sent all I had to send and hope they will do nobly.
Frances to a son — ~1862
I have wept until I am almost blind. I could not read Henry’s letters without weeping.
Frances, after learning of Henry’s death — 1862
Survived the war but lost three of her four sons. She outlived them all, dying in Iowa in 1890.

Henry was the eldest brother and the first to answer the call, enlisting in June 1861. His letters reveal a thoughtful young man with dry humor and a philosophical streak — noting he could spell “diarrhea” but not “rheumatism,” and reflecting that his entire military life felt dreamlike.

He served in the Eastern Theater through the Peninsula Campaign and Second Bull Run. His letters show someone who avoided the quarreling of camp life, preferring to write about landscape, food, and the small absurdities of army routine.

Henry’s last known letter was July 1862. On September 17 he was killed at the Battle of Antietam — the single bloodiest day of the war. His body was never recovered. The family learned through conflicting reports: a casualty list, then word he was wounded, then a soldier’s account of seeing his name on a headboard.

It is all like a dream, my being here. I can hardly realize that I have been from home so long.
Henry, from camp in Virginia — 1861
I have learned to spell diarrhea but can’t spell rheumatism yet.
Henry, on camp ailments — 1862
Killed in action at Antietam, September 17, 1862. He was approximately 22. His body was never recovered.

Alexander was just 17 when he enlisted — the youngest brother in uniform. Despite his age, he became the family’s most dedicated correspondent, writing 120 letters that form the backbone of the collection. Promoted from Corporal to Sergeant, he served in both theaters, fighting at Antietam, Lookout Mountain, and through Sherman’s Atlanta Campaign.

His letters reveal the most emotionally expressive of the brothers: fiercely patriotic, deeply homesick, unafraid to show vulnerability. He begged his mother for five dollars when the army didn’t pay for months. He was wounded at Lookout Mountain and suffered scurvy during the Atlanta Campaign.

After the war he settled in Iowa, married Lois Wood in 1887, and had four children. His daughter-in-law Gladys spent two and a half years transcribing these letters by typewriter in 1947. His grandson Fred wrote the family introduction in 1996.

I love my country ten times better… No, not ten times, but ten times ten times better than I ever did before.
Alexander, early in the war — 1861
I feel old. I have begun life’s battle for myself. I sometimes feel as though I was about 50 years old.
Alexander, age 20 — 1864
Survived the war. Settled in Iowa, married Lois Wood in 1887. Died Dec 7, 1894 at age 50. His descendants preserved the letters for five generations.

Charles was the most matter-of-fact of the brothers — practical, observant, and largely free of sentimentality. He enlisted in the 153rd New York alongside his younger brother James. Remarkably healthy throughout his service compared to his brothers, he kept a clear record of camp conditions.

He served in the Shenandoah Valley Campaign under Sheridan, including Cedar Creek. He was the one who confirmed Henry’s death, writing plainly: “I think there is not the least doubt that Henry was killed.”

After the war, Charles followed Alexander to Iowa and settled as a farmer. He died March 21, 1875 at about age 34 — from disease contracted during service. He survived the war only to be killed by it a decade later.

Did not enlist with the expectation of sleeping on a feather bed.
Charles, on camp conditions — 1863
I think there is not the least doubt that Henry was killed.

James was the youngest of the four soldier brothers, enlisting at about age 19 alongside Charles. His nine surviving letters are the fewest of any brother, but they reveal a lively personality — the kind of young man who apologized for getting hair oil on the writing paper and described hardtack with dark humor.

He served in the Shenandoah Valley alongside Charles and was wounded at Cedar Creek on October 19, 1864. He spent months in hospitals recovering. When the war ended, he was still too weakened to travel home.

James died on October 19, 1865 — exactly one year after being wounded — at Albany Hospital while being transported home from Savannah. The war was already six months over. He was approximately 22.

You will have to excuse me for getting hair oil on this paper.
James, in camp — 1863
Hardtack… is nothing more or less than flour and water mixed together and rolled out and cut in squares about three inches and baked or dried. And I tell you, you have got to have a pretty good set of grinders to eat them.
James, on army rations — 1863
Wounded at Cedar Creek, Oct 19, 1864. Died exactly one year later at Albany Hospital. The war had been over for six months.

Fannie was the eldest child and only daughter, four years older than Henry and seven years older than Alexander. She worked as a teacher in Dover Township, helping support the family after their father’s death.

Though none of Fannie’s own letters survive, her presence is vivid in what the brothers wrote. She received at least 38 letters — addressed as “dear sister,” sometimes sharing different details or a more informal tone than letters to their mother. She was confidante as much as sister.

Fannie never married. Around 1872 she followed the family west to Iowa. The details of her later life are sparse — she appears in the record primarily through the eyes of the brothers who loved her.

The brothers wrote to Fannie separately from their mother — sharing camp mischief, personal frustrations, and opinions they might not put in a letter home.
