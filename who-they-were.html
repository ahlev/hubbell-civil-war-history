Who They Were

A widowed mother. Four soldier sons. A sister who kept the home together.
These are the people behind the 273 letters.

The Hubbell family lived in Champlain, Clinton County, New York — a small farming town on the Canadian border near Lake Champlain. Frederick Augustus Hubbell died in 1853, leaving his wife Frances to raise five children alone. When the Civil War began eight years later, she sent every son she had.

What survives is not a family history written after the fact. It is 273 letters written during the war itself — by four brothers from camps, hospitals, marches, and battlefields, and by a mother who scanned the published casualty lists with trembling hands. They did not know how the story would end. Neither, reading them now, do we — until the letters stop.

Alexander was seventeen years old when he enlisted in September 1861, two weeks after Henry wrote home advising him to stay and go to college instead. He did not take the advice. Over the next three years and ten months he would serve in both the Eastern and Western theaters, fight at Antietam, Gettysburg, and Lookout Mountain, march through Sherman’s grinding Atlanta Campaign, and write 120 letters home — more than his three brothers combined. They form the backbone of this collection.

His first letter, from the barracks at Ogdensburg, described a boy dropped into chaos: straw berths, armed sentinels, a half-drunk fifer. By July 1862 he was calling himself one of the hardiest men in his company. At Antietam, on the bloodiest day of the war, he came through with only a bruise below the right knee from a nearly spent ball — then learned that Henry was missing. At Gettysburg, his regiment held Culp’s Hill through two days of assault; on the Fourth of July, 1863, he wrote home while burying the dead. A week later, camped seven miles from the Antietam battlefield, he resolved to visit it and find what remained of his brother.

In November 1863, transferred west with the Army of the Cumberland, he was wounded by a rifle ball in the side during the bayonet charge at Lookout Mountain. He recovered. The following summer, during the Atlanta Campaign, the army went weeks without fresh vegetables, and Alexander developed scurvy at twenty. From a hospital bed he underwent a political transformation — the young soldier who in 1862 had written that he hated abolitionists as much as rebels was, by 1864, ready to cast his first vote for Lincoln. Sick, exhausted, and uncertain he would survive the summer, he wrote: “How little the people at the North realize how much our country has cost us. It ought to be prized beyond rubies.”

His final wartime letter, written the night the muster-out order arrived, is the finest passage in the collection — a valedictory for the entire war, composed by a twenty-one-year-old sergeant who had served since he was seventeen. After the war, he settled on a farm in Fonda, Iowa, married Lois Wood, and lived quietly for nearly thirty years — the only Hubbell brother to reach old age. He died at fifty. His daughter-in-law Gladys would one day open a trunk in a basement, find his letters, and spend two and a half years transcribing them by typewriter.

God has again in his great kindness preserved my life through another most terrific battle. I have not received a scratch.
Alexander, July 4, 1863 — Gettysburg
I felt when in the battle, as tho I had a Mother upon her knees praying for me, and I took great comfort in the thought.
Alexander, November 25, 1862
Survived the war. Settled in Iowa, married Lois Wood in 1887. Died Dec 7, 1894 at age 50. His descendants preserved the letters for five generations.

Charles was the third brother to enlist and the most clear-eyed of them all. At the Plattsburgh recruiting station, Dr. Foote nearly rejected him for near-sightedness, but Charles had two brothers already in the Army and one at West Point, and the doctor relented. A family acquaintance told him afterward that he was “too honest.” It was as accurate a summary of his character as anyone would ever write.

He fought at Chancellorsville in May 1863, where his regiment lost seventy-three men in a single engagement and Lester Bond was shot through the forehead during a bayonet charge. He spent seven months investigating Henry’s fate through conflicting rumors and secondhand reports before a soldier named Abram Fry told him he had seen Henry’s name on a headboard at Antietam — a grave marker. Charles reported it with the bluntest statement in the collection: “I think there is not the least doubt that Henry was killed.”

His mother, working through the wife of Lieutenant Colonel Strain, arranged for Charles to be detached as a clerk at General Banks’s headquarters in New Orleans — a safer posting. He went without shame. But he did not stay safe. He carried dispatches on a steamer up the Red River under guerrilla fire, and by autumn 1864 was back in the field for Sheridan’s Shenandoah Valley Campaign. At Cedar Creek, on October 19, he and James came under surprise attack before dawn. James was wounded. Charles came through without a scratch. Two weeks later, he counted what was left of Company H: twenty-two men out of forty-five. In the presidential election, he cast one of only two votes for Lincoln in the entire company.

Charles survived the war and followed Alexander to Iowa. He died on March 21, 1875, at about thirty-four, from disease contracted during service. The war claimed him a decade after Appomattox.

Did not enlist with the expectation of sleeping on a feather bed.
Charles, December 4, 1862
I think there is not the least doubt that Henry was killed.
Charles, April 27, 1863
Died March 21, 1875 at age ~34 from disease contracted during service. The war claimed him a decade after Appomattox.

Henry was the eldest brother and the first to enlist, joining the 34th New York in June 1861, two months after Fort Sumter. He was twenty-one, a farmer’s son whose father had been dead for eight years. His mother was managing the farm in Champlain alone. He passed through Baltimore — where Massachusetts soldiers had been attacked by a secessionist mob weeks earlier — and noted bullet holes in the depot roof but reported that no one molested them.

His letters are the quietest in the collection. He tracked every dollar sent home with meticulous care, apologized for his spelling, and described his entire military life as dreamlike. On July 21, 1861, he sat in camp at Washington and wrote to his mother while listening to the cannons at Bull Run, twenty-eight miles away. That evening the Union army routed, and his regiment was ordered to prepare to move. The whiplash between ordinary life and catastrophe — shirt laundry in one paragraph, mobilization in the next — would define his correspondence. He served through the Peninsula Campaign and the Seven Days battles, where his regiment charged into the woods on the double quick and Henry lost his cap. He was chronically ill with diarrhea and refused to go to the hospital when the sick were ordered away.

His last letter, dated July 28, 1862, is the most relaxed he ever wrote — teasing his sister about French lessons, fantasizing about coming home in time for sugar season and spring farming. He closed with an instruction: “Burn this scribble — hope to do better next time.” They did not burn it. Fifty-one days later, on September 17, he was killed at the Battle of Antietam — the bloodiest single day of the war. His body was never recovered. The family spent seven months piecing together the truth from casualty lists, conflicting rumors, and a soldier’s account of seeing his name on a headboard in a field.

It is all like a dream, my being here, but if I ever go home I can say that I have seen a good deal of the world, by hard marching.
Henry, April 6, 1862
My diarrhea is very bad. Sherman, who was head doctor, is now in Congress.
Henry, July 12, 1862
Killed in action at Antietam, September 17, 1862. He was approximately 22. His body was never recovered.

Frances McNeil was born in Charlotte, Vermont, to a family of Scottish descent, and married Frederick Augustus Hubbell in 1833. They settled in Champlain, on the Canadian border, and raised six children. When Frederick died in 1853, she was left alone with the farm and five surviving children, the eldest barely sixteen. She managed for eight years. Then the war came, and she sent every son she had.

Her seventeen surviving letters are the emotional center of the collection — not because she wrote the most, but because she occupied the position no one else could: the woman who waited. She scanned the published casualty lists “with trembling,” searching for her sons’ names. She wrote to Henry four days after Antietam, not yet knowing he was dead, asking him to describe the battle “in the most minute detail.” When his name appeared on the wounded list, she wrote to Alexander in anguish: “My heart is aching to bursting, and I can write no more.” Only the want of money prevented her from traveling to the hospitals herself.

She was not passive. She shipped boxes of supplies, sent money she could not spare, and quietly worked the network of officers’ wives and family connections to secure safer assignments for her surviving sons — corresponding with Mrs. Strain, wife of Lieutenant Colonel Strain, to arrange Charles’s transfer to a clerk position in New Orleans. She was the person every son wrote to first, the address on nearly every envelope, the audience for every confession of homesickness and fear and faith.

After the war, Frances followed her surviving sons west to Fonda, Iowa, around 1872. She outlived three of her four soldier sons. She died in 1890, at approximately eighty-two, having buried a husband and three children and carried the rest to safety through the worst years of the republic.

If I was a man I am sure I should enlist. In reading the descriptions of the battle fields — my hair almost rises.
Frances, September 21, 1862
I have wept until I am almost blind. I can see his sweet face and hear his sweet voice.
Frances, May 4, 1863
Survived the war but lost three of her four sons. She outlived them all, dying in Iowa in 1890.

James was the youngest of the four brothers and the most scholarly, entering West Point while Henry fought on the Peninsula and Alexander drilled in Virginia. His early letters describe cadet life with a boy’s precision: lockers five feet long, one foot high, shared among three; older cadets dragging new arrivals around the grounds at night, trying to steal their guns. James held his. On September 21, 1862 — four days after Henry was killed at Antietam — James sat at his desk on the Hudson and wrote to his mother asking, “Have you heard from Henry and Alex yet?” He did not know.

He left the academy and joined the 153rd New York alongside Charles, entering the field in Louisiana in 1864. His nine surviving letters are the fewest of any brother, but they are vivid and particular. He defined hardtack for posterity — “Bake a flour and water cake with no salt or saleratus on a griddle till it is hard — throw it on a shelf for three or four days” — and described Southern children calling out, “Ma, there is a Yankee down cellar taking all our preserves and bacon.” He made rings on the march from Louisiana to Washington, whittling them while walking. Most broke in his pocket book. One survived, and he mailed it home in a letter.

At Cedar Creek, on October 19, 1864, he was wounded in the thigh during the Confederate surprise attack at dawn. He walked five miles to the field hospital, fashioning a bandage from his own underclothes, because the alternative was a Confederate prison. From a hospital bed in Baltimore, he cast his vote for Lincoln. By December, at Chestnut Hill Hospital in Philadelphia, the wound was nearly healed. He described the Thanksgiving dinner the hospital served — turkey and ice cream, all cold, piled on a plate, served at four in the afternoon because his ward was among the last.

James died on October 19, 1865 — exactly one year to the day after the bullet struck him at Cedar Creek. The war had been over for six months. He never made it home. The ring arrived before he did.

There is a little oil on the corner of my paper; you must excuse it as it is the best I have — my hair oil got upset.
James, August 6, 1861
Do you know what hardtack is! Bake a flour and water cake with no salt or saleratus on a griddle till it is hard — throw it on a shelf for three or four days and see what kind of a meal it will make.
James, August 15, 1864
Wounded at Cedar Creek, Oct 19, 1864. Died exactly one year later at Albany Hospital. The war had been over for six months.

Fannie was the eldest child and only daughter, four years older than Henry and seven years older than Alexander. In early 1862 she took a teaching position in Canada — Henry noted “Frances teaching in Montreal” (LTR-1862-01-09), and Alexander addressed a letter to her at “Longale, Canada,” encouraging her to “perfect yourself in the French language” (LTR-1862-01-25).

By spring 1864 she had returned home and risen to preceptress of Champlain Academy, prompting Charles to write, “Frances is preceptress of the Academy. She is rising in the world, but I am afraid it is too hard work for her” (LTR-1864-04-23).

Though none of Fannie’s own letters survive, the brothers craved hers. She received at least 38 letters across the war years — addressed as “dear sister,” often sharing a more informal tone than letters to their mother. She never married. Around 1872 she followed the family west to Iowa; the details of her later life are sparse.

“I have had nothing from her but a little scribble on a little scrap of paper for a long time. I shall have to pull her ears when I return.”
Alexander to Mother, July 8, 1862 (LTR-1862-07-08)
Taught in Montreal and at Champlain Academy during the war. Moved to Iowa around 1872 with the surviving family.
