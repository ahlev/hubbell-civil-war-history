## Full Transcription

> April 21st, 1862 — Monday
> Camp near York Town
>
> Dear James:
>
> When I sat down to answer your letter I almost forgot and thought it was Charles that wrote the letter, you have improved so much in writing. I guess you have been practicing some. Keep on and you will be a good pennman.
>
> As I am writing I can hear the rain beat on my tent. We have had a driving rain for some time. By the by, we have new tents, (the whole Regt). They are only made for summer, are not tall enough to stand in. They are made of linnin. We will have to carry them when we march.
>
> York Town is still in possession by the rebels, but we are preparing for them and will give them a good shpping one of these days. The drums are not beat morning nor does the band play for fear that the rebels will find us out and throw shells into our camp. Night before last our Regt. was routed up (driving rain in the bargain) by the heavy firing musketry. It seems the rebels attacked the New York 2nd Regt. Two of their companies were on Picket, and the rebels tryed very hard to drive them in, but did not make out. One of the New York 2nd said there was a thousand of them, but I do not believe it. An order has been read that if the pickets are attacked again the pickets are to fall back on their reserve and then to have reinforcements and drive them back (the rebels).
>
> I have just been cleaning my gun. We have inspection of arms every night at five o'clock and every morning we are obliged to be up at four with guns and drill till day light. It has cleared off some since morning. I would not think much of walking three or four miles to the sugar bush if I was at home, every morning and night for I would be sure of having a good night's rest, but here we are liable to be called up any time, but then there are better days coming. I can lay out all night on the ground and not have a cold. I often think of the time you and I made sugar last spring with pleasure. I thought then it was rather hard, but it is nothing to what I have been through, or may. Nothing more to write this time. Write often.
>
> I remain your aff. brother,
>
> Henry

## Editorial Notes

The silenced drums and band — "for fear that the rebels will find us out and throw shells into our camp" — mark the transition from garrison life to active siege. The NY 2nd Regiment picket attack is the closest combat Henry has witnessed, and the tactical order (fall back to reserve, then counterattack) shows evolving field doctrine. Henry's sugar-making nostalgia — "I often think of the time you and I made sugar last spring" — is his most tender sibling memory. Making maple sugar with James in Clinton County, spring 1861, was the last normal thing Henry did before the war.
