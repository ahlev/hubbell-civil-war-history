## Full Transcription

> Camp near Warrenton Junction
> July 30, 1863
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I have at length found leisure and materials to write you a few lines. We are now having a few days of rest after our long and painful marching and much we need it. I was nearly worn out when we reached this place, but am now fast gaining strength. Oh, of what we have endured you can have but a faint conception; nevertheless it has been done cheerfully and much more will be done to put down this war. The whole army is wonderfully pleased at this draft taking place and much merriment is excited as some familiar name is observed in the list of the drafted. Several commissioned officers and six sergeants have gone from this regiment after drafted conscripts. Each Co. is recruited up to eighty men, I believe. We have only about two hundred men left. I presume we shall be on the move soon, though we may wait for the conscripts.
>
> Please acknowledge to Mrs. Boyle the receipt of those papers and thank her warmly for me. You can have no idea what a blessing they are to myself and comrades. Your Tribunes and Mrs. Boyles' papers are now going the rounds of the Co. much to their pleasure and benefit. Mrs. Boyle sent me a paper with the list of drafted. Company H has a jolly time over it.
>
> So Albert Cook and the rest of the 34th are at home, eh! I wish them much joy. Tell Albert that I am again in the same region where I saw him last and that it is the same hot, parched up country as of old. I had hoped I should never see this part of the country again, but Providence has decreed it otherwise. The "Sunny South" is no home for me. It is too confoundedly sunny to suit my taste. I do hope and pray that this war may end soon. I hope Charleston will fall down. It is just what is needed to crush the rebellion, I think. I am sorry to hear that H. D. and others have got exempt and don't see how they have done it. How will their places be supplied? Will another draft take place? Shame on their cowardly, craven hearts. I hope the returned soldiers will give it to them as they deserve.
>
> I will endeavor to write you often if I can procure the materials which it will be very difficult to do as I have no money. Mother — I am sorry to say it — if you can, will you send me a little? If you can, be careful and send no other than U.S. money, as no other is good here. Love to all. I shall direct this letter to you, Miss Fannie.
>
> Yours with much affection
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
>
> P.S. I had the satisfaction of firing about fifty or sixty shots into the very teeth of the rebels at Gettysburg. They charged on us with hoots and yells, but stopped their clack pretty quick. They did screech, but it was out of the other corner of their mouths.

## Editorial Notes

The P.S. is the collection's best combat writing. Alexander waited until the postscript — almost an afterthought — to describe firing "fifty or sixty shots into the very teeth of the rebels at Gettysburg." The rebel charge came "with hoots and yells" — the famous rebel yell — and Alexander's regiment stopped them cold. "They did screech, but it was out of the other corner of their mouths." This is a soldier who enjoys having won.

The contempt for draft dodgers is white-hot. "Shame on their cowardly, craven hearts." Alexander has survived Antietam, Chancellorsville, and Gettysburg. His regiment is down to 200 men. He has earned the right to his anger.
