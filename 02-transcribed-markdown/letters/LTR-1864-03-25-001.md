## Full Transcription

> March 25, 1864
> Headquarters Dept. of the Gulf
> New Orleans, La.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I have anxiously been expecting a letter for some time, but as yet have rec'd none. Should like to hear about the boys and how you and Frances get along. But I suppose that I will get one after a while. Perhaps there may be a letter for me with the regt. and it has not got in yet.
>
> I am still at these Head Quarters but think I shall not remain here much longer. Have rec'd. no detail yet from these Hd. Qtrs. They tell me my writing is stiff. I think that I can not get to the regt. very readily now and will have to wait a few days. But I am not gone yet, so please direct your letters as above. If I am not here the letter can be forwarded me from this place. If I leave here, I will let you know as soon as I leave. The regt. must have been through some hardships by this time and I have escaped them, so that if I go back to regt. I have profited so much.
>
> A day or two since a woman was killed by the street car. She was standing on the track looking at the Military Co. and did not notice the car coming till the mule was almost on her, and then she was so scared that she ran against the mule, was knocked down, and run over. She was laid on the walk some time waiting for the coroner to come, and was covered with a piece of old carpeting. She was a poor, white woman.
>
> The streets in this city are kept very clean and are well paved. It is a large city and is called the Crescent City because it is built on a bend in the river in the shape of a crescent.
>
> Our army has had two or three victories on the Red River in western Louisiana where it now is, I suppose. You must have, or will, see an account of it. It captured Fort DeRussy, also the town of Alexandria, and has taken a great many prisoners. It is on its way to Texas. — Capt. Fox of our regt. has been dismissed from the service for disloyalty and conduct unbecoming the character of an officer and a gentleman.
>
> The weather for the last few days has been quite cold so that we have kept a fire in the fireplace. The niggers say it is because the whole North has come down here that it is so cold. It costs a good deal to live in this city as everything is very dear. A picayune which is 5¢ is the smallest change that circulates here.
>
> This is called a wicked city and I suppose it is. There are two or three theatres here which are nightly attended. I have not been to one and don't mean to go to them. I hope you don't worry about us boys, but take things as they come, and make the best of them. Should like to know whether James has enlisted. It is a long time since I heard from home, some time before we left Wash. This letter is written in haste, so as to go on the steamer in the morning, so please excuse writing, etc. My health is very good, and I hope that you all are well. Please write often and tell the boys to write to me and I will write to them. From your aff. son, C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

The woman killed by the mule-drawn street car is one of Charles's most vivid vignettes — watching a military parade, not seeing the car, running into the mule in panic, "covered with a piece of old carpeting" while waiting for the coroner. "She was a poor, white woman." The racial qualifier is deliberate — in occupied New Orleans, the social taxonomy matters.

The "niggers say it is because the whole North has come down here that it is so cold" — Black humor about the Northern occupation, reported by Charles without comment. The picayune as smallest change — New Orleans's distinct currency culture. Charles is a tourist in an occupied city, observing everything with his usual precision while his regiment marches toward disaster on the Red River.
