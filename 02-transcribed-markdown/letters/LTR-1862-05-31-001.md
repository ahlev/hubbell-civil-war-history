## Full Transcription

> May 31st, 1862
> Harpers Ferry
>
> My Dear Mother —
>
> I must write you at least a few lines. We do not know how soon the rebels may attack us. We slept on our arms last night and have done so ever since we have been here. Our batteries opened on the rebels strongly yesterday and set them scampering in fine style from some heights they had the boldness to show themselves on. A part of our Regt. was kept in line all last night and several times the whole Regt. was called out. Every night except one since we have been here the Regt. has been called out. We sleep in the open air. Last night we made ourselves a slight shelter to keep off the rain by spreading some tents on boards inclined against a fence. We are stationed behind a powerful battery. If the rebels do attack us, there will be an awful battle. There goes a shell from one of the batteries to keep the rebels awake. It does me good to hear our shells shriek and howl over our heads. We are about to shell the town to prevent the rebel sharpshooters from getting in and picking off our men. The few remaining inhabitants have been warned to leave. Such is war. I have once been out on picket guard. We were called in about three the next day. The rebels had begun throwing shot and shell at our camp. Our batteries replied in fine stile and I understand dismounted a rebel gun and killed several. A rebel deserter came and gave himself up to us. We sent him into Camp. When we were coming in a rebel shot struck within about ten rods of us.
>
> I received your letter and Charles yesterday. Please write often. I do not know when I can write you again. Do not worry. My life is in God's hands. Corporal Luther sends his regards to you all. Remember me to Mrs. Brinkerhoff.
>
> Your affectionate son
> (Corporal Hubbell)
> Alexander
>
> I am enjoying excellent health and spirits.

## Editorial Notes

This is Alexander's transformation letter — from garrison soldier to combat soldier in four days. The man who "should feel rather cheap to come home without having been in one battle" is now sleeping on his arms behind a battery, with rebel shells falling within 165 feet. His exhilaration is unmistakable: "It does me good to hear our shells shriek and howl over our heads." The "(Corporal Hubbell)" signature — his first use of formal rank in closing — may signal a new seriousness. "Such is war" — his one-sentence reflection on shelling civilians out of their homes — is the most mature observation in his letters. The parallel with Henry is striking: both brothers find combat stimulating rather than terrifying, at least in their first encounters with it. Alexander's Harpers Ferry action coincides with the end of Stonewall Jackson's Valley Campaign, when Union forces were scrambling to trap Jackson and defend the Shenandoah crossings.
