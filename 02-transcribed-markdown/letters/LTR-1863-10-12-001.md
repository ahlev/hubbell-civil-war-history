## Full Transcription

> October 12, 1863
> Capitol Hill
> Washington, D.C.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your letter enclosing Frances' letter of Oct. 4th in due time. Was glad to hear from you both.
>
> About Alex, I do not know how soon you will hear from him, but as they are so unsettled, it may be some time. He told me that he would write me as soon as he could. He was in good spirits when he left here and all the men seemed in good spirits.
>
> Direct his letters the same as usual and he will get them. I think it will be perfectly useless to try to get him discharged. The most they can do is to put him in the "Invalid Corps." Alex may prefer not to go into that, but perhaps had rather stay here he is doing that.
>
> Yesterday I attended church in the forenoon and after dress parade a minister from one of the churches in this place preached us a short sermon. Every Sunday night we have preaching at this hour, but very short sermons, which is a good thing these cold nights. In the evening I attended an experience meeting. The Chaplain related his experiences first and was followed by others. The singing was good. We have a Roaring Methodist in our Co. He has a good pair of lungs and makes plenty of noise with them. No one is obliged to go and hear the Chaplain. It is all voluntary. A school is held here every evening in the week except Sunday. A good many are learning to read and write in this regt. These schools and meetings are held in the mess rooms of the companies in rotation, one week in each Co. A few days since there was a grand parade of colored Odd Fellows in this city. They looked very well as they marched through the streets in their uniforms and with gay banners. They had a n****r band. The rear of the procession was brought up with n****r women riding in coaches, alongside white women. Policemen were on the watch to see that they were not disturbed.
>
> 13th: 7:00 P.M. Alex Douglas left at 6:30 this evening for home. He has a fifteen days furlough. I don't know as I want anything except socks and I now have two pairs of them. If you have any apples I should like a few of them if Alex will bring them. Two for 5¢ is the price of apples here. He dried apples for a long time. I think they are good. Have not had any lately. I think I was never wrote you that Major Strain's wife was here some time since. I don't know how long she stayed here.
>
> I think very likely our regt. will stay its time out — two years more. If the war ends soon an army will have to be kept up, but it don't look now as if it would be over very soon. If Rosecrans should be defeated, I don't know but it would be a seven years war, unless there was foreign interference. But I guess Russia keeps her fleet here to be ready if France or England troubles us to take our part.
>
> Please write soon. Have you sold your wool and if so how much did you get for it? I think about the wood, I think it will be the best plan to hire it cut by the cord. I don't know which place it had better be cut on. It seems to me that there is not much wood to cut on the farm. Don't you think it will be a good plan to have those elm logs drawn from Sid's pasture near our house? If it can be split, it will make good wood.
>
> From your aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

The Colored Odd Fellows parade is a snapshot of wartime Washington's social upheaval. Black citizens parading through the capital's streets in uniforms, with police protection — something unimaginable three years earlier. Charles records Black women riding in coaches "alongside white women" as remarkable but doesn't editorialize. His use of racial slurs is typical of the era, not a marker of unusual hostility.

Charles's geopolitical awareness is striking: he knows about the Russian fleet, understands the French/English intervention threat, and can game out war scenarios based on Rosecrans's success or failure. These are not simple farm boys — they are informed citizens following a complex war.
