## Full Transcription

> Dec. 16, 1861
> Camp Jackson
>
> My dear Sister:
>
> I wonder what sort of weather you are having up there. Pretty cold I guess, eh? The weather here is warm and sunshiney — very much like our September weather.
>
> We came very near losing our Captain the other night but then "an inch of a miss", you know, "is as good as a mile." I feel very, very thankful that his life was spared to the Company. I hardly think his place could have been filled. The Major paid him quite a compliment the other day. He said to the Company "You have a good Capt." and that was a great deal for him.
>
> Now I suppose you would like to hear how Capt. Ransom came so near losing his life. It was about as narrow an escape as I ever heard of. The only marvel is that he is now living. I can almost hear you say, "Out with it you provoking boy." Well, "here goes". The Capt. was going the "Grand Rounds". One of the officers is obliged to go over the line every night between the hours of one and six to see that the sentries are awake on their posts doing their duty and are properly instructed. This is called the "Grand Rounds". A freight train just then passed and as it was going slow he jumped on to ride out to one of the pickets about a quarter of a mile distant. (The freight engines on this road are different from those up North. They are fed with coal. The cabin is directly over the boiler which extends back so that the fireman standing upon a platform of the tender (which is open in front) is able to shovel coal into it. The Capt. stood upon the platform with the fireman. He (the fireman) had just commenced "firing up" when the boiler burst blowing the engineer, I presume, two or three hundred feet into the air. In his descent he fell through a tree breaking a limb about three inches in thickness, and on xxxx striking the ground he bounded and fell some fifteen or twenty feet from where he first struck. The Capt. stood still for a moment "thunder struck" I suppose when it occurred to him that the cars might come pileing one upon another, as they had been under some headway. He stepped off and started to go up the bank (about twenty feet in heighth.) When about half way up the body of a soldier (Haine, I believe) fell close beside him and pieces of the wreck all around him. If some of those large pieces had struck him they would have killed him instantly. The dome (weighing I should judge a ton perhaps, two, three or four) was thrown some thirty rods from the engine. The engineer was thrown about twenty-five rods. You can have some idea how high the men were thrown from the time it took them to fall.
>
> The Capt. soon heard some one calling for help and immediately went in search of him. He found the fireman on fire and extinguished the fire by throwing water upon him from the ditch with his hands. The fireman died a day or two after. The Capt. is the only one that escaped alive of those upon the engine. He did not receive even a scratch. The fireman was so badly burned that he died, and Capt. Ransom standing beside him not even scratched. We certainly have great reason to be thankful.
>
> Now, Miss Fannie, I want you to write me often — very often — once a week certainly. You tell James and Charles if they don't write me more often I will pull their ears for them when I get home. I certainly shall and they may look out for their noses, too. I have received four papers and letter dated Dec. 9 since I last wrote. You would never neglect writing me if you knew how much good that letter did me. Do not forget to send me some postage stamps to frank our letters. The ladies of Chazy are making up a box to send to those in the Company who came from there. I wish the ladies of Champlain would follow their example. Some warm woolen gloves would be very acceptable indeed.
>
> Mr. Luther wrote Amos for a list of the names of those who came from Chaney. I do not have many idle moments. I am on guard duty tomorrow if all is well. I was up to Baltimore yesterday to church. I will tell you about it in another letter. I have sent my "picture" home. I will let you and Mother settle which shall have it. Capt. Ransom was very, very, very, very kind to me when I was sick. He came often to see me and brought me toast and tea a number of times. I wish Mother would write him a note. It would please him. Love to all.
>
> Your affectionate brother,
>
> Alexander.
>
> P.S. I see by the papers that there is danger of war with England. Let her come, I am as ready to fight Britons as Southerners. If she does interfere I hope my brothers and every other able bodied man will turn out and help us fight. I hope our regiment will be sent up the frontier.

## Editorial Notes

This is the most technically detailed eyewitness account in the collection. Alexander's description of the boiler explosion reads like a disaster investigation report: the coal-fed engine design, the fireman's position on the tender platform, the sequence of the blast (engineer thrown 200-300 feet, falling through a tree; dome thrown 30 rods; fireman on fire in the ditch), and Ransom's reaction ("stood still for a moment 'thunder struck'"). The detail about the engineer bouncing on impact and landing 15-20 feet from first contact is forensically specific.

Ransom's heroism (finding and extinguishing the burning fireman) and his earlier kindness to Alexander during illness ("very, very, very, very kind") explain Alexander's deep loyalty to his captain. The Major's compliment — "You have a good Capt." — reinforces Ransom's reputation.

The Trent Affair P.S. is historically significant: "I am as ready to fight Britons as Southerners... I hope our regiment will be sent up the frontier." Alexander, stationed near Baltimore, imagines his regiment deployed to the Canadian border — the frontier he grew up near in Clinton County. This places the Hubbell correspondence within the Anglo-American diplomatic crisis that nearly expanded the Civil War into an international conflict.
