## Full Transcription

> Sept. 27, 1862
> Hospital at Harpers Ferry
>
> My Dear Mother —
>
> I suppose you will be both surprised and grieved to hear that I am in a hospital. I have had an exhausting diarrhea for several weeks past, which with the severest military duty has brought me pretty low. I feel much better today and think that with proper care and nourishment I shall get along finely. Orderly Dickinson was down here this morning and purchased some paper and some envelopes and a loaf of bread for me (with his own money). He is very, very, very kind to me. That bread will do me a world of good. I wish you could by some means or token express my gratitude to him. I can never forget his kindness and only hope I may find some means of repaying it. He showed himself every inch a man in the last fight.
>
> I have heard from Henry. He is slightly wounded in the hand, but is still with the company. When I am able I will try and give you some of the particulars of the fight.
>
> I am now, Dear Mother, going to ask a great favor, and it is only the stearnest necessity that makes me do it. Can you send me some money. You know, Mother, what it is to be away from home, sick among strangers and to be without money. I will not try to describe my feelings when I ask for this. You know them all. We shall have four months pay due us the first of Nov. ($52) and I shall be able to return it to you ten fold. Do you suppose you could spare me five dollars, if not, less will do. I will leave you to judge how painful it is for me to ask this of you. If you have not got it to spare do not send it. I do not wish you to deny yourself. I shall try and write often.
>
> Your affectionate son
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
>
> Direct to Washington and I will always get the letter.

## Editorial Notes

This letter is a study in pride and desperation. Alexander, who wrote from a mountaintop about sunsets and college plans two months ago, is now in a hospital asking his mother for five dollars. The emotional architecture is careful: he softens the blow of his hospitalization first (feels better, proper care), then delivers the good news about Henry (alive, only a hand wound), and only then makes his request — and even then he hedges: "if not, less will do," "if you have not got it to spare do not send it." The triple-underlined "very" for Orderly Dickinson's kindness suggests how starved Alexander is for basic comfort. A loaf of bread "will do me a world of good." This is what war does to a twenty-year-old corporal who wrote poetry about the Blue Ridge eight weeks earlier. The resolution of Henry's disappearance is almost thrown away — a single sentence in the middle of the letter — but it's the news that matters most: Henry is alive, wounded in the hand, still with his company. After Sedgwick's disaster in the West Woods, this is remarkable.
