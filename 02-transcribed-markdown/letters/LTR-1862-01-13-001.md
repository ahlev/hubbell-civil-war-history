## Full Transcription

> Jan. 13, 1862
> Baltimore
>
> My Dear Mother:
>
> The mail has arrived and no letter. What is the matter? The last letter I received from you is dated "Dec. 20". I received one from Charles dated "Dec. 22". That is the last I have received from home. What can be the reason? Are you all sick or are you dead? Something must be the matter for I know you would not forget me. What can be the matter? I am afraid you are sick. Do not keep me in suspense any longer. Let some one of the family write. Why don't Frances write and James, and Charles. Well — well — it certainly is strange. I have a great deal of news to write, but "shant" write a word until I hear from home.
>
> One of our pickets (from our Company) was killed on the ninth of this month. I went out and helped to bring him in and saw a sight that but few of our Company could bear (I have my courage "screwed up" to almost anything). Now, my dear mother, if you want me to describe the affair, funeral and all, you must write me. (I hope to get a letter in this way). I have my letters filed, and they will show whether my friends are faithful in writing or not. I received a letter from Henry mailed on the sixth and was very glad to hear from him. He says, speaking of the New Year "who knows whether at the end of 1862 we will all be in the land of the living. It matters not when we leave this world if we are only right in the sight of God." I was very glad to have him say this for it showed me he had not forgotten his God, as most in the camp seem to have done.
>
> I have not much spirit to write. Please write soon. Love to all.
>
> Your affectionate son,
>
> Alexander

## Significance Flags

### Historical Significance: `major`

> Alexander writes home from Baltimore, frantic that no letter has come since December 20 — "Are you all sick or are you dead?" Then the harder news: a picket from his own Company was killed on the ninth, and he helped bring in the body, "a sight that but few of our Company could bear." He quotes Henry's New Year letter — "who knows whether at the end of 1862 we will all be in the land of the living" — glad his brother "had not forgotten his God."

### Emotional Intensity: `high`

> Frantic about silence from home ("Are you all sick or are you dead?"). Withholds news as leverage to get letters. Moved by Henry's religious reflection.

## Editorial Notes

Two threads converge here: Alexander's anxiety about home silence and his first brush with violent death. The picket killed on January 9 shook him — he helped recover the body and saw "a sight that but few of our Company could bear." His decision to withhold the story unless the family writes is both manipulative and understandable — a 19-year-old's leverage play born of genuine loneliness. The quoted passage from Henry's letter is the most poignant moment in the collection so far: "who knows whether at the end of 1862 we will all be in the land of the living." Henry did not survive 1862 — he was killed at Antietam on September 17. Alexander's appreciation of Henry's faith ("it showed me he had not forgotten his God") adds retrospective weight to a letter that would have read as routine at the time.
