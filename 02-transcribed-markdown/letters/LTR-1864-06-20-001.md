## Full Transcription

> June 20th, 1864
> Camp in field near
> Marietta, Ga.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> By the kind providence of God I am permitted to write you again, and inform you that I am still uninjured. We have been fighting nearly every day since I last wrote you and working the greater portion of every night (and sometimes the whole) entrenching ourselves in positions gained the previous day. I shall not attempt to give you any detailed acct. of our proceedings. Suffice it to say that everything thus far is proceeding favorably. During the last week we have driven the enemy from two strong lines of entrenchments and are now burrowing away at him in his third. Yesterday was the Holy Sabbath, and I suppose, was passed by you in quiet and meditation. But far different was it with the 60th N.Y. Vets. We were the advance regt. of the division yesterday, being deployed as skirmishers, and were kept engaged with the enemy from two P.M. until after ten o'clock last night — when we were relieved.
>
> The list of killed and wounded in our regt. is beginning to pile up fearfully. None of the boys from Champlain have been injured. Our Regt. was relieved from the front line this morning by the 2nd, and we now rest a little in the rear of the line of battle but not entirely out of range as the whistle of an occasional bullet plainly tells us. Our batteries are keeping up a pretty steady fire. The skirmish lines are also pretty hotly engaged. The rebel sharpshooters are at present keeping up a pretty lively fire on one of our batteries on the hill just above us, but with not much effect.
>
> Please write often. I will write as often as possible. Keep a stout heart. We are all in God's hands. May He grant us all submission to His will.
>
> Have you heard from James yet? His silence is singular and I think very wrong. The reason of it I think is that he feels his failure keenly and he wishes to make something of a mark in the world before he writes you, so that he may be able to compensate you for your disappointment in his failure. I received your letter of May 21st a few days since, also a N.Y. Tribune and papers from Mrs. Boyle. Please write upon the margin of your papers how you all are as I receive the papers oftener than the letters. The mail seem to be delayed at Chattanooga, I suppose from the difficulty of transportation. Love to all. I hope I will come out of this campaign all right, but if God orders it otherwise may we all meet in heaven. My home and trust is in Jesus our Saviour. Much love to Frances and tell her I shall if possible write her soon.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
>
> How little the people at the North realize how much our country has cost us. It ought to be prized beyond rubies after this is over. I wrote you in my last to send me a little money if you could do so without inconvenience. This letter may reach you before the other — that is the reason I speak of it. If you can send it please do so. Inclose a silk handkerchief. I need one much. I also want you in one of the letters to send me a silk handkerchief. Send it by mail in the form of a package if you can send it without inconvenience.

## Editorial Notes

"How little the people at the North realize how much our country has cost us. It ought to be prized beyond rubies after this is over." This is Alexander's political testament — the veteran's claim on the nation's gratitude, written while bullets whistle and the dead pile up. The postscript is more urgent than the letter: the country's cost counted in blood, not dollars.

The campaign has become industrial — fight, entrench, fight, entrench, every day and every night. "Working the greater portion of every night (and sometimes the whole) entrenching ourselves in positions gained the previous day." Sleep is rare, writing is stolen time, and the killed-and-wounded list grows. Alexander's faith deepens as the danger intensifies: "My home and trust is in Jesus our Saviour."

The repeated requests for a silk handkerchief — in this letter, the last letter, the letter before that — suggest the small comforts that sustain a soldier. He needs it badly enough to ask three times.
