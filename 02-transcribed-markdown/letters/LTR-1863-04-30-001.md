## Full Transcription

> April 30, 1863
> Champlain, N.Y.
>
> Dear Sir:
>
> My son, Henry Hubbell, of Co. D, 34th N.Y. reg't., has been missing since the battle of Antietam. He fell as the enemy charged, and was not seen after. The surgeon assures me that he sent trusty men to search the field after the battle, that every union soldier killed was fully identified, and that my son certainly was not among those. His belief is that he was slightly wounded and taken prisoner.
>
> The Col. thought he was taken to the hospital. He was in very feeble health some time previous to the battle.
>
> The Rev. ---- advises me to write to you with a request from him to ascertain if possible, where he is, or may have been. If he is not living, it would be a great satisfaction to me to know when and where he died, whether in one of our own or a rebel hospital. I have noticed in a late paper that many of our soldiers are in an insane asylum at Williamsburg, Va. I sometimes fear he may be among them. He was of an extremely nervous temperament, and very much reduced in health.
>
> That the Lord will direct and give you success in this matter, is the prayer of his afflicted/ mother,
>
> Frances M. Hubbell.

These cannot both be right — either the surgeon's search was incomplete, or Henry was not killed on the field itself.

## Editorial Notes

This is the only letter written by Frances M. Hubbell in the entire collection, and it may be the most important document in it. A mother writes to an unknown authority, marshaling every piece of evidence she has: the surgeon searched and Henry wasn't among the dead. The Colonel thinks he was taken prisoner. He was sick and nervous before the battle. And then the devastating fear: he might be in an insane asylum in Virginia.

The letter is a masterpiece of controlled desperation. Every sentence is calibrated to extract action from a bureaucracy — the reverend's endorsement, the specific regiment and company, the concrete question about Williamsburg. But beneath the formal language is a woman who has been living with uncertainty for seven months and is running out of explanations that don't end in death.

The contradictions this letter raises are significant: if the surgeon truly identified every Union dead and Henry wasn't among them, then the Abram Fry report of a headboard grave is wrong — or the surgeon's search was less thorough than claimed.
