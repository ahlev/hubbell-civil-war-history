## Full Transcription

> Jan. 25, 1862
> Baltimore, Md.
>
> My dearest Sister:
>
> I received your unfinished but welcomed letter some days since and you may be sure I was glad to hear that you had not forgotten me. How do you like your new situation. I am glad you have obtained what you have so long wished for — a situation in Canada where you may perfect yourself in the French language. How lonely, very lonely, Mother must be this winter in that great castle of a house with no one to speak to all day long, not even the old cat for company for I had her shot before I left home. I hope you will write her often. I shall try to. We must not neglect this, for she writes me .... (a portion of this letter cut out at this point.) .......
>
> ...... I heard a great roaring and rushing behind me. I looked back and saw a passenger train coming with great velocity hardly ten feet from me. The engineer just at that moment whistled an alarm. I gave one jump — Jump — that don't half express it. It was a half-down all into one. I had so little time to get out of the way that I verily believe it would almost have brushed my coat tails, if I had had them. One of our men, Edwin Porter, was killed some nights since by the cars. Mother will tell you about it.
>
> I think our Regiment will leave Baltimore soon. I am not at all sorry. I wish we could go on some of the "naval expeditions." If we leave here I think we will be sent to Washington to garrison some of the fortifications left vacant when the grand army moves forward.
>
> Now, Miss Fannie, you are in a land where they are not very friendly to the United States. Stand up for your country. You can use your tongue for that country if you can not take up arms in her defense. I charge you be valiant. I believe the hate towards England of 1776 has sprung up in my bosom. Please write often and do not forget to write Mother.
>
> Your affectionate Brother
>
> Alexander F.
>
> Addressed to: Miss Fannie Hubbell
> Longale, Canada

## Editorial Notes

Alexander's near-miss with the train is told with characteristic humor — "I gave one jump — Jump — that don't half express it. It was a half-down all into one" — but the juxtaposition with Porter's death by the same railroad makes it chilling. Alexander was guarding the very tracks that nearly killed him, and that did kill his comrade. The physically cut portion of the letter is a tantalizing gap in the record — someone (the transcriber? an earlier family member?) removed content between Alexander's concern for Mother and the train incident. The charge to Frances — "Stand up for your country. You can use your tongue for that country if you can not take up arms in her defense" — is remarkable: Alexander deputizing his sister as a patriotic advocate in British Canada, where anti-Union sentiment ran high. The detail about shooting the cat before leaving for war is startlingly practical — a young man tying up loose ends before going to fight.
