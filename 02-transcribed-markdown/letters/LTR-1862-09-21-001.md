## Full Transcription

> Sept 21, 1862
> West Point Academy
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> You must be very lonely since Charles has left. How did he enlist? I have been expecting a letter every day, but have been disappointed. I hope he did not go as a private, but then if he did, by being on the lookout and proving efficient — which I know he will — he can easily get promoted. I suppose you will say to yourself, "he talks a great deal about getting an office", I suppose I do, but then when a man enlists I think he ought to look out for his own interests. Won't you send me his address and also that of the boys, if there is any place where a letter can be directed, so that it will reach them. Have you heard from Henry and Alex yet? You said in your last letter that you had not heard from them for some time.
>
> You asked me how I stood in my class: I am now in the third section; the whole class is divided into six. They have been trying us for three weeks, and have placed us in the different sections according to our merits. The transfers were read out last night at parade. I am section marcher of the third as my name comes first alphabetically. A man in the third section is regarded as having a very good standing in his class, but I shall try to go up higher, and I think, can. I think if I could have had my choice of the sections, I would have chosen the third. Yes; I know I would.
>
> But it is time for the bible class, and I will finish this after supper.
>
> We had a very interesting class, and became so interested that the first drum beat for parade before we were aware of the lateness of the hour. I came very near getting a late and so did all, as we were obliged to go to our rooms and get our trimmings and get out there before the second drum which beats five minutes after the first. As it was we were obliged to fall in ranks with shoes unblackened, but Mr. Michie, the Cad Lieut, of our company, was very kind and did not look at our shoes. Prof. French gave each of us a prayer book, who was without one, and he also gave us each a book entitled "The Bible and Men of Learning". We cadets, by ourselves, have prayer meetings twice a week. They are attended by quite a number from each of the four classes; the first classmen generally lead the meeting.
>
> But I must close as it is now near ten and at that time every light must be out. I delayed finishing this until late in the evening. Ask Frances if she has forgotten me. P.S. That young Hoyt Uncle Hubbell spoke of was thrown down into the immortals. The "Immortal Section" is the sixth, the lowest.
>
> Your aff. son
>
> James

## Editorial Notes

James writes this letter on September 21, 1862 — four days after Antietam, the bloodiest day in American history — and has no idea. He's asking about class sections and Bible class and whether Frances has forgotten him. Henry is missing on a battlefield in Maryland. Alexander is camped below Maryland Heights with a bruised knee and sick with worry. Charles is drilling at Plattsburgh with his near-sighted eyes. Mother is home alone, likely receiving Alexander's devastating letter within days. And James is worried about getting his shoes blackened before parade. The irony is not his fault — it's the war's. Information travels slowly. The family communication network, routed entirely through Champlain, means James won't know about Antietam for perhaps another week. His question — "Have you heard from Henry and Alex yet? You said in your last letter that you had not heard from them for some time" — is the most innocent sentence in the collection. "Mr. Michie, the Cad Lieut, was very kind and did not look at our shoes" — this is what September 21 looks like from West Point.
