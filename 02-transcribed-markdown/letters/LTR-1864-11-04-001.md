## Full Transcription

> Nov. 4, 1864
> Camp at Cedar Creek, Va.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I read your letter announcing grandmothers death two days since. Was not surprised as she was so old. I suppose Aunt Mary will come North if she can get away.
>
> Have not heard from James nor any of our wounded in the last fight. Two of our Co. who saw him say that his wound is not dangerous. Our Chaplain saw him in the hospital sitting up in bed. He told me James was doing well. I asked the Chaplain if his wound was serious. He said he did not know. I told him that James walked to the hospital. He then said it must be a flesh wound. Our Chaplain is none home sick. The badly wounded were at the hospital at Winchester. The others are forwarded on to the other hospitals. James Lucas, I heard, lay on the ground all day till he was taken up by an ambulance. I did not see him. However that I ought to write his folks, but if you will tell them that he was shot then he head and an arm it will do as well. Our Chaplain told me that he probably would not recover. I do not whether James is at Winchester or been sent on to some other city. The morning of the fight there were no ambulances and if a man could not walk he had to lay on the ground unless some of the soldiers carried him. Segt. Pelkier, of our town, shot in the leg, was carried by some of our Co. & left in a barn to be made prisoner of by the rebs as was supposed, but when we came back that night he was there.
>
> We now drill & have dress parade, regular camp life. Do not know how long we will stay here, but think not long. De Sweeney was taken prisoner the morning of the fight, but got away from the rebs that night. I do not know how long we will stay here, but think not long. De Sweeney was taken prisoner the morning of the fight, but got away from the rebs that night. Did not know what had become of James until that night after dark.
>
> Sunday, 6th: This morning we were called up at 4 oclock & fell into line with our arms. It looked as if they expected an attack, but after standing till daylight we broke ranks & cooked our breakfast. After eating we had Brigade Review & inspection.
>
> I sent my vote to Silas, voted for Lincoln. Only two votes for Lincoln in our Co.
>
> (Remainder of letter missing) From,
>
> C. F. Hubbell (Charles)

## Editorial Notes

"Only two votes for Lincoln in our Co." — Charles is one of two Lincoln men in a McClellan company. The 153rd NY's company is exactly the border-state/Irish constituency Alexander described: soldiers who fight for the Union but vote against Lincoln. Charles votes his conscience and counts himself in the minority.

The company has 22 men left. Two fights in the Shenandoah — 23 killed and wounded. The math is brutal: the company is nearly destroyed. "In the fight bullets flew all around me but I came out without a scratch." Charles's survival is statistical improbability.

"The morning of the fight there were no ambulances and if a man could not walk he had to lay on the ground unless some of the soldiers carried him." Cedar Creek's medical reality: wounded men on the ground with no help coming.
