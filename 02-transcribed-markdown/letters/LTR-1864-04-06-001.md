## Full Transcription

> April 6, 1864
> Stevenson, Ala.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> The last letter I rec'd. from home was dated March 14th. I have, I believe rec'd two papers from home since then. I am feeling very anxious about you all. Where is Charles and have you heard from James? I am very anxious to hear from him. Please give me his address as soon as possible, also Charley's, although I suppose his will be the same as usual.
>
> We are under marching orders. An order was read last night at Parade ordering Brigade Commanders to have everything prepared that is necessary for the coming campaign. May God be with us!
>
> Mrs. Hoyle is again sending me papers. Please thank her warmly for me. Say, Mother, please find out if you can who in Champlain voted against soldiers voting. I see there were twenty-five votes cast against it. Whoever did deserves to be (and I may say will be) blasted with eternal infamy. What class of men are more vitally interested in the success of this Gov't. than the soldiers? What liberties would the Northern people enjoy if it were not for the soldiers? I wish they would think of it. If M.M. Bass cast a vote I am sure it was against.
>
> The mail here has not yet come in. I shall not send this until it does. I hope I shall get a letter. You complain that I do not write often enough. I think it is now your turn to complain now. I have very little heart to write when I do not get an answer to my letters.
>
> Everything is moving on here at the usual pace. Our reg't. is relieved from doing picket duty. It is now done by other regt's. of the Brigade. We have sent all our surplus baggage to the rear. I sent my overcoat off yesterday. I do hope I shall get a letter today.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
>
> P.S. The 11th & 12th Army Corps have consolidated and now form the Army Corps under command of Gen. Hooker. Gen. Slocum, commands of the 12th Corps, has been assigned to the command of Vicksburg. We shall probably have a hard summer. General Hooker never cusses his men. I hope we shall be able to give the rebels a finishing blow this summer. The mail will not be in till afternoon.
>
> 6 P.M. The mail has come in and no letter. I was very much disappointed. I fear all is not right at home. Perhaps I shall get a letter in the morning. I shall wait, I think, until the next mail. I am very much concerned about you all. Please write often.

## Editorial Notes

The army is stripping for action — surplus baggage to the rear, overcoats sent off, marching orders read at parade. Alexander knows what this means: "We shall probably have a hard summer." The formation of the 20th Corps under Hooker replaces Slocum, whom Alexander respected. "General Hooker never cusses his men" is a careful, minimal endorsement.

The soldiers' voting controversy reveals the home front's fissures — 25 people in Champlain voted against letting soldiers vote. Alexander's rage is barely contained: "blasted with eternal infamy." The men who fight for democracy being denied its exercise by those who stay home — it's the same anger that drove his scorn for draft dodgers after Gettysburg.

The 6 P.M. postscript — "no letter... I fear all is not right at home" — captures the agony of mail dependency. The war is about to begin in earnest, and Alexander doesn't know if his family is alive.
