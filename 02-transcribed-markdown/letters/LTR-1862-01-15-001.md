## Full Transcription

> Jan. 15, 1862
> Camden Station
> Baltimore
>
> My Dear Mother:
>
> You have no idea how lonely I am. I have not heard from home since the twenty-second of Dec. I am imagining almost everything. Are you all sick or are you dead. Perhaps the Chay River has overflowed its banks and swept Champlain into oblivion. It may be that the earth has opened and swallowed it up. But I think the most probable thing is that you are completely buried up in the snow. If that is the case you (if such a thing be possible) had better send us a message and "the Sixtieth" will come and dig you out. But what is the use of my talking in this way. I feel very — very anxious about you all. I hope you do not suffer for want of money. I hope you will not scruple to use my money. Every cent is yours. I shall soon receive my pay (twenty six dollars.) You perceive by the heading of my letter that I have again changed my quarters. Corporal Myers was taken sick and I was ordered to take his place. So that I am now in the first platoon or in other words "at head quarters". We take our meals at the "Union Relief."
>
> I will tell oh! so much, if you will only write. What do you think about my getting a revolver. What do you think about my getting a revolver. There is a great demand for "Corporal of the Guard with a file of men" to take drunken soldiers to the guard or station house. These soldiers often carry knives or pistols. Perhaps my bayonet will be enough. You can see what it is in my "picture."
>
> Will you be so kind as to send me some postage stamps. I am out. I shall try and get one from the Capt. to send this letter. Perhaps the reason you do not write is that you are so busy getting Frances off that you have not time to write. My greatest fear is that you are sick. Take good care of yourself. I have no notion of coming home and not finding you there. I have got a "precious Bible" from the Clinton County Bible Society. It is a great comfort to me.
>
> Please write soon and do not forget the stamps.
>
> Your affectionate son,
>
> Corporal A. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Alexander's humor-in-desperation is on full display: imagining Champlain swept away by the Chazy River, swallowed by the earth, or buried in snow. Behind the comedy is real fear — "My greatest fear is that you are sick. Take good care of yourself. I have no notion of coming home and not finding you there." The revolver question is revealing: as Corporal of the Guard, he's responsible for subduing drunken soldiers who carry weapons. His bayonet may suffice, but he wants his mother's opinion — a teenager asking permission to buy a gun. Signing as "Corporal A. F. Hubbell" for the first time marks a threshold: Alexander is adopting his military identity. The $26 pay (vs. his first $17.33) reflects both the corporal's rate and likely back pay.
