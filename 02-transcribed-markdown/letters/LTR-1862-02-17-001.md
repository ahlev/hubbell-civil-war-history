## Full Transcription

> Feb. 17, 1862
> Baltimore
>
> My dear Mother:
>
> Last night about 12 oclock Commissary Howes came in (he rooms with me) and said he had brought a letter for me and that it was on the table. I had a great mind to get up and read it, but restrained myself. I went to sleep thinking about my letter and expected to dream about it, but did not. I generally sleep very sound, but not more than three or four hours at a time. (I mean that I usually 'wake up' every three or four hours.) Being so much on guard duty has given me the habit. After roll call (six A.M.) I lighted a candle and read my letter. It did me good. I have been watching the mails for the last week anxiously expecting one. I expect that letter will be read and reread before I get another.
>
> You speak about my going to college next fall. I have been considering the subject for some time. I will be eighteen the twenty-eighth of next month. After weighing the subject in all its bearings I have come to the conclusion that it would not be best. In the first place when I go I wish to be well fitted. Secondly. I am young yet and there is no need of my pressing in at a disadvantage. And thirdly. I think I can make enough here to clothe myself and pay my school bill and by cutting the wood, making the fires, milking the cows, taking care of the horse, making the garden, hoeing the corn and the potatoes, etc., etc., I can pay my board. I think this would be the best thing I could do, if you have no other plans. For to shut myself up in college after living in the open air so long would, I think, be worse than having a fever in the army. Perhaps I have come to a wrong conclusion. I am but a boy yet, I realize more and more each day how little I know. I try, Mother, to keep my promise 'to you' to do nothing you 'would not approve'. You speak in your letter about 'Fathers psalm'. I generally read each morning the first and nineteenth psalms. They are beautiful. I can join heartily in the Psalmists prayer, 'Let the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be acceptable in thy sight Oh Lord my strength and my redeemer'. I feel that God is near me.
>
> You do right to 'bless me' for writing such short letters. The reason was I was wearied out writing. I had had five letters to answer. One from Aunt Laurie and Cousin Nellie, another from Henry, another from Charlotte, another from Willie Savage, another from home.
>
> (Continuation of Feb. 17th, 1862.)
>
> The report just came that our brigade is going on an expedition to South Carolina. I hope it is so. General Robinson is the commander of the brigade. The 12th Mass., 10th Maine and 16th New York form part of it. It is called the 'railroad brigade'.
>
> In telling me about home matters you said nothing about the hens and heifer and if Plummer fulfilled his contract. When will it be time to pay the taxes? I wish to pay them this year. I shall not be sorry when we leave Baltimore. One of our men was poisoned a few days since and came near dying. Yes poisoned — and by a woman too. He was on picket duty. A covered carriage or wagon came along. A woman looked out and asked him if he would not like a home made cake. He suspected nothing, took and ate it. Not long after he was taken violently ill and vomited up a green substance. A doctor was sent for. He came and gave him castor oil and turpentine to make him vomit. The man frothed at the mouth. He is now nearly well.
>
> When I go out in the city I usually wear my sidearms. The Sergeants and Corporals now wear stripes on their pants. The sergeants wear a stripe an inch and a half in width and the Corporals one a half inch. The stripe is dark blue. Love to all.
>
> Your affectionate son,
>
> Corporal A. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

**ALEXANDER'S COLLEGE DECISION: STAYING IN SERVICE (Feb. 17, 1862)**

Alexander declines to attend college in fall 1862, despite his mother's apparent suggestion that he do so. His reasoning is mature and practical:
1. **Preparation**: He wants to be "well fitted" before enrolling, not enter at a disadvantage
2. **Age**: At 17 (turning 18 March 28), he sees no rush — he's young and has time
3. **Financial independence**: He believes he can earn money in the army to clothe himself, pay school bills, and farm work can cover board ("cutting the wood, making the fires, milking the cows, taking care of the horse, making the garden, hoeing the corn and the potatoes")
4. **Practicality of army life**: He argues that returning to civilian college after living in "the open air" of military life would be worse than having fever in the army

This decision is significant for family history: Alexander remained in service instead of pursuing education at this juncture. It also shows his maturity at 17, his sense of financial responsibility, and his willingness to sacrifice personal advancement for family/military duty.

**ALEXANDER'S SPIRITUAL LIFE: DAILY PSALMS**

Alexander states he "generally read each morning the first and nineteenth psalms" and quotes: "Let the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be acceptable in thy sight Oh Lord my strength and my redeemer." This is Psalm 19:14 (KJV). The letter references "Father's psalm" — a family tradition (Alexander's father was deceased by 1862). This deep spiritual practice is remarkable in a 17-year-old soldier and contrasts with the drunkenness he deplores in comrades. Alexander's faith is central to his identity.

**CORPORAL PROMOTION & NCO STRIPES (Feb. 17, 1862)**

Alexander has been promoted to Corporal. The 60th NY now issues NCO stripes: "The sergeants wear a stripe an inch and a half in width and the Corporals one a half inch. The stripe is dark blue." This specific measurement provides uniform documentation. Alexander now wears 0.5-inch dark blue stripe on his pants. He also carries sidearms when going into Baltimore. This reflects his elevated status in the regiment.

**"RAILROAD BRIGADE" EXPEDITION TO SOUTH CAROLINA**

A major report has just come: Alexander's brigade is going on an expedition to South Carolina under Gen. Robinson. The brigade consists of:
- 12th Massachusetts Infantry
- 10th Maine Infantry
- 16th New York Infantry
- 60th New York Infantry (Alexander's unit)

This is called the "railroad brigade" — likely referring to rail transport or railroad reconnaissance/protection. Alexander "hope[s] it is so" — suggesting eagerness to leave Baltimore and see action. This expedition likely relates to follow-up operations after the Burnside Expedition's success at Roanoke Island (Feb. 7–8).

**POISONING INCIDENT: ATTEMPTED ASSASSINATION (Feb. 17, 1862)**

"One of our men was poisoned a few days since and came near dying. Yes poisoned — and by a woman too." This is a remarkable and rare documented incident of attempted assassination. The soldier was on picket duty. A woman in a covered carriage asked if he wanted "home made cake." He ate it. Shortly after, he was "taken violently ill and vomited up a green substance" (likely arsenic or strychnine). A doctor administered castor oil and turpentine to force vomiting. "The man frothed at the mouth. He is now nearly well."

This incident reveals:
1. Confederate sympathizers actively attempting to poison Union soldiers
2. The vulnerability of picket duty
3. Soldier naivety (accepting food from strangers)
4. Medical treatment available in camp (prompt response saved soldier's life)
5. The hidden war behind the lines — civilians as potential combatants

Alexander mentions this matter-of-factly, possibly to underscore why he's eager to leave Baltimore (Confederate sympathizer hotbed).

**COMMISSARY HOWES: MESSMATE & ROOMMATE**

Commissary Howes is Alexander's messmate and roommate. He appears to manage mail delivery or handle correspondence. This is the only detailed mention of him; he may appear in other letters or regimental records.

**HOME FARM QUESTIONS**

Alexander asks about home farm status: "hens and heifer and if Plummer fulfilled his contract. When will it be time to pay the taxes? I wish to pay them this year." This indicates:
1. Alexander has financial responsibility for family property or taxes
2. Plummer is a farm contractor (unclear exact role)
3. Animals (hens, heifer) are part of farm operation
4. Taxes are coming due (likely spring assessment)
5. Alexander intends to send money home to pay taxes

This shows the Hubbell family's economic situation: rural farm-based, with some hired help, and the 17-year-old soldier taking responsibility for family finances while in service.

**NEW CORRESPONDENTS: CHARLOTTE & WILLIE SAVAGE**

Charlotte and Willie Savage appear here for the first time. Alexander has letters to answer from:
1. Aunt Laurie and Cousin Nellie (lazy correspondents — wait 4–6 weeks to reply)
2. Henry (brother)
3. Charlotte (relationship unclear; possibly female friend or relative)
4. Willie Savage (relationship unclear; possibly male friend)
5. Home (mother or family)

The introduction of Charlotte and Willie Savage suggests romantic interest or close friendship, but the letter doesn't clarify.

**SLEEP DEPRIVATION FROM GUARD DUTY**

Alexander notes that guard duty has given him a habit of sleeping "not more than three or four hours at a time. (I mean that I usually 'wake up' every three or four hours.)" This is significant for health: chronic sleep disruption was a serious problem for Civil War soldiers. The fragmented sleep likely contributed to illness, poor morale, and cognitive decline — all of which affected military readiness.

**MOTHER'S LETTER RECEPTION & RE-READING**

Alexander describes receiving the letter late (about midnight, brought by Commissary Howes). He was tempted to read it immediately but restrained himself. Instead, he "went to sleep thinking about my letter." After roll call at 6 A.M., he read it by candlelight. He states: "I expect that letter will be read and reread before I get another." This shows the emotional significance of home correspondence — Alexander anticipated reading and re-reading the mother's words multiple times before receiving another.

## Event: Poisoning Attempt on Picket Duty (Feb. 17, 1862)

A soldier in Co. H, 60th NY was on picket duty when a woman in a covered carriage offered him home-made cake. He accepted and ate it. Within a short time, he was taken violently ill, vomiting a green substance (likely arsenic or strychnine). A regimental surgeon was summoned and administered castor oil and turpentine to force vomiting (standard emergency treatment). The soldier frothed at the mouth but survived. By the time Alexander writes (Feb. 17), the soldier was "nearly well."

This incident represents the hidden war against Union soldiers: Baltimore-area Confederate sympathizers actively attempting to poison soldiers through poisoned food. It also shows vulnerability of picket duty and the naivety of soldiers accepting food from strangers. The availability of medical care (prompt treatment) likely saved the soldier's life.
