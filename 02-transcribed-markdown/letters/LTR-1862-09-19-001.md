## Full Transcription

> Sept. 19, 1862
> Camp on the battle field — Va.
> Near Antietam
>
> My Dear Mother —
>
> I presume you will be greatly rejoiced to hear that I came out of the battle of the third safe and sound. I cannot be thankful enough for God's great kindness to me. The only injury I received was an inconsiderable bruise a little below the right knee by a ball nearly spent. I only wish I could report the same of Henry. I saw one of the 34th this morning and he said that he is missing, also that he thought he was quite sure that he was not killed. Thinks he is either a prisoner or missed the Regiment in the confusion (something that very often happens.) I feel very anxious about him, but hope for the best. A large number of our men reported missing have come in. It is very probable he left the field to assist in carrying off some of his wounded comrades. There was as instance of this in our Company. I have strong hopes concerning him and think he will turn up alright. Don't hardly think he was hurt. Our Regiment went into the fight about 200 strong.
>
> Camp below Maryland Heights
> Sept. 21st
>
> I have not heard from Henry since writing the above. I hope and think he is yet all right. Do not worry. Our loss was between thirty and forty.
>
> Your affectionate son
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
>
> I will write and give you the particulars of the fight as soon as possible.

## Editorial Notes

The most important letter in the collection so far. Alexander writes from the Antietam battlefield itself — two days after the bloodiest single day in American history — and his first thought is Henry. The structure of the letter is a study in controlled panic: gratitude to God for his own survival, minimization of his wound ("inconsiderable bruise"), then the devastating pivot: "I only wish I could report the same of Henry." What follows is a masterpiece of hope-construction: a man from the 34th says not killed; confusion is common; missing men come in; he probably helped carry wounded. Each argument is plausible. Together they read like a man building a wall against the worst possibility. The September 21 addendum — two days later, Camp below Maryland Heights, still no word — collapses the hope into a single sentence: "Do not worry." The 60th NY went in 200 strong and lost 30-40 — a 15-20% casualty rate in a single engagement. Henry's 34th NY, in Sedgwick's catastrophic advance into the West Woods, likely fared far worse.
