## Full Transcription

> Nov. 18th, 1862
> Camp near Alexandria, Va.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your letter directed to Fonda some days since & have received three since, the last dated Nov. 10th I received on the 15th. We will receive our letters without being delayed, I think, after this. I have recd. two Observers, also for which I am much obliged. The daily newspapers are sold here from 3 to 5 cts. each.
>
> I am much pained that we hear nothing about Henry, but hope that he is a prisoner. I don't think he can be according to the surgeon's account. But we must make the best of whatever comes.
>
> I wrote Jones yesterday & sent Alek's letter to him. Hope he will succeed at West Point, but if he does not it will be nice to have him at home. I do not think
>
> [page break]
>
> I will be at home very soon as the war does not look to me as if it would close this winter. Number of the men expect to go home next spring as they think the war will close by that time. Also a great many wish they had never enlisted. Yet Charles for one. A. Douglas & I are not sorry that we came to the war. But I think we will not go to the front this winter as our regiment is not well drilled & there is plenty of guard duty to be done. Unless a lot of new troops come to take our place we will remain here. There is a resort that we are to go into the city into the barracks now building to stay this winter. I don't know whether it is true.
>
> Two regiments are now guarding the city. All citizens are allowed to come into the city, but none to go out except they have a pass. All soldiers are allowed to go in & out at it is a favorite their camp, but none to come into the city without a pass, after nine p.m. until morning none can pass either way without the countersign. I was glad that the Captains & Lieutenants have to be on guard duty also. I like to see them work. Their business is to pass the men on their post & to look after them once in a while to see that they are doing their duty. We are on 12 hours at a time outside the city. There are 2 or 3 men at a post so that the men can relieve each other. At night we carry our blankets, but it is cold to sleep on the ground & in the open air. No picket fires are allowed, but we find a fire sometimes where we can get warm. We come on duty nearly every day sometimes in the night & other times in the day.
>
> Our Regt. guards the convalescent camp, also the parole camp & furnish a funeral escort for the soldiers that die in the hospitals. So that we have no drill now as there are not men enough. No liquor is allowed to be sold in Alex., but it is done privately — good many bottles of liquor are daily taken from the soldiers in the city & sent to the provost marshals.
>
> A large number of our men are sick, some with one thing & some with another. The hospital here is in a tent. It is full & some are taken to the city. One sickly man enlisted & died not long after coming here. He bled at the lungs. His Co. sent his body home. He was from Fonda. It cost $50, for embalming & $25 for freight, making $45[sic]. His Co. raised $72, nearly every man paying $1.
>
> Two corporals in our Co. have been reduced to the ranks, that is, had their stripes taken off for absences from camp without leave. Another had his stripes cut off for threatening to shoot his Lieut & then he was tied to a telegraph pole till he could behave himself. He was an ugly fellow. Our Co. Corporals are all right for.
>
> I tent with James Lucas, Sam Pelky & a lot of Frenchmen. They all speak good English & are decent men. I was with A. Pouglias[?] some time when we had a change of men to fill up another tent. I heard that I was to go into this tent which was occupied by the worst men in the Co. But Lieut. Col. Frentress[?] made the change instead of someone else & I was put here into the best tent in the street. L. C. Frentress made quite friendly towards me. The men from Fonda do not like him. He is to strict I suppose. Col. Sabastine[?] has not been here since he came from. He has been sick, but is better. The officers boots are a short distance from ours, all in a row. Some of the men cook for them. Each tent has a corporal or sergeant in it. The tent I was in had three corporals so that it was unnecessary for some of us to move. There are a sergeant also with us. As there was no non officer in the at tent a sergeant was put in it & I got into a good place.
>
> Mrs. Ruth Hubbell & sons prosper pecuniarily & otherwise. I think we had better not buy any of their land — so we have more land now that we can manage. And besides we have not more timber enough in all on [?] land I do not think it would be good policy to strip our land of growing timber. You speak of perishable property. I do not think that timber is that kind of property. Those cedars that have been cut ought to be drawn from the first snow. There is where we missed it last winter.
>
> [page break]
>
> Battiste will draw them perhaps, if not he may know someone who will.
>
> That Plummer is a cheat. He owed us three loads at the end of last winter. They were small loads. I should threaten to sue him for trespass if he goes there again.
>
> The weather is now drily, but it has not rained much yet. Over a week since, on Nov. 7 it snowed all day, but it was all gone the next night. I heard that it was more snow that they have had for some years. I think I shall buy me a pair of boots for the mud.
>
> I think you had better dine out Thanksgiving if you are invited. You have lonesome times enough & ought to see company when you can. Frances must be full of business teaching French, etc. Fancy she likes Mr. Simons better than she did Mr. Karns. Has she got into a fight with that French teacher? I hope she will write & tell me all about her class & other things. Reed. her letter, will answer it soon. How is your health? Mine is good & has been good. My cold is better but is in a good place here to catch a new one. I hope that you will try to make yourself as comfortable as possible in every way. Two months & a half pay is now due us, but I do not know when we will be paid. Write soon & all the news.
>
> Aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell
>
> ..... 30 .....

## Editorial Notes

Charles's most comprehensive letter: a panorama of garrison life in occupied Alexandria. He catalogs guard schedules, punishment methods (telegraph pole), funeral costs ($75 to send a body home), barracks politics, property disputes, and his sister's teaching career, all in one continuous letter. The funeral detail is particularly striking — a company collection at $1 per man to embalm and ship a dead comrade. This is the business side of death that the Hubbell family may face with Henry.

The casual mention that "a great many wish they had never enlisted" is significant coming from Charles, who adds "Yet Charles for one, A. Douglas & I are not sorry." He is distinguishing himself from the demoralized majority. His confidence in his own choice will be tested as the winter progresses.
