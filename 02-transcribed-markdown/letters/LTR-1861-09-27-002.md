## Full Transcription

> Sept. 27, 1861
> Ogdensburgh, N.Y.
> Camp Wheeler
>
> My dear Mother —
>
> I write to ask your advice. I am in a xxxxxxx dilemma. The office of Adjutant in the Society of the "Brother Craftsmen" has been proffered me. What shall I do? I presume you will not be able to judge until I tell you something about it. Well, the object of the Society is this — to band together a body of men who will care each other as if they were brothers. If one is sick they will see that he is cared for. If sore pressed in battle, they will come to his assistance. If set upon by any person or persons, they will succor him, but if he picked the quarrel they will let him alone. He must suffer for it. If wounded in battle they see that he is properly cared for. If anyone is convicted of licentious conduct, he is dismissed from the Society. The headquarters of the Society are at Watertown. If I accept this office I shall be third officer in the Society. I shall receive a salary of one dollar (perhaps more) per month. Now what do you think about it? I have accepted the office with the privilege of withdrawing if it does not meet your approval.
>
> I attended the prayer meeting last night. I don't no what to do or what to think. One man there began to shout, roar, and bawl, calling on God to "come down now and bless us" until he was exhausted; all the while clapping his hands and swaying his body. He then began to draw his breath between his lips and xxxxx mutter and ejaculate. After he had somewhat recovered he began to shout "The Lord is with us", "He is here", "Glory to God", etc. All this was plentifully interspersed with Amen and ejaculations from the others. The Colonel was in the room below drilling some officers in the use of arms. This must have disturbed him. I felt xxxx ashamed that I was there. Perhaps I feel wrong. What shall I do? I wish to go to meeting, but I do not wish to see such scenes as that.
>
> [page break]
>
> Contd. Sept. 27, 1861
>
> Ask Mr. Copeland what I shall do. I tell you what it is, Mother. If I ever hated vice I hate it ten times worse now. The writing on the first page of this letter was written by the second officer of the Society. I don't like the way he talks about me. It seems to me it would be a good plan to join it as I have no friends in the Reg.
>
> I am well. Hope you all are. Have you heard from Henry? How is he? I shall write him soon.
>
> Your affectionate Son
>
> Alexander F. Hubbell
>
> NOTE:
> (The letter mentioned was written in a faded pencilled scrawl which was not legible.)

## Editorial Notes

This is the most emotionally revealing letter in the collection so far. Alexander, barely a week into military life, is wrestling with genuine moral dilemmas — the Brother Craftsmen society (join for protection since he has "no friends," but he distrusts the second officer), the prayer meetings (wants spiritual community but is disturbed by revivalist excess), and the pervasive profanity of camp life. His repeated appeals to Mother's judgment — "What shall I do?" appears three times — and his request to consult Mr. Copeland show a young man of deep religious conviction feeling morally unmoored.

The contrast with Henry is stark: Henry, after months in camp, has pragmatic advice ("However" — make the best of it). Alexander is still reeling from the moral shock of army life. His admission of having "no friends in the Reg." is poignant and explains his interest in the mutual aid society despite his reservations.

The transcriber's note about the enclosed faded pencilled letter is a reminder of the provenance chain — even the 1947-1949 transcription couldn't recover everything from the water-damaged originals.
