## Full Transcription

> April 13, 1863
> Atlas
>
> Dear Cousin Charles:
>
> We, or rather Father, received a letter from you a few days ago and were glad to hear that you were enjoying good health, and that you like a soldier's life so well. I think you have been fortunate so far to escape hard service, and hope you will not see anything hard, but if you do, I hope you will pass through it unhurt and with due honor. I suppose there is no knowing when the war will end, but hope it will soon. Uncle Charles thinks the opposing parties will enter into a truce before long for a short time and let the soldiers go home, but I don't hardly think it is possible. There is considerable feeling manifested here in consequence of the approaching draft. There are many that say they would sooner fight for the Union than to free the negroes.
>
> The weather here is quite springlike now. The nights are frosty and the days are warm and sunny. It is quite sickly here now owing, I suppose, to the fact of the open winter and changeable weather. I never knew the bill of mortality to be as large as it has been during the last few weeks. There have been two cases within three weeks where two children have been buried in one coffin. It is, indeed, a solemn time. I understand there was a wedding in the neighborhood last week which was quite an agreeable divertisement.
>
> Is your mother well now? I have not heard from her for a long time. I hope you and Alexander will not expose yourselves recklessly to danger on her account.
>
> I hope this will find you "sharp set" for dinner, as I am sure I am and Mother is afraid I'll starve if we don't have dinner pretty quick. I must come to a conclusion as I have an opportunity to send this away. Please write often.
>
> From your aff. cousin
>
> Mary J. McNeil
>
> This is Cousin Mary's first to me and two or three days since I answered it. You must not let her know that I sent her letter to you.
>
> I don't know as the citizens will like it to have the government stockade its stores and have nearly the whole city out of it. But then the city is well fortified. There are a good many forts around it; also a trench with cannon around the city. I think the rebels will never get in, but suppose the gov't. wants to be sure that its stores will not be destroyed. The President was here a few days since. I heard cannons booming and was told that it was a salute to Lincoln. Heard today that the rebels captured a train of sixteen cars from us near Garrettstown and that our Lieut. ran, but that a sergeant staid with some men till they were driven back. If the Lieut. had not run we could have whipped the rebels. Our regiment has been called up a good many nights to be ready for the rebel cavalry and now it goes regularly on picket and it also does the patrol duty so that we have an easy time.
>
> The chaplain has been here twice, and will continue to come if we want him, to talk about an hour each Sunday after inspection to us. There is time to go to church after he is through.
>
> [page break]
>
> I hope you have had a pleasant visit with Henry Smith and I think it would be a good plan for you and Frances to make a visit to Charlotte. That is the news from our big toad! Somebody at Champlain is always in too big a hurry to make bonfires. Vicksburg is not taken yet.
>
> Your aff. son
>
> C. F. Hubbell
>
> P.S. I have written to Uncle John once and that is enough as we don't agree in sentiment. I will answer Frances' letter soon.
