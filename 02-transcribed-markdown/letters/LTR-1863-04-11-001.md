## Full Transcription

> April 11, 1863
> Alexandria
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your and Frances' letter of Mch. 29th in due time. Glad to hear that you both are well. My health is very good.
>
> Yesterday all the soldiers were mustered that are in the U.S. service. The regiments are to be filled up by a draft. I think Uncle Sam will draft so as to have men enough to soon end this war. I see that we are about attacking Charleston, but I think it will be long before we take it. It is a strong place. Our reg't. has just received new guns. They are long Enfield rifles. These guns have the iron maw all well blacked which prevents their rusting. Our old guns we scoured the black off which made them look better as the barrels were bright, but a great deal more work to keep them clean. I hope that we will not be ordered to brighten our new guns.
>
> The man from our Co. that went home to Plattsburgh on a furlough from our Co. has not come back; goes to Canada, we all think. Another Plattsburgh man has now got a furlough from our Co.
>
> We commenced yesterday to fire at a target. Our reg't. at battalion drill fires blank cartridges. It is afraid our Co. will get behind the reg't. in drilling, but we will have Co. drill soon, I think. Ten men and two sergeants go every afternoon to the convalescent camp for stragglers. They bring them to the soldier's rest where they stay till morning, then take the mail steamer to the creek. Then ten fresh men and a sergeant go with them to the creek. Our men bring two or three hundred stragglers a day from the distributing camp which is close to the convalescent camp. We corporals have an easy time as we don't have to go to the creek or to the distributing camp. We are on duty about three days, but the duty is light. A few days ago I received three Tribunes and the Observer.
>
> I have just seen Freeman Bowron. He came from Washington where his Co. is to see us Champlain boys. Looks very well and says when his three years are out he will reenlist in the regular army. He is orderly sergeant in the cavalry. Says his captain dated his commission as orderly back two months before he got it. If Henry had gone to the hospital when he was sick, he might have escaped some of the battles he was in. I am afraid he was killed.
>
> I have not heard from Alex for some time. Think Hooker's army will soon march. The weather today is too warm for comfort, but the nights are cold. Need all our blankets at night.
>
> I hear that hay is $20.00 a ton. Your arrangement about the horse and cow was a very good one. Have you had any sugar yet? Seems to me there are not many maples on the strip of land Mr. Rye claims. If the land is not his, he ought not to have it. Mr. Crea claims a corner; he is a hard man, very close.
>
> What does Mr. Copeland think of ministers being drafted? I don't agree with Alex about Silas and Sid. Think they would be very foolish to enlist. I have not got sick of soldiering yet; will not, I think, till the hard times come. Hope our Co. will stay here this summer. Please write all the news. Did you see Charles Dodds? He says he would not stay home for anything. It is too lonesome.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Charles says "I am afraid he was killed" for the third time across three letters (March 19, March 29, April 11). It's no longer a revelation — it's a refrain. But the new detail matters: Henry was sick before the battle and could have gone to the hospital. He didn't. He fought. The parallel with Alexander's March 29 refusal to seek safety is stark — the Hubbell brothers share a code of honor that may have killed one and will endanger another.
