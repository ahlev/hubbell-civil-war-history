## Full Transcription

> Nov. 28th, 1870
> Champlain, N.Y.
>
> My dear boys —
>
> You have never acknowledged the thirty dollar draft which I sent you about the middle of October. I fear you have never read it. Let me know soon if you have or not.
>
> We spent Thanksgiving at your Uncle Williams — with your Uncle James and Henry with their families and other company. Had a grand New England dinner, such as could not be got up anywhere except in New England. Had a glorious time — enjoyed it much. The only drawback was that your Uncle W. had to send us at ten P.M., a dark, rainy, muddy night, two miles from his house. I never rode in such fear. It was pitchy dark — it could be felt. We were obliged to take the evening train, as it was the last day of school.
>
> Friday morning your aunt Louise took us down to your Uncle Henrys where we staid until Saturday morning. They invited us up in the evening. The Mendes (?) were there. We had a delightful time. Sat. morning, we took the cars for home. I stopt over a train at Burlington to visit aunt Meade. Her son, Sam. Hewitt died recently. She is almost heart broken. She has paid one hundred dollars to have high Mass said for the repose of his soul, then he died he had a couple of diamond studs in his shirt bosom. They were taken out by some men, and very common ones put in their place. Mrs. Hennings lady who is staying with her, is suspected. I think the priest or nun who were there would be as likely to take them. Now that she has no child, it is thought that the priest will get her property, which is large. They are already talking about taking her place for a school. The large and elegant solid silver branched candelabras, which were my aunts, are in the Catholic church. Mrs. Edgar says eight thousand dollars — as the personal property was given to her. Edgar says eight thousand dollars. Her home is valued at about ten thousand, which is here also. Her nieces have come over trying to be to make heirs, but there is no hope for them. The Jesuits have her — she is not allowed to see any one alone. Not one moment during the day did Mrs. Edgar see Aunt M. alone. I leave as Aunt M. tried her best to get her away for a few moments. The poor old lady is eighty-six years of age.
>
> Your Uncle William built a cheese factory last summer, and he says it is better than bonds. There are ten stock holders. The factory cost five thousand dollars and they say it will pay for itself in five years. They intend to milk 25 cows next summer. I have time I will tell you all about this concern. I hope you will have one near you. It is such an easy way to dispose of milk. The cheeses make their weight eighty pounds and sold for fourteen cts. They are the best in market. Some factories last three or four hundred pounds from not having the right kind of persons to run them. They are getting rich there with their fruit and cheese and milk. The factory is closed for the present and they are now making butter which is selling for forty cents. We have been paying that for sometime.
>
> I have been mending windows today — have set seven lights — mostly upstairs. We are about ready for winter. By the by — I should say you will freeze to death if you do not have another thickness on your house. I should think so — that it ought to be doubled. You must bank up well. He says you can get refuse brick at Ft. ——— for nothing to line your house and make it comfortable. I would do so. There is nothing like having a warm house in our climate. I brought Websters master pieces home with me, & if it does not cost more than the book is worth, will send it to you.
>
> Your affectionate,
>
> Mother
