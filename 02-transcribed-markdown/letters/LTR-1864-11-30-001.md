## Full Transcription

> Nov. 30, 1864
> Camp near Newtown, Va.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your welcome letter of Nov. 22nd today containing one dollar & a silk Hdkf. also fine comb. Am sorry that the people of Champlain are in such trouble about the rebs in Canada. Think that the alarm is needless, and you will not be disturbed.
>
> Seven months pay is now due us and we are daily expecting to be paid, but may not be, and that the dollar you sent me will be all I will need, I think. Do you know how much the Hdkf. Alex sent me cost. It is almost too nice for a soldier. I was very much surprised to hear of Alex being home. He is not of age a soldier and therefore not entitled to vote. But am glad that he got the furlough. He was very lucky. I wish he could get into the Invalid Corps. I think that is the best place for him. Am glad to hear from you that James is doing well. I have not heard from him directly, but the Sergt. in our Co. that went to hospital with James, received one from him some time since.
>
> The eighth of Nov, election day, we were called up very early and cooked our breakfast, we then packed up to move and stood to arms till daylight, but did not move and lay under arms all day. Next morning were called up the same way, but did not march till ten oclock, when we reached here after a few hours march. We are in good winter quarters, but some distance from wood & water. Our cabins are quite large & have a stone fireplace & chimney. Have been very busy which is the reason that I have not wrote.
>
> Dec. 9th: Received six months pay last night, one hundred & eight dollars. We signed the pay rolls some days since and I have been waiting for pay before I sent this letter.
>
> Dec. 10th: Last night about six inches of snow fell. They say this morning that our Regt. is going to make about ten miles from here to the other side of Winchester to guard a railroad. He will probably move tomorrow or next day. It is too hard sitting for Thanksgiving we drew turkey for dinner, but the ration for the three in our tent was so small that we annexed it to off for four big hard tacks. Hard tack better. I do not draw enough hard tack. I have bought it at the commissary. There is plenty to do, roll calls, reviews, guard duty, backing wood from the woods, etc. For two Sundays we have had Brigade review; next Sunday, tomorrow, we may be marching, that is our Regt.
>
> Frances wants to know how I look. Well our men say that I never was fatter. I wear mustache & chin whiskers, look quite savage, shave the side of my face every week or two — could oftener but no razor. I do not think I have straightened any since I left home, but am as round shouldered as ever. Was much pleased yesterday to receive a letter from Frances and you, also two Tribunes. You ask if I want a vest — not so, but please send me a pair of No. 8 Rip Skin Boots, one small coarse towel, one small colored Hdkf. Ed better be sent by express, I guess. I will send some more money in a few days. Enclosed is fifty dollars.
>
> From your Aff. Son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Charles's self-portrait: "I wear mustache & chin whiskers, look quite savage, shave the side of my face every week or two — could oftener but no razor." Round-shouldered, fatter than ever, two teeth lost in the valley. The middle brother draws himself with a practical pencil — no romance, just a man who looks older than his years.

The Thanksgiving turkey traded for hard tack is peak army economics. The ration was "so small" for three men that they exchanged it for four hard tacks. Hard tack was more sustaining. Charles buys extra at the commissary — he knows what he needs.

Alexander is home on furlough — the first time any brother has been home since enlistment. Charles wishes he could stay — "I wish he could get into the Invalid Corps." The war has damaged Alexander beyond field service, and Charles knows it.
