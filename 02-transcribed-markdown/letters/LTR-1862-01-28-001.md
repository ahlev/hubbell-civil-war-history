## Full Transcription

> Jan. 28, 1862,
> Baltimore
>
> My Dear Brother:
>
> You seem to be the next man in the "docket" to write to. I have "come to the conclusion" that I had better try. Mothers plan to write a little every day. In that way I shall have a letter done "before I know it".
>
> I came off guard duty this morning. When I went on yesterday morning I was what some people would say, down sick with a cold. I feel much better this morning, and it is "my experience" that the best way is, to do your duty and whole duty, and you will feel a great deal better both in mind and body. At least I do.
>
> I think our Regt. will leave here soon. I hope it will. Where we will go to, I do not know. All sorts of rumors are afloat. I presume you must have heard of the resignation of our Colonel (Hayward) long before this. Well may we be styled the "Orphan Regiment". But then I think we are quite as well off and a little better than when we had a Colonel. It is said that we have another Colonel (Green). I hope he will show himself a smart and brave man.
>
> It is raining quite hard today. We have had no very very cold weather this winter, and very little snow. Just enough to make the roads muddy.
>
> Feb. 4: I came off duty again this morning. I am now on every third day. We had quite a heavy fall of snow yesterday from the north-east. It made it very uncomfortable for me with my shoes, as every time I went out with the relief I filled them with snow and consequently went with wet feet. However, I feel quite well this morning. I like the snow and cold weather. It reminds me of home. I don't believe I could get along very well without frost and snow. The "South" is no home for me. Give me the North with its pleasant summers and fall and old winter.
>
> I received two letters and a paper day before yesterday. One letter and paper from home and the other (letter) from Aunt Laurie and Cousin Nellie. You can imagine the pleasure they gave me. (I just received a letter from Willis Savage. I wrote him some time since. I now have five letters to answer.)
>
> You ask me "what is the health of our Company". There was a time when there was quite a number on the sick list although it was not quite so bad as Mrs. Upton represents it. There has not been one case of the black measles in the Co. and I do not think in the Regt. though there has been quite a number of the red measles. The measles, I think, have had their run. Quite a number have died out of the Regt., but none from our Company. The reason I think is that we gave them better care. The hospital is no place to send sick men. They are not properly taken care of. One of our men (sick with the measles) was sent there. At one time he was so low that he was rolled over twice to ascertain whether he was dead or alive. You must not believe all Mrs. Upton or any other person tells you. My health is good. Luther gets along pretty well. He bought him a six shooter and sold it again with three dollars profit.
>
> The boys say I am getting fat. Trudell is in the hospital. Lablue is getting along very well. We are doing hard service. I think I shall have a chance to smell powder before the war is over. I wish I was at home to enjoy the singing school, but then I am content here. How do you like Mr. Partridge?
>
> I return you many thanks for your kindness in sending me the stamps. I feel anxious, very anxious, about James. I hope and think he will pass his examination. That examination will be very important in his life.
>
> Our quarters are excellent. The entire Regt. is now in barracks. So it is decreed, is it, that the calf shall die. I am sorry that I am not there to raise it. I should indeed like to get a taste of the veal, but then we have plenty of fresh beef and I must console myself on that.
>
> (Contd.)
>
> Contd. Jan. 28, 1862
>
> Drunkenness did prevail to a great extent, but it has been greatly checked since the Capt. has prevailed upon most of the Company to sign a pledge of total abstinence during their term of enlistment. You ask me how the men and I agree. Well, we agree pretty well, very well. The men generally obey orders very well. Trudell and I had a few words once. I was taking out the nine o'clock relief and having one man less than the required quota I was obliged to lengthen the beats. Trudell declared he would not walk the beat. I gave him and began to be quite noisy as Frenchmen usually do. I told him he would do as I thought best and that it would be good policy to have no more words about it. No, he would not walk it. No man could make him. Said I, "You can take your choice either to walk that beat or I will get a file of men and bring you to the guard house a prisoner." It was amusing to see how quick he "wopsid" around. He didn't care a --- how far he walked. --- I was the best corporal in the lot, etc. If I had reported him he would have been in a bad fix.
>
> Give my love to Mother and James and Frances. Give my respects to Mr. Holcomb and the rest.
>
> Your affectionate Brother
>
> Alexander

## Editorial Notes

Alexander's confrontation with Trudell is the first direct account of him exercising authority as corporal — the Frenchman who "declared he would not walk the beat" forced to comply by threat of arrest. Alexander's telling is both proud and generous: he didn't report Trudell, who "would have been in a bad fix." The measles epidemic context — men dying, hospital so negligent a patient was "rolled over twice to ascertain whether he was dead or alive" — shows Co. H's relative health as a point of unit pride. The "Orphan Regiment" nickname after Hayward's resignation captures a real morale problem: the 60th NY was losing its command structure. Luther's six-shooter arbitrage ($3 profit) is a small but vivid detail of the camp economy.
