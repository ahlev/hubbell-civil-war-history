## Full Transcription

> Oct. 15, 1862
> Camp Mohawk
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> We left for this place Monday morning at seven A.M. Arrived here at about 2 A.M. the next morning. On our arrival a large number of men were routed out of their bunks & tents for us to sleep in as there is not sleeping room for the whole of us. A large number of men sent home to sleep last night. The guard have bonfires to warm themselves.
>
> We have 72 tents which will hold 5 or 6 men each & a wood barracks. The barracks is a very rough affair. The bunks will hold four & are covered with straw in which the men lay like so many pigs. The tents have no floors but are covered thick with straw. I slept the first time in a tent last night — slept comfortable. We fare well here — have as much fresh beef three times a day as we wish. The meat is boiled. The men are very fond of onions which they peel themselves & eat raw. The camp is very lively. Large numbers of ladies come here to see friends & sight seeing. We have about 900 men here. We are only a short distance from the village of Fonda which is quite a large place. It is in the country town, I think.
>
> There is some talk of part of us giving up our tents to the men here & we sleep in the hotel at the village. We will know tonight. We have received our new blankets which are large & warm of course, a little heavy. The men are having blankets, boots, socks, shirts, who marked or perused being lost or stolen. There is a good deal of stealing here. There are a good many Dutchman or Germans in this regiment. The men that we found here received a heavy bounty. The towns paying $100 & some a good deal more besides giving a month. Please write me what they are paying in Vermont if you know. Essex pays $100.
>
> On our journey here we had hard crackers & cheese which went well enough, but we could get no water at supper time & could not get out of the cars to get any as the doors were guarded. But finally they gave us some extra. We saw plenty of handkerchiefs waving on the route, to took the ears for Fonda at Whitehall. Passed through Saratoga, Schenectady, etc., but did not see much as it was dark.
>
> I sent a $25 check home by Mr. Marshall. I wish you would show consent the money he gives you & find out if it is good. We will soon have our pay which I wish you would put into Mr. Savages hands at interest. "Will you pay off Mr. Savage, J & R.L. Savage s/o is $16.55. Joel Savage s/o is $27.69. You had
>
> [page break]
>
> better show Mr. Savage the s/o he rendered some time since as it will save him trouble. I sent home by Murray 1 towel, 1 hdkfs. & 1 testament with small book to be left at Savages as I could not carry them.
>
> There is trouble here about bounty again. At Plattsburgh the men said they would not leave till they had all their bounty. But Sunday night in the eating room at supper limit, Col. Armstrong made a speech asking the men if they would go. The reply was a great hurrah to go. The majority were for going so after eating yank'd butter & looking at half raw cork & eating a little bread we left at 7 A.M. the next morning. Our officers say that we shall not leave here till we have all our pay. I believe I am appointed Corporal but none of the officers are named to the men yet, not even the captains. I mean us Plattsburgh fellows.
>
> I wish you would write me. If you write soon, I think we will be here. Have you heard from Henry yet? How do you & Frances get along? I hope you are prospering finely. Please direct your letter to Camp Fonda, Fonda, Montgomery Co. N.Y. It turns out that the men sleet in a barn in the village instead of the hotel. The men are buying revolvers here. I don't know as they will be allowed to carry them. I shall not buy one. I have lost my razor for which I am sorry, but no use crying about spilt milk. Suppose I let my whiskers grow till I come back. I think I will.
>
> Your aff. Son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell
>
> Will you please send me a list of my bad spelling.
>

## Editorial Notes

Charles's longest and most detailed letter yet — a travelogue of mobilization. The journey from Plattsburgh to Fonda takes him through the heart of upstate New York by rail, and he catalogs everything: the tents, the food, the theft, the bounty disputes, the lost razor, the growing whiskers. His voice is increasingly confident — he's becoming a soldier, managing money, settling debts, forming opinions about his fellow recruits ("a good many Dutchman or Germans"). The postscript about spelling is pure Charles: earnest, practical, self-improving, and entirely unselfconscious about asking his mother to edit his letters while he heads to war.
