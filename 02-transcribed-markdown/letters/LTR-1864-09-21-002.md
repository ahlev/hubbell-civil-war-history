## Full Transcription

> September 21, 1864
> U. S. Hospital No. 2
> Chattanooga, Tenn.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> Have you taken a realizing sense that I have now been in the U. S. Service as a soldier three years — three long — long — weary years! Yes — three years ago today I took an oath to serve the United States of America faithfully as a soldier for the term of "three years or the war," and I can conscientiously say that I have with God's help done my best to bear that oath and do my duty as a soldier should do. And now, dear Mother, I want you to join me, (and I know you will) in thanking God for his kind and watchful care over me in preserving me through so many dangers and temptations to which I have been exposed in the past terrible three years of war, and to ask a continuance of his kind care over me during the remainder of my term of service.
>
> When I think of all the things which I have passed my heart is too full to speak. This is the first time since I have been in the service that I have been in a general hospital sick. You know I was in one last fall but then I was wounded and was only a short time from the regt.
>
> Well, I wrote you, only a day or two since, and have nothing of special interest to communicate. I am getting along very well, I think. There is one very singular thing about it — as soon as it is night comes I am as blind as a bat. However it may wear off in time. There are a number here troubled in the same way. Direct your letters to the regt. Give my regards to Mrs. B. Love to all,
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell.
>
> P.S. The morning papers give an acct. of a heavy fight in the Shenandoah Valley. I am very anxious to hear from the boys.

## Editorial Notes

September 21, 1864 — three years to the day since Alexander took his oath. "Three long — long — weary years!" The dashes are Alexander's breathing. "My heart is too full to speak." This is the closest Alexander comes to breaking down on paper.

The night blindness is grimly specific — a symptom of severe vitamin A deficiency, which accompanies scurvy. "As blind as a bat" every night, with "a number here troubled in the same way." The Atlanta Campaign didn't just exhaust these men — it blinded them.

The postscript is its own drama: "The morning papers give an acct. of a heavy fight in the Shenandoah Valley." Alexander reads about Third Winchester in a Chattanooga hospital. His brothers were in it. He doesn't know if they're alive. On the same day, Charles writes from Strasburg to say James came through unhurt. The letters will cross in the mail.
