## Full Transcription

> July 2nd, 1864
> In the Field near
> Marietta, Ga.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> Must try and write you a word at least though I am pretty tired as I was up all night working with pick and shovel in the trenches. Our batteries along the whole line opened up on the rebs this morning but everything has settled down again to its usual opening — i.e. — sharp skirmishing and a shot now and then from the batteries. Movements are now being made that will I think, bring matters to a decided issue soon. I hope we shall be able to bring this campaign to a close soon.
>
> Luther was wounded this morning while we were after a log for the top of the breast works. We had just gone up with several men to an old house from which we intended to get the log. When the Capt. (I was at work with the shovels) ordered me with several others to go on and give them a lift in bringing it down, I had just started when a ball whistled close by and was in succession followed by a yell of agony. I ran forward and had gone but a little ways when I met Luther coming back leaning upon one of the men. You may be sure I felt a little queer. To my anxious inquiries Luther replied that he thought he was not much hurt — that he thought that it was only a flesh wound. And so it proved though it was a pretty bad hurt, but not dangerous. The ball tore a furrow in his right side and passed through the fleshy part of his right arm a little above the elbow. He will probably be laid up two or three months. God in his kindness great kindness has as yet preserved me from all harm. Culver Bostwick is also well. (He have just drawn a ration of whiskey. I gave mine to the boys for two hard crackers. I believe Bostwick did the same. We do not draw whiskey every day — only when we have had an unusually hard time. We were up last night, and the night before and — well, I may say we hardly know what an uninterrupted night's rest is. He will know how to appreciate rest if ever we get it. Culver and I are getting worn down bully thin. We suffer terribly from the heat — and — and terrain — if I must say it.
>
> Of army movements I will not attempt to tell you. You can find out pretty much from the papers. James has not yet written me. I hope he has not forgotten his brother Alex. How do all things flourish in Champlain! and what were to be the doings on the Fourth? There is a likelihood of there being considerable powder burned here, though if burning powder makes Fourth of July we have had fourth of July ever since we started. Love to all. Tell Frances I shall wait until there is little firing before I write her, which I hope will be soon.
>
> Your aff. Son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
>
> Received a letter from Capt. Ransom last night. Will endeavor to write him soon. Please give him my regards. I received the handkerchief some time since, also all the stamps you sent. I get all your letters in the course of time. I am glad that Frances has gone to Charlotte. I hope she will have a pleasant visit. She needed rest. Will she go into school again next term?

## Editorial Notes

Luther's wounding is narrated in real time — Alexander heading toward the old house with shovels, the ball whistling, the yell of agony, Luther coming back leaning on a man. "You may be sure I felt a little queer." The understatement of a man who has been close to death many times but still feels it when it's his friend.

"Culver and I are getting worn down bully thin. We suffer terribly from the heat — and — and terrain — if I must say it." The dashes and the reluctant confession — the campaign is destroying them physically, and Alexander can barely bring himself to say it. Trading whiskey for hard crackers tells the story: hunger trumps comfort.

"If burning powder makes Fourth of July we have had fourth of July ever since we started." The grim humor of a man who has been under fire for two months straight.
