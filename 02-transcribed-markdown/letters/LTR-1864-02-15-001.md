## Full Transcription

> Feb. 15, 1864
> Capitol Hill
> Washington, D.C.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I rec'd. Alex's letter of Feb. 9th enclosing one dollar two or three days since. Have been on guard or would have answered it sooner. Since the 14th N.H. regt. left for the front two or three weeks since our regt. have been doing their duty and our own, too. Instead of having three reliefs which makes each relief two hours on post and then four hours off, we now have four reliefs, which makes 2 hours on post and 6 hours off. Then we had three reliefs the guard was changed once every twenty-four hours, but now with four reliefs the guard is not changed, but stays on duty all the time. There are not enough men to change the guard. Of course as all the men are on duty now we have no dress parades. The corporals have a good deal more leisure time than the privates as we do not have to be on duty all the time. I have just come off duty. "As on four days am" was relieved by Jim Lucas who has been promoted again to Corporal.
>
> Over a week since I was down to Point Lookout which is at the mouth of the Potomac. About one hundred of us went there with nearly 400 rebel prisoners. The camp of prisoners there contains about 8000 rebs. It is surrounded by a board fence about 8 feet high, with a walk near the top of the fence for the sentinels to walk their beat. Our Regt. now guard the old Capitol prison and the Carroll Prison which are both close to our barracks. Also guard the railroad depot and the Central Guard House. I don't know how long we will stay here. We may be relieved by the Invalid Corps. I hear that the old Capitol Prison used to be the Capitol of the U.S.
>
> Sergt. Peltier of our Co. went home on furlough two days since. He lives at Kerbo.
>
> How much bounty did Alex get? If Alex does not receive the town bounty it will be cheating him. Sam Underhill was not entitled to it and Alex ought not to have consented, if he did, to divide with Sam. But since the President's new call for troops the quota of the town may not be full, and they may all receive the town bounty. Can you tell me about this?
>
> I don't know whether Alex made a good investment with his money or not. At all events I had rather buy county bonds if they pay 7 percent with my money than to invest it in that bank. My money that is out at 7% is well enough where it is.
>
> Have you paid the land tax yet? If so, how much was it? I rec'd Frances' letter some time since. Tell her I am expecting that long letter that she promised and hope that she won't forget the promise to write. Also that I shall be glad to pay my share towards those furs that she bought for you. They are just the things for our Canada climate and I am glad that she bought them.
>
> I saw Chas. Carte the other day. He was here to see us Champlainers. He said he attended the reenlisted soldiers party at Biglows. Asked me if I knew who he took there. Told him I did not, but guessed Helen Scott. Do you know who she is and who did take her? Have you heard from James lately? It seems too bad for him to go into the army. He might go to school a while and fit himself for a teacher or a month or so. I think as much of James' abilities as ever. A great many smart men have failed at West Point. If James enlisted I hope he will do it where he can get the most bounty. He can go into any regt. he enlists for old enough to be this regt. or Alex's regt. But I hope he will not enlist. Please write soon and all the news. How does Sid get along?
>
> From your aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Charles's description of Point Lookout is the view from the guard's perspective — the board fence, the sentinel walk, the 8,000 prisoners below. Point Lookout would become one of the war's most notorious camps, with high mortality rates. Charles visits it as a work detail, escorting prisoners, and reports it with his usual matter-of-fact precision.

The note that the Old Capitol Prison "used to be the Capitol of the U.S." is historically accurate — it served as the temporary Capitol after the British burned Washington in 1814.

Jim Lucas's promotion back to Corporal after his arrest for ration theft completes a remarkable arc of military redemption — reduced, then restored. The army's personnel system was more forgiving than its rhetoric suggested.
