## Full Transcription

> July 28th, 1862 - Monday
> Camp, near Harisons Landing
> James River, Va.
>
> Dear Francis —
>
> I received your letter not long since and was very glad to hear from you. I am rejoiced to know that you have decided to remain at home this summer, or for the rest of the summer, and perhaps the fall months. You no doubt will have a large French class. I should like it very well if peace should be proclaimed just before Sugar time, or in the fore part of next winter, so that I could make arrangements for farming next spring, but there is no such luck for me, as matters and things look now, I am bound to stay my time out. I shall make the best of it.
>
> You have such a good knowledge of French now I have no doubt you could command a good salary almost anywhere.
>
> The weather here is very warm, sultry evenings and nights are the pleasantest part here. Last Tuesday there was a grand review of the Army of the Potomac near the James River within sight of the gunboats. We were marched around in line of battle the same as if we were in battle, only we were not. The battery was also with us and fired blank cartridges. I have no doubt we were sketched by an Artist, as there was a good opportunity to do so. You may perhaps see the sketch in the Harper's Weekly. Papers here are ten cts, such as the New-York Herald and others. The Sutler charges a great deal for his goods, or is very extortionate, but I suppose he has a right, as it is some trouble for him to bring his goods from the river. He makes more than our merchant at home, almost doubles on his goods, but after all a person must indulge a little now and then. When I was sick I do not know what I should have done if it had not been for the Sutler. Reich is head man there yet.
>
> Well here I am on the last page, and I must bring my letter to a close. By the by, how does Sid come on in farming since he is married and works the farm himself. I fancy he does not work much. For my part, I do not intend to run into the fire before I can see my way through — that is, get married. I do not mean to insinuate that Sid will not do well, but he must look out and not hire to much; but I must bring my letter to a close. Please excuse all mistakes as I am oblige to write just as I can. I expect Lieut. Scott must be on his way by this time. James can direct his letters the same as before.
>
> I remain your aff. Brother
>
> Henry
>
> Give my regards to all friends. Burn this scribble — hope to do better next time.

## Editorial Notes

Henry writes to Frances with an ease he doesn't show in letters to Mother — he talks about marriage, teases about Sid's farming, and admits his peace fantasy: home "just before Sugar time." The grand review is a set piece: the whole Army of the Potomac marching in line of battle near the James River, batteries firing blank cartridges, an artist sketching for Harper's Weekly. Henry notices all of it but is more interested in Sutler prices. "Burn this scribble" at the end is devastating given the collection exists because the family ignored this request. Henry's "I shall make the best of it" uses almost identical words to Alexander's "I make the best of everything" from the day before (July 27), but where Alexander sounds Stoic and proud, Henry sounds like a man who's given up on alternatives.
