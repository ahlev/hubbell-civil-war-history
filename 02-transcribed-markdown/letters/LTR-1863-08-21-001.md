## Full Transcription

> August 21, 1863
> Capitol Hill
> Washington, D.C.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I was very glad to receive a letter from you and James and Frances yesterday. Have been expecting a letter from you for some time. Am glad that you and Frances are both well. My health is good.
>
> I like living in barracks better than in tents as we have plenty of room and can keep our clothes and guns cleaner, and they are warmer in winter, I guess, and I know they're cooler in summer. There are plenty of windows and now the window sashes are taken out to let in the air. Tents, unless they are raised up to let in the air, are hot as an oven in summer. But there is one objection to our barracks, which is bed bugs. A good many of the men say they can't sleep in the barracks on account of them and they sleep on the ground close to the barracks. But I am lucky to have a bunk where there are none, at least I have not felt any as yet or anyway. If there were bed bugs, I believe I should sleep in the bunk as I don't think it would be healthy for me to sleep on the ground exposed to the heavy dew.
>
> We have plenty of guard duty to do. Our reg't. guards two prisons, the R.R. depot, and does other duty besides. The draft has taken place and every day about fifty of our men go to the City Hall where the drafted men or their substitutes are examined, to keep order and take the men passed by the board to the barracks where they are kept. We on get passes to go into the city when we are not on duty. I have had one pass and have been to the Capitol, also to the patent office. Have not been to church yet as I have been on duty every Sunday but one. The Chaplain talks to those that want to hear every Sunday night. Benches are placed in the yard and the Chaplain, with his hearers around him and caps all off, proceeds to business.
>
> I think it would be a good plan to break up keeping house this winter and you go to Charlotte. You could pay them something for your board. And the woods could be left in care of some good person. Frances would like to leave home, I suppose, and leave Montcalm. It might be more pleasant for you both to break up.
>
> About the cow you sold Mr. Boyle, I would not be small with him as he has been a good friend of ours. I do not know what cows are worth, but I should think $40 a great price for her. I am glad you are going to settle up with Mr. Savage. Last fall Savage rendered their acct. to you; it was $16.55. Joel Savage's separate acct. was then $37.49, but was not rendered to you. Then there will be interest on these debts since last fall. The Surrogate wrote you last summer that there was nine or ten dollars in his office due you. I forget the amount. I guess it was sent to my Flave or some one else. But they never got it. It would be well to get this.
>
> I think it very strange that Sid and wife and the rest of the family can't live in that big house. Do you think Sid will get rich farming? I guess all that Mr. Clark told you some time ago about our good luck that was coming won't come true. Guess if we get any more land in Champlain, it will be by paying for it. What do you think of the war? Do you think it will be a thirty year's war now? I guess Jeff is about played out, but there will be a good deal of guerilla fighting yet.
>
> I am much obliged to you and Frances for your long letters. Hope you both will write often. I will write Alex today. I remain
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

The bed bug passage is pure soldier correspondence — the hierarchy of discomforts that fills letters when no battles are being fought. Charles prefers bed bugs in a bunk to sleeping on dewy ground, a calculation that captures garrison life's small negotiations with comfort.

The suggestion to "break up keeping house" is significant. The Hubbell household in Champlain is hollowing out: Henry dead, Alexander and Charles at war, James at West Point, Frances wanting to leave. Mother alone in the "stone castle" — the big house that even Sid's family can't fully occupy. Charles is gently proposing the end of the family home as a functioning household.
