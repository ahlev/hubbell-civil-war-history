## Full Transcription

> Dec. 29, 1861
> Baltimore
>
> My dear Brother:
>
> You tell Frances that I shall not write her again until she writes me. That I am really provoked with her. I have received two letters from home within a few days and not a word or message did she write or send. You are nearly — yes — just as bad. Now do you both "mend your ways". I shall have you both "hauled up" before a "Court of Inquiry" if you dont look out.
>
> I began a "Synopsis of a day in Camp" in a letter to James. I shall now try and finish it in this. I believe I left off at "Guard Mounting". That interesting ceremony sometimes comes off (always at half past eight, before some of the men have finished or even began breakfast. If they are on the "first relief" they are immediately marched off to their posts and are obliged to stand there until eleven. You see this has a tendency to make the men prompt and smart "right up the handle" as the soldiers say. (Whenever this is the case the men usually before going out catch up a piece of bread and run).
>
> I shall try and get a pass and attend service this evening. I have attended once today.
>
> Dec. 30. I attended service again last evening. It seemed like old times. I enjoyed the singing very much. This love of singing seems to be a perfect passion with the soldiers. The church has a very fine organ. I am wandering from my story.
>
> If I am on guard duty I am obliged to take out a part of the relief guard in one direction and the other Corporal in another and the Sergeant places the remainder around the Camp. (I am now speaking as though I were at Camp Jackson.) (I presume you would like to know what orders the sentinels have.) When within six paces of a sentinel I halt the "relief" and command "Number ### — Advance". He advances up to the sentinel and both stand at "arms apart". The sentinel then proceeds to give his orders "I am ordered to guard this road from — to —, to let no one meddle in anyway with the track or wires except those working on the road, and keep a sharp lookout on them. Halt every soldier that comes along and demand a pass. If he has none, take him prisoner and hand him over to the next sentinel. (Thus you see that he could be passed down to Camp without the sentinel leaving his post. "Arrest any person that you may think suspicious. You need not (a sentinel is now "calling" Corporal of the No. 1) stop commissioned officers". The relieved sentinel then falls in the rear and the relief proceeds. The night is the hardest part of the guard duty. At night the orders to the sentinels are the same as in the day time with the exception that they halt every one the comes along when within gun shot. If any one does not stop after being halted three times by the sentinel, he is fired upon. Each sentinel in succession discharges his piece and thus the Camp is alarmed. The Guard is immediately drawn up at a distance from the fire and a reconnoitering party is then sent out to ascertain what is the matter.
>
> As I have said above, the night is the hardest part of it. All the shelter we have is a small tent capable of holding four or five men. The remainder of the Guard "camped down" around the fire. I generally sat up by the fire all night. Sometimes I would take my blanket and stretch out on the ground. Many a night when you have been sleeping comfortably in your bed have I been keeping my vigils by the guard fire. Every two hours the relief is called out. I take my men go out and relieve the sentinel, return and sit by the fire until the next two hours and thus it continues through the night.
>
> At nine A.M the old Guard is marched out to discharge their guns. We sometimes fire in a volley, sometimes by file, and sometimes at a target. Those not on guard cook for those on.
>
> (I will now take a day when I am not on guard and try and give you an account of how I spend my time. I have already in a former letter described how the morning is passed. I believe I omitted one thing. Before (sometimes not till after) breakfast I take a towel and soap and go down to the brook which runs through our Camp and have a "good wash". It is really refreshing. I feel like taking half-a-dozen "somersets" after it. After breakfast I usually take out my rifle (these tents are a great place to rest them) clean and oil it up nicely. If I did not do this it would soon get so rusty that there would be no doing anything with it.
>
> I will now suppose that beef was served out in our days rations and that we had cut all the meat off down to the bone and cooked it for breakfast. I now take a "camp kettle". Fill it two thirds full of water, hang it over the fire, put in the beef, potatoes (pared) and cabbage (if I can get it) and let it boil about an hour. I then season it with salt and pepper if it is to be had. That is soup fit for a king to eat. At least you would think so from the way we all pitched into it. For supper we usually make some tea and sometimes cook a little hominy or rice. Sometimes Luther and I would get a pass and go up town. Sometimes I would stretch out in my tent and go to sleep (after I had been all night and the previous day on guard duty.
>
> At 4 P.M. all not on guard duty are obliged to go out and drill. If any are absent they are placed on guard duty next day with a knapsack of twenty-five, thirty and thirty-five pounds weight. As I have said before I do not have many idle moments. I hate a lazy man. I have quite a number of letters generally to write. It is now late and I have no more time to write. You must excuse this disconnected letter.
>
> Well, my man, you have just saved yourself from the clutches of the law. I have received a letter from you today. So Frances is really going to Canada. I am glad for her sake and sorry for Mothers. How pleasant it must be at home this winter. As quiet up there as though there were no war. Cars are passing here continually loaded with cannon gun carriages, hay, flour, pork, beef, etc, etc, etc. Large numbers of troops pass almost every day though not so large as when we first came on the road. We are, I understand, to be mustered in for pay tomorrow. We are to be inspected I believe at the same time by the Inspector General. New clothes will soon be given us.
>
> Your affectionate brother,
>
> Alexander.
>
> P.S. I have received three letters from home within the last few days and one from Jerseyville. I have also received two papers, one Evangelist and one New York Times. Love to al and especially to Mother.
>
> Dec. 31, 1861. 6½ A.M. You mention in your letter that on going home you "found James reading the January Harper and Mother and Frances talking". How pleasant and cozy that must have been. How I should like to be at home one of these long winter evenings. Wouldn't I have a jolly time, eh? In case of Englands interference I hardly ever expect to see home. I shall never lay down my arms until peace is restored. I hope you and James will show your "pluck" by joining the army, if there be a necessity. I have no more time.
>
> Alexander

## Editorial Notes

This is the capstone letter of the 1861 collection and arguably the most valuable single document for understanding the daily life of a Union corporal. Alexander's verbatim sentinel orders, two-hour night relief rotation, camp alarm procedure, and morning-to-evening routine constitute a primary source of genuine military-historical value. The detail is extraordinary: the sentinel's scripted challenge ("Number ### — Advance"), the passing of prisoners down the line without leaving post, the three-halt-then-fire protocol at night, the camp kettle soup recipe (beef on bone, pared potatoes, cabbage, salt and pepper, boiled an hour). Alexander is self-consciously writing an ethnography — he began it for James, continues it for Charles, and explicitly notes "I am now speaking as though I were at Camp Jackson" to clarify that procedures may differ at the new Baltimore posting.

The New Year's Eve entry (Dec 31, 6:30 AM) is the emotional heart: the cozy domestic scene at home — James reading the January Harper's, Mother and Frances talking — juxtaposed with Alexander sitting by his guard fire in Baltimore, hundreds of miles away, contemplating whether England's interference will mean he never sees home again. His final exhortation ("I hope you and James will show your 'pluck' by joining the army") is prophetic — both brothers eventually did enlist. The Trent Affair thread that runs through Alexander's December letters (Dec 16 → Dec 23 → Dec 31) captures a young soldier processing an international crisis in real time, moving from combative readiness ("I am as ready to fight Britons as Southerners") to grim resolution ("I shall never lay down my arms until peace is restored").
