## Full Transcription

> Jan. 29, 1864
> Capitol Hill
> Washington, D.C.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I rec'd. your welcome and long looked for letter of Jan. 20 yesterday. Am glad to hear that Alex is enjoying his furlough. Hope that he will continue to have a good time. But I hope that he won't neglect to give his attention to his penmanship. He is a bad spender. About his bounty, I think he ought to have his $450 town bounty. Do you know how much bounty the state gives? Alex did not answer my questions whether Luther reenlisted and if there were many of his regt. that did not do so. I wish Alex would write me a good and a long letter. Shall not excuse him if he does not. He must not forget to answer the questions. I am sorry that we do not hear from James, but as it does no good to worry or borrow trouble I don't do it.
>
> About Messrs. Savages' acc'ts., it was $16.56. I don't know how they made it out $20. The interest would not make it amount to that, I think. Guess you did not pay them quite $20. While I was at Plattsburgh I made a memoranda of Joel S's. acc't from memory. It was $27.60. The interest will make it more than that. Perhaps Joel will make some discount. It would be well to settle with him immediately. Have you paid your land tax yet? If so, how much was it? I wish you would send me one dollar as we don't expect to be paid till March when 4 months pay will be due us.
>
> The weather is delightful and has been for about a week back. It is so warm in the daytime that we need no fire and we keep the windows up. The mud has dried up which makes it nice for our dress parades. Last evening I attended with a large number of soldiers, a love feast in a church close by. One of the speakers said that he felt the love of God so in his heart that he felt sometimes when walking the streets that he should holler right out and scare some one. He was a soldier of our regt. The most profane man in our Co. when the bread and water was passed around took some of it. When asked about it afterwards he said it was for the congregation. But I guess he wanted to appear good in the eyes of his girl. I know three of our Co. who attend church regularly for the purpose of seeing some ladies.
>
> I am sorry that Sid'ney is having trouble in his family. Hope that you or Frances will not get mixed up in it. I see you heard it from Sid's girl. Guess the girl lied some, or else greatly exaggerated.
>
> A private in our regt. while on guard at the prison for a bribe of $200 let one of the prisoners escape. The prisoner while trying to escape from this city was captured. He swore that he paid $200 to the sentry for letting him escape. The sentry has been confined in prison for a long time and the rumor now is that he is to be shot.
>
> Yesterday I attended Congress. There was a debate in confiscation. I heard one long speech against it by a Democrat and part of one in favor by a Republican. Please write often and all the news. If Frances can find time wish she would write me about her school.
>
> From your aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Charles attending Congress is remarkable — a corporal walking into the Capitol to listen to debates on confiscation policy. Washington garrison duty gave soldiers access to the machinery of democracy in a way no other posting could. The confiscation debate — whether to seize Confederate property — was one of the most divisive issues of the war, splitting along party lines exactly as Charles describes.

The love feast scene is vintage Charles: the most profane man in the company taking communion bread "for the congregation," soldiers attending church "for the purpose of seeing some ladies." His eye for hypocrisy is sharp but gentle.

The guard bribed $200 to release a prisoner faces execution — the same army that pays its soldiers $13/month. The economics of corruption in wartime are starkly visible.
