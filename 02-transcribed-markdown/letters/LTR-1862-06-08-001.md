## Full Transcription

> June 8, 1862
> Winchester, Virginia
>
> My dear Mother:
>
> Well here I am at Winchester and have not yet had to shoot at the Rebels, much as I desire it. They took "leg bail" at Harpers Ferry and we have not yet caught up with them. They well merit the degree of F.F.V (Free Footed Virginians.) We (our Regt.) are now doing the provost guard of the city. The General of the Rail Road Brigade is trying to get us back again, but I think General Siegel and Stow will make a few objections. If we go back I shall be furious, raving.
>
> Well I suppose you would like to know how I stood the march of about (I believe) forty miles. I stood it "first rate". Marched into the city hall (where we are now quartered) with my Regiment to the tune of "Yankee Doodle". When we were within a few miles of the city quite a number fell out, not being able to keep up. Luther fell out.
>
> We left Harpers Ferry last Tuesday about dark and marched nearly all night, the mud — if I say knee deep I wont much stretch the truth. We halted within a mile or two of Charlestown and rested until daylight. We threw ourselves down by the roadside and in the corners of the fences and slept until morning. It rained almost constantly. Charlestown is quite a large place. John Brown was hung there. We halted the next day about one oclock at Smithsfield and remained there until the next day. It rained all that night and all the next day and in what kind of condition do you think the roads were in? You can guess. We have no tents with us, and very few have rubber blankets, and those who have purchased them themselves. We camped in the open fields. We started early the next morning and did what I call "some tall marching". We reached Winchester a little after twelve A.M., a distance of about eighteen miles. Now dont you think to march that in about six hours is doing pretty well fro a soldier encumbered with his knapsack, haversack, canteen, cartridge box (with forty rounds of cartridges in it) rifle and bayonet. The regulation days march is fifteen miles. We beat every other regiment on the road marching. I hope our other Companies will soon join us.
>
> Afternoon. — Who do you think I have just seen. Guess. Dont believe you can. It was the last person I expected to see here. I never was more astonished in my life. Well — I will tell you. Dont believe you can guess. It was Albert H. Cook, himself. Yes, Cook — Albert Cook. He is a fine looking fellow. I saw last night Lieut. Brinkerhoff Miner. He is about the same old sixpence. They are attached to Banks "Signal Corps". We are now in Banks Division. So Capt. Rich has been cashiered. What a pity. We are under command of Siegel but he is under Banks. Love to all. I hope we shall soon have a chance to charge bayonets on the rebels. Four hundred were brought in prisoners today. I hope James will pass. I feel very anxious.
>
> Your affectionate son,
>
> Alexander
>
> P.S. It will make it very pleasant to have Cook so near. Write often. Do not wait for me. I shall probably have very few opportunities of writing. Direct to Winchester, Virginia. I received a letter from Henry the day we left the Ferry. (letter head bears photo of Maj. Gen. McClellan)

## Editorial Notes

Alexander's march from Harpers Ferry to Winchester is the most detailed march narrative in the collection — knee-deep mud, roadside sleeping, constant rain, no tents, 18 miles in 6 hours with full equipment. His itemization of what a soldier carries (knapsack, haversack, canteen, cartridge box with 40 rounds, rifle, bayonet) is invaluable period detail. The "F.F.V." joke — turning "First Families of Virginia" into "Free Footed Virginians" — was common Union soldier humor. Passing through Charles Town where "John Brown was hung" gives the march historical resonance. The Albert Cook reunion is written with breathless excitement — three sentences of "Guess. Dont believe you can." Cook will likely appear in future letters. Luther falling out on the march is a quiet but concerning detail; the older Luther is struggling while 18-year-old Alexander thrives.
