## Full Transcription

> May 4th, 1863
> Champlain
>
> My dear Alexander:
>
> I am feeling so sad today. I have just finished the perusal of all of dear Henry's letters. The reading of them brought him so vividly before me. I have wept until I am almost blind. I can see his sweet face and hear his sweet voice — it seems as if I have had a visit with him. How often in his letters he expressed a longing for home and his disgust with the vices of the army. He speaks of you often and expresses fears that a soldier's hardships will be too much for you. Every line of his letters is full of affection. He says "I often dream of
>
> [page break]
>
> being home; I think of you every night when I am alone; I long to see you all; at this time next year, I hope to be with you," etc., etc.
>
> You remember how often McDonald has said that he saw him after he was wounded — limping to the rear leaning upon his gun. I came across McKee's letter today, written me after the battle, in which he says, "I did not see Henry after he was wounded." That reliance can be placed upon the statement of such a man! I cannot realize it. As I read his letters today, it is as if I should soon see him again as if he would soon be home where he so much longed to be. He has gone to his far home of bliss — yes, one of them, and I shall follow. And you, my dear boy, try to plod on borrowing a little longer in this vale of tears — yes, tears, it is so — and must be, till my pilgrimage is ended. She notes in the letter he says you have marching orders — any may soon meet the enemy. I am ready, for my trust is in One who will not forsake me." How comforting.
>
> I could not — This is the first time that I have read his letters since his death. Today I was necessitated to do it to get evidence in regard to money matters which Mr. L. wished. I do not think he will accomplish for what he has undertaken. I have put the letters all away, and may never read them again, a retrospect of his sufferings. They were very great. I wonder that he survived them with his feeble constitution.
>
> I mailed you your furlough this morning and took your county bounty to the cashier of the bank this morning when he says he will get around next week. Mr. B. said this morning that Mr. A. V. told him you boys offered him twenty dollars for getting your bounty; that he thought it was too much, and refused it. He reconsidered and concluded to living the more, I should think beware of lawyers hereafter. Some of them get their living by fleecing soldiers.
>
> I probably shall not finish this letter for three or four days. I mailed one to you yesterday and one this morning. I hope to here from Chas. this week. He has not heard from home since early in Feb. How strange it is that our letters are lost. I do not know how to get letters to him. Co. I of the 153rd had sit from their Co. in the Red River Battle. How thankful I am that Chas. was not with them. That last, They have a good Serg't. I am sure, must get into a great deal of hard fighting.
>
> May 5th — Oh, Alex, I have shocking news to impart to you this morning. A terrible tragedy occurred here last night. Isaac Dedds shot Mr. Bass through the right arm and then himself through the head. I have not yet learned all the particulars. About ten o'clock, Mr. Averill, who was in his office, heard the report of a pistol near Mrs. Allison's. He went there three times — distantly and found Dr. Allison. Mr. B. was taken immediately to the hotel. Physicians were called who pronounced the wound mortal, and told him that he had but a short time to live. His mother was sent for the evening. He made his will during the night. He is not expected to survive the day out.
>
> [page break]
>
> Frances has gone down to dismiss the school which is doubtless closed
>
> There was a lecture at the Methodist church last eve which Mr. B. attended. He called at Mr. Vitohoek's on his way home. Mrs. A. was there — and he could do no less than accompany her home. He has been desperately in love with his aunt, S. Allison's widow. She has repeatedly refused to marry him. About three weeks since he married a girl for whom he had no affection and was talking about getting a divorce. He was jealous of Mr. Bass without the slightest cause (he would about as soon shoot as to marry her). So he thought he could put an end to all their lives. It was premeditated. He has been practicing with his six shooter several days past in the ravine part back of our house. I heard it at seven o'clock last night. I overheard some one say this morning that he was pierced by five balls. Mrs. Allison's arm will be amputated. Mr. B. talks about his mother constantly. She will probably be on the seven train to Rouse's point this eve, and get a conveyance up. I fear she will be too late — poor woman.
>
> Frances will be up before long and will know more. She will probably see him. It is a very sad affair and has cast a gloom on the place.
>
> Young Carter of the 34th who had his thumb shot off in the battle of Antietam, returned last week, discharged. He left the field before Henry was wounded — says his position in the ranks was fourth and next to Ballerjy who was killed. Thinks the reason why so little is known of Henry is that those who were near him were killed. Have you done all that you can do in ascertaining whether he is a prisoner. Cannot you get one of the officers to make inquiries under a flag of truce sometimes going to Richmond or other parts of Va.? You should do all in your power to learn if possible whether he is in rebellion. I very much fear that he died on the field. It will be a life long sorrow to me. "My grave-stone will be brought with sorrow to the grave," if he cannot be found. My heart is breaking, breaking, breaking. I cannot dwell upon this painful, painful subject without being almost deranged.
>
> Wednesday Morn. — 14th — I am wondering if it can be possible for you poor soldier a to keep on your feet this tempestuous morning. The wind is blowing a perfect hurdle. I am afraid your tents will skeddadle over to Lee's army.
>
> Mr. Cook says Charles is looking very well. He has not yet seen the rough side of a soldier's life, as he says. God grant that he may not. I wish he was home. Your Uncle Fullerton back your address. Thinks he shall go and see you. He has contracted to furnish the army ninety thousand dollars' worth of clothing. You know he has factories at the east.
>
> Frances and I are yet hoping that you will be home this week. I hope we shall not be doomed to disappointment in not seeing you soon. Oh, how sad that Henry's time for returning home was so near and our anticipation of seeing us him in a short time should be blighted. He was so happy in the prospects; said he should begin to count the days soon; dear, dear Henry, my darling boy. Oh, what a pall is over my heart, and must be while I am in this vale of tears. But my pilgrimage is nearly over. Already the loved ones who have gone, are bearing me home.
>
> You do not say whether Mr. Luther is with you. If he is, remember us kindly to him. How is his health. The small pox is quite prevalent here. Mrs. Beaumont has had it. There have been no deaths as yet. There is no sleighing here, and has been none for several weeks. I hope you will bring snow with you, for pony needs exercising. God bless and preserve you, my darling boy.
>
> Affectionately,
>
> Mother

## Cross-Reference Notes

### Connections to Other Letters in Collection
- **McDONALD FULLY CONTRADICTED.** Mother has found McKee's letter: "I did not see Henry after he was wounded." McDonald's story — the man "limping to the rear leaning upon his gun" — is now contradicted by a named witness. The hope that Henry survived the wounding is collapsing.
- **Why no witnesses:** Carter's testimony provides the answer the collection has been seeking: Henry's neighbors in the ranks were killed. The men who would have seen what happened to him after he went down were themselves dead. This explains the information vacuum.
- **"Dear, dear Henry, my darling boy":** This passage — which also appears in Charles's April 27 letter — is confirmed as Mother's voice. Charles was quoting (or the typist mixed in) this passage from Mother's letter.
- **Mother's dual grief:** While mourning Henry, she witnesses a shooting in Champlain. The violence of the home front mirrors the violence of the war.

## Editorial Notes

This is the heart of the collection — a mother reading her dead son's letters, weeping until she's nearly blind, recording his words ("I often dream of being home"), and then pivoting to a shooting in her own village. The juxtaposition is grotesque: Henry dead at Antietam, Bass dying in the hotel, Dedds shot through the head, Mrs. Allison's arm amputated — violence everywhere, on both sides of the war.

Carter's testimony is the collection's most important forensic detail: Henry was 4th in the ranks, next to a man who was killed. "Those who were near him were killed." This is why no one can say what happened to Henry — the witnesses are dead. McDonald's claim to have seen Henry limping away is now contradicted by McKee, who was closer to the action and says he saw nothing.

Mother's "My heart is breaking, breaking, breaking" is the rawest grief in the entire collection. Seven months of uncertainty resolved into this: her son is dead, his letters are all that remain, and she may never read them again because the pain is too great.
