## Full Transcription

> June 27, 1865
> Near Alexandria, Va.
>
> Dear Fannie:
>
> I advised in my last letter home that my next should be to you, so here goes. My last letter was dated — I believe — the 16th of June. That's a naughty boy I have been I know you'll say, but I have been waiting for the confirmation of certain rumors and now my dearest Sister, Fannie, I am rejoiced to inform you that last night the long wished for order came to muster the Sixtieth New York Veteran Volunteers out of the United States service immediately. Therefore just as soon as we can make out our papers and get transportation we shall come to Ogdensburgh, and there be discharged and paid off. That's a happy day that will be, eh, Miss Fannie, agreed and a proud one for me. God spare my life to see the time. When I left home nearly four years since our country was racked from its base to its center by one of the most violent and gigantic rebellions recorded on the pages of history. We knew not friends from foes. Treason walked our legislative halls and had command of our armies. Now by the providence of God, after four years of unparalleled struggling we stand a free and united nation, than which in point of honor we have no superior and in point of freedom, no equal.
>
> The mail will leave soon — I must close. Please give much love to Mother and Mrs. Hoff. I received a magazine and some papers from home the other day. I am very much obliged for the magazine. The papers were more than welcome. Please write soon. A letter will come from C. here in two days.
>
> Your aff. brother,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
>
> P.S. Please return to Mrs. Bostwick the enclosed letter. Culver was discharged a few days since, and is doubtless at home with his friends by this time.

## Editorial Notes

This is the letter the entire collection has been building toward. "The long wished for order came to muster the Sixtieth New York Veteran Volunteers out of the United States service immediately." Four years — from September 21, 1861 to June 27, 1865 — and the war is over.

Alexander's valedictory is the finest passage he ever wrote: "When I left home nearly four years since our country was racked from its base to its center by one of the most violent and gigantic rebellions recorded on the pages of history. We knew not friends from foes. Treason walked our legislative halls and had command of our armies. Now by the providence of God, after four years of unparalleled struggling we stand a free and united nation, than which in point of honor we have no superior and in point of freedom, no equal."

The boy who enlisted at 17, who survived Chancellorsville, Gettysburg, Lookout Mountain, the Atlanta Campaign, and the siege of Nashville — who was wounded, scurvy-ridden, night-blind, and half-starved — writes his sister with the joy of a man who lived through everything. "That's a happy day that will be, eh, Miss Fannie."
