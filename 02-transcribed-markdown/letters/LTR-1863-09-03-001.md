## Full Transcription

> Sept. 3rd, 1863
> Ellis Ford
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your letter of the 20th of August tonight — and feel — Oh! so bad. I do not blame you for writing as you do, but I should like to know the reason why you do not get my letters. Mother, during the last two months I have received but two letters from home. I have felt so bad that I had but small heart to write. I wrote to you and to Frances and to Albert Cook. Sent a paper to Frances, to Capt. Ransom, to Cook, and to Ingram, and heard nothing from any of you. The letter I received tonight has written on the envelope "60th N.Y. Vol. — not 6th Corps." Direct to the 2nd Division, 12th Army Corps, and then there can be no mistake.
>
> Mother, could you for a moment suppose I could ever forget my old home and my Mother? Never, never. I love my Mother better than life itself. There is nothing I would not endure for your sake, and it makes me feel so badly to think that you could for a moment think I had forgotten you. It is thoughts of you and my old home that have sustained me in our long marches. No, Mother, I can never forget you.
>
> One of the Divisions in this Army has been warned in "general orders" not to send valuable matter in the mail as a number of letters from their Division were found beside the railroad rifled of their contents. Measures were then being taken to ferret out the thief. My letters may have disappeared in a similar manner.
>
> We are now camped near the river in a very pleasant spot, with plenty of nice cool water close at hand. A small stream runs close by in which we wash, etc. The Reg't. is now obliged to go on picket every other day. A rather funny thing happened one night before last. About twelve or one o'clock the picket opposite me fired two shots into his own men surrounding us, wounding one man severely. It was criminal to hear his groans. The river is about one hundred yards wide and very shallow in most places. It is just about the same as the river at G. The rebel picket stands on one side and I on the other. We sit and look at each other very composedly as we have both learned better manners than to fire at each other. The rebels are very anxious to exchange papers with us, but we have strict orders not to do it. If the settling of the war was left to the soldiers we would do it in short order. Some of the Rebs are pretty nice fellows, much to my surprise.
>
> Now, Mother, please write me often. I promise you I will, and I hope they will not be miscarried or destroyed. I was very much worn out when we reached here. The last day we marched thirty-five miles. I have been blessed with any number of Job's comforters, of late. My candle is about burnt out. Love to all and a great deal to Frances. She seems to doubt my love for her. Shall write her soon. Her letter needs attention. I came within an ace of tearing it up before I had read three lines. As I continued reading, my wrath cooled down measurably and when I had finished I felt more like giving her a kiss than boxing her ears. Please write soon. Much love to Albert.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

"Mother, could you for a moment suppose I could ever forget my old home and my Mother? Never, never." Alexander's anguish is not about the war — it's about Mother thinking he has forgotten her. The postal system has failed them: letters stolen from railroads, misdirected between the 60th NY and 6th Corps. The family's communication lifeline is broken.

The picket scene is one of the war's most repeated images: enemies sitting across a narrow river, looking at each other "very composedly," wanting to trade newspapers but forbidden to do so. "Some of the Rebs are pretty nice fellows, much to my surprise." The gap between the war's politics and its soldiers' humanity opens in that sentence.
