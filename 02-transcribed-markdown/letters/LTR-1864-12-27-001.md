## Full Transcription

> Dec. 27thths, 1864
> Camp Russell
> Near Newtown, Va.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I recd. your letter of Dec. 20th two or three days since. Am very, very sorry that you have been so anxious about me, but bad news travels fast, as you heard no bad news it would have been better to have taken it for granted that I was all right.
>
> I wrote James yesterday advising him to be in no hurry about coming back here. Recd. a letter from him a few days since in which he says that he will be back soon here.
>
> Christmas passed off very quietly here, all the men had a ration of whiskey which is now a gill. Enclosed I send twenty dollars. After using all you need of my money to pay taxes, etc., if there is any left you can invest it in the three years V — 30 Bonds. Please write how much the tax is. If the collector calls on you for the tax, you will have to pay five etc. on a dollar, but if you pay him on the day he advertises it will be only one ct. on a dollar for the collectors fee. I hope you will have no trouble in getting your wood drawn & cut. How is Frances going to get through the snow drifts to school. I guess she & you do not like the big snow storms.
>
> I have not written Alex yet. Shall wait till he rejoins his regt. If he was with his regt. he would have had a chance to help take Savannah, but he is lucky that he missed that march of 500 miles. I hore the war is nearly over, but have not much faith that it is. If it lasts James & Alex will be in it, but I will be out next fall.
>
> Not long since De Sweeney called on me at my tent & showed me a letter he had read from you about me. I told him that I had written you two or three days before. Did he write you? He said that I must write oftener. When? On the march last fall De Sweeney did all he could for James & I. Has been a friend. Lt. Col. Strain was the one that got me the class at the commissary & at Banks HQ. Gr. He was very friendly. Have not heard how he is getting along.
>
> I must stop as I have to go on picket in a few minutes. Please send me some postage stamps as I am entirely out. Think James has no stamps.
>
> Your Aff. Son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

The last letter of 1864 — and the last in this PDF. Charles writes from picket duty at Camp Russell, the year ending as it began: in the field, on guard, out of stamps. "I hore the war is nearly over, but have not much faith that it is." The pessimism of a man who has been fighting for two years and sees no end.

The fall of Savannah produced "rousing cheers" from the brigade. Alexander, in Nashville, is "lucky" to have missed the 500-mile march. Charles's assessment is practical: marching is worse than missing a victory.

Dr. Sweeney and Lt. Col. Strain — the two officers who befriended the Hubbell brothers. Sweeney "did all he could for James & I" on the march. Strain got Charles his commissary position. The army's personal networks — officers who look out for enlisted men — are as important as its chain of command.

"Please send me some postage stamps as I am entirely out. Think James has no stamps." The war's most persistent supply problem: postage stamps. Three brothers, two theaters, zero stamps.
