## Full Transcription

> Sept. 30/62
> U.S. Plattsburgh Barracks
>
> Dear Alek —
>
> It is three weeks yesterday since I left home for this place.
>
> I was very much concerned last night to hear that Henry was wounded. Alek Douglas told me, said that he thought he was only slightly wounded. I hope that it is true that he is not badly wounded. I hope that you & I will come out of the war all right. But we must take our chances. I mean to take care of myself as well as I can. I have had an easy time so far. Have been on duty as Corporal of the guard once & as Sergeant of the guard once. The privates are on guard every three or four days. They lay pretty thick in the guard house on an elevator floor, 16 sleeping at once packed close together. 21 men are now on duty with 2 corporals & 2 Sergeants. The privates can sleep 4 hours at a time at night.
>
> We have had two men deserts. They had a pass to Plattsburgh village about one mile from here & they went to the village & took the steamer for Albany. We have had any amount of elbow jousting, but I think there will now be a stop put to it. The men do not much mind being shut up here if they can jump the pickets & have a good spree. But there is a new order now which if a picket jumper will be sent to Albany as deserters. Two were sent as such last night.
>
> [page break]
>
> The barracks here are very good stone buildings. There are two separate buildings one for the officers & one for the men. In front of the mens quarters are two verandahs, an upper & lower the the whole length of the building, which is quite long. It is a two story building divided into rooms, 6 bunks long & 4 bunks high. It is 6 rooms long. Some of the rooms have no bunks but have little cots made of rough boards for the sick. There is a doctor here & he has had some patients already, but I have had good health so he has not called on him.
>
> There are two new rough board barracks used by the last reg. here, besides an old wooden building used as a barracks. The bunks are made of rough boards wide enough for two to sleep comfortable. Each one has a straw tick & a blanket. I have only taken my pants off twice since I left home. But I think I shall take them off hereafter. There is a fire place to each room. We had a fire in them before the stoves were put up.
>
> I have been appointed corporal while here & I suppose it will be permanent. The non commissioned officers have been drilling in a squad by themselves.
>
> Since commencing this letter I have received a letter from home. Mother is frightened about Henry. But I think he was wounded in the thigh slightly so that he could not walk well & then taken prisoner. That big Shaw, I guess you know him, over 6 feet high, is enlisted here. He lived in Dudleys farm awhile. He told me that he saw Henrys name in the list of wounded — wounded either in the thigh or his or his forgets which. I wish you could make some enquiries in Henrys Co. There is a man here who had a brother wounded in the battles before Richmond. He was reported missing & no one knew what had become of him. He has just had a letter from his brother in Richmond who is well & says he could as lief be there as with our army. I think Henry is with the rebels in Dixie.
>
> Alek will you write soon & let us know all the news. I suppose I will not like military life as well when I come to camp out, but I have made up my mind to take things as they come. I do not expect to have a very nice time. Forewarned is forearmed you know.
>
> Now Alek remember remember & write often.
>
> Your Aff. Brother
> C. F. Hubbell
>

## Editorial Notes

This letter is the most detailed description of Union training camp infrastructure in the collection. Charles catalogs the barracks like an architect: dimensions, materials, sleeping arrangements, guard rotation schedules. He is a new soldier discovering the physical reality of military life ("only taken my pants off twice since I left home") and already noting the institutional dysfunction (desertions, picket-jumping, "elbow jousting"). His description of 16 men sleeping packed together on an "elevator floor" is vivid material culture.

But the letter's emotional center is the second half, where Mother's letter arrives mid-writing and shifts everything to Henry. Charles's prisoner theory is notable for its internal logic and its function: if Henry is a prisoner, he is alive, and if alive, there is hope. The anecdote about the man whose brother wrote from Richmond is offered as evidence but reads as wishful thinking. "I think Henry is with the rebels in Dixie" — Charles wants this to be true because the alternative is worse.
