## Full Transcription

> Mch. 7th, 1864
> Stevenson, Ala.
>
> Dear Fannie:
>
> So you think I know you would give a thousand worlds to see me — eh? You would like to make me think I am something great, wouldn't you? A fine idea, isn't it? How do you know but what I would get so puffed up that I would not see my boots for six months and perchance, like a certain ancient philosopher who always walked with his head among the stars, might fall into a ditch and break my neck? I warn you to beward of flattery, especially to a young gent who is as susceptible as I am — beward.
>
> So you and Miss Averill have lost your sled rides, eh? Well, I hope it will not be long before the terrible title of a "veteran soldier" will be laid aside by your humble servant and he walk among his friends again in the meek garb of a citizen. Honestly, Frances, it will be the proudest day of my life. You tell Miss A. that when that happy time arrives we will reverse the order and I will draw you both on the sled to the houses. The Brigade band is practicing this afternoon. The music is grand. Have you received the History of the Regt. yet? Please write soon.
>
> Your aff. brother,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Alexander's letter to Frances is pure literary charm — the ancient philosopher falling into a ditch, the mock warning against flattery, the promise to reverse roles and pull her on the sled. But underneath the playfulness: "Honestly, Frances, it will be the proudest day of my life" — when he can walk among friends as a civilian again. He has re-enlisted for three more years. The Atlanta Campaign is two months away. The proudest day is very far off.
