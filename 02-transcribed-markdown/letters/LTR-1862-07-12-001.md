## Full Transcription

> July 12, 1862
> Camp near Haryson's Landing
> James River
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received two letters from you the other day and was very glad to hear from you and that James had passed his examination. I sent ten dollars in two letters, five in each, but had no way of registering them. I hope you will receive them.
>
> In my last letter to Frances I gave some account of our retreat. I will state some more of the particulars. When the sick were ordered to the hospital, McDonald came to me twice and urged me to go, but I did not feel like leaving the rest and so stayed. The battle commenced the last of June about five o'clock in the afternoon. We were counter-marching for some time before we came to the woods. We were then ordered on the double quick and had to throw our knapsacks. One of our officers, Billings, was sun-struck. I lost my cap in the charge. We came to a bayonet charge just as we entered the woods. Our regiment fired very low and did great execution.
>
> The Irish Brigade charged a rebel battery — it was a splendid sight, shouting at the top of their voices. We lost a great many men, and the battery, ammunition, and provisions. We marched all the next day in a hard rain without a blanket. There are many who shirk the fights. The rebels claim a victory but I think McClellan managed very well. We were not whipped by any means.
>
> Lieut. Webb has invited me to his tent. He is a very fine fellow. I should like very much to get my discharge but it is very difficult. I have now served thirteen months. If I serve my full time I shall receive one hundred dollars bounty and a land warrant.
>
> My diarrhea is very bad. Sherman, who was head doctor, is now in Congress. Walker is head man now. We have a new doctor from New York. I received a letter from Alexander dated June 22nd. He was well, at Middletown. I hope Capt. Rich and Lieut. Scott will return for our company needs them badly.
>
> David Savage wants a secesh bill. I have mailed James's letter to Alexander. Please write often.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Henry

## Editorial Notes

Henry's refusal to go to hospital when McDonald urged him twice is characteristic — he won't abandon his comrades even when sick with worsening diarrhea. The Irish Brigade charge at a rebel battery is one of the most vivid images in the collection: "a splendid sight, shouting at the top of their voices." Henry's simultaneous admiration and horror captures the paradox of combat spectacle. His calculation of bounty and land warrant ($100 + land) shows the financial bind keeping soldiers in service — the same man who confessed temptation to desert is now weighing what he'd lose by leaving. The medical turnover (Sherman to Congress, Walker in charge, new doctor from NY) suggests instability in regimental medical care at precisely the moment soldiers need it most. "Capt. Rich" is almost certainly "Capt. Reich" from earlier letters — Henry's spelling varies.
