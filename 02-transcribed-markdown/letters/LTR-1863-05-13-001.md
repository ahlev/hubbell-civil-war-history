## Full Transcription

> May 13, 1863
> Alexandria, Va.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I went to Aquia Creek this morning and on my return this afternoon at about 4:00 P.M. found a letter for me that came this morning after I left. Went to the Creek for two reasons; one of them was that I wanted to find out whether Alex came out of the fight all right, and the other reason was that if he had I wanted to see him. Lieut. Knapp told me that he could get me a pass to come back from the Provost M shall at A. Creek. Well, the Lieut. asked the Provost Marshal for a pass, and he replied that it was no way to do business. As he would give me no pass the only way to get back if I stayed would be to wait till our guard came down again and go back with them. But as it was uncertain when the guard would come again I did not want to stay two or three days which I did not, so I came back today with the guard.
>
> I only heard last night that the 60th reg't. was about a mile from Aquia Landing, so I started this morning to see him, but with what success I have just told you. I hope Alex is uninjured. Have been searching to hear about him for two or three days. Two days ago I saw the 16 Regt. N.Y.V. on board a steamer that stopped here for some old clothes they left here boxed up a year ago. The reg't. was on its way home, discharged as its time was cut out. The men looked hard; that is, they were tanned and clothes very dirty. They belonged to Sedgwick's Corps. Said it on coming out of the woods the first thing they knew the rebs fired a volley into them. This was from the first line of battle which then laid down and the second line fired and laid down in turn, and then the third line fired. Then they had three rebel volleys in a few minutes.
>
> I saw George Corbin with the 126th Reg't. You say that you want me to go over the battle ground of Antietam. I don't know where this battle field is, and don't know who would go with me that knows where the 34th reg't. fought. As for his Fry, his story is very doubtful and there is no telling where he is now. I think there is not the least doubt that Henry was killed. We would have heard from him long before this if he had been living.
>
> Every day reg'ts. are going home, their time being out. I guess we will stay here all summer. Hope we will for we are having a good time. If the cavalry come on here and the satisfaction of sending us up is in a
>
> [page break]
>
> short time should be blighted. He was so happy in the prospects; said he should begin to count the days soon; dear, dear Henry, my darling boy. Oh, what a pall is over my heart, and must be while I am in this vale of tears. But my pilgrimage is nearly over. Already the loved ones who have gone, are bearing me home.
>
> Enclosed is my ambrotype taken May 11th. My coat is too large, always was. In this picture gallery I see a woman looking at her picture taken while she was walking a rope from the top of the strate to the top of the building on the opposite side of the street. I have seen her walk it two or three times, once with a very short dress, circus fashion; another time dressed in tights. She had a long balancing pole in her hands, and kneeled down, stood on one leg, the chair one swinging around up very high. The street below was crowded to see her walk the rope.
>
> How come Frances to take three of Mr. Simonds classes? I am afraid she will work herself too hard.
>
> I would not sell your cow if I were you. I bought a watch for $12.25 and have sold it for $16.50 and wait till pay day. Think I shall buy one soon, as it is necessary to have the time. Have $12. still left. I wish you could use what you want of my money and put the rest out at interest. Remember, I want you to use as much as you want of it. You had better pay our new tax out of my money. Ought not to sit in a seat without paying. My health is good. Hope your and Frances' is also. Have had two swims in the Potomac this week.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Charles's search for Alexander after Chancellorsville is a mirror of the Henry search after Antietam — same regiment area, same desperate inquiries, same inability to get through military bureaucracy to find a brother. The difference: Alexander is alive, and Charles will find out. The ambrotype — Charles's portrait — is a tangible artifact: a young corporal in an oversized coat, swimming in the Potomac, watching tightrope walkers in Alexandria. The war as spectacle and routine simultaneously.
