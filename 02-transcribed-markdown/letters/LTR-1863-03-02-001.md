## Full Transcription

> March 2, 1863
> Alexandria
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your letter of Feb. 23, also the Observer, in which you said Alex had been home. I was surprised to learn that he had been home. Your letter did not say much about his visit, but I intend to go and see him before long to hear about his visit and to get these socks which came just in time.
>
> I went to the Episcopal Church last evening. Heard a very good sermon. There are two or three Methodist churches here that are open. I must go to them. Two or three days since our chaplain made our Co. a visit. He visited each tent and left a few tracts. The men all like him, as he is a cordial man, shakes hands with each one. I have not heard him say anything about religion. I guess the men would rather talk about something else.
>
> I received a letter from Uncle John which I will send in this. Wish you would send it back in your next letter and tell me how to answer it. Did he visit us a year or two since? He is a strong democrat and a little sesesh, I think. I don't like to write to almost strangers.
>
> We were mustered in for pay two days since; expect our pay this month. I have bought me a pair of new boots for $4.25, also sold my watch for $12.00 to be paid at 60 days. The man is not very good, but if he don't pay, I will take the watch back. He is a great spendthrift, has borrowed $5.00 and given $10.00 for it. This man is from Canada. A corporal needs a watch to relieve guard, but I will buy another.
>
> About that land that Mr. Rye claims, I don't know as we can say anything. It is doubtful if we could succeed about the road off our garden. I should not let them have it unless they pay what it is worth. Did Grandfather in his will lay down the boundary between Mr. Rye and us? If he did I do not suppose a surveyor would go by it in settling a dispute.
>
> Lieut. Knapp told me that they had no Champlain officers in Co. H, 60th Reg't.; that both Lieut.'s had resigned. Asked me what sergeant Alex was and I told him that I did not know. There was a soldier shot dead by a citizen and I told him that I did not know. He (the citizen) has been arrested. Have you had a visit from Lieut. Col. Armstrong? I heard today that he was routed out of Plattsburgh. A woman who has just come on to live with her husband here in camp tells the story. I also heard that the friends of some of the soldiers had sued him for that $270. bounty which they did not get. We none of us got it because we enlisted too late. But he promised it to us. I don't believe either of these stories are true. There are twelve or 15 women living with their husbands in our reg't. Each man has a tent for himself and wife. The wife draws
>
> [page break]
>
> rations. She must wash clothes for them, but they must pay her. Today we brought a lot of stragglers from the camp to go to their regiments. I stout woman was marching through the mud after her husband. Our men thought she ought to stay at home.
>
> Have you called on Mrs. Burdick? I wish Frances would write me. How do you and Frances get along? Has Silas made his speech yet? I think he will not make a brilliant speech. Are drygoods very cheap now and groceries? I wish you would send me the Tribune after you have both read it unless you want to keep it for the story. Doesn't Alex want the Observer? The mud here is drying up very fast. After that snow, sleigh rides were taken here.
>
> The war does not look like closing soon to me. I say stay my three years. I will close by asking you to write soon.
>
> Your aff. Son
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

"The war does not look like closing soon to me. I say stay my three years." Charles's realism is a counterpoint to Alexander's post-furlough optimism ("I believe this war cannot last much longer"). Charles, who has not been home, sees more clearly. The women-in-camp passage is sociologically rich — wives drawing rations, washing for pay, marching through mud to follow regiments. This is the domestic underbelly of the Army of the Potomac that rarely makes the histories.
