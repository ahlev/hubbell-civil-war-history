## Full Transcription

> Dec. 9th, 1864
> Chestnut Hill Hospital
>
> Dear Mother and Fannie:
>
> I received your letter, papers and package this morning. All were directed Walnut Hill, but they got here nevertheless. The vest is a very nice one, fits very well, and is very comfortable. You took too much trouble with it, considering it is to be worn under a blouse. I carry thread and needles, buttons, pins each, in my pocket book, and find that is the most convenient thing I can carry them in. The handkerchief, I think Miss Avey; my last one went to pieces not long ago. The papers will be doubly acceptable when I get to my Regiment, for there reading is very scarce.
>
> Yesterday a fellow from the 153rd told me that he had heard that the First Brigade of the first Division was now in winter quarters at Newtown. I do not think you need be troubled about Charlie, if he is sick, he will be well taken care of.
>
> I can now walk around very well. My wound has nearly healed. I feel like getting back to my regiment again. You asked what we had for dinner Thanksgiving Day. The dinner was turkey and gravy, oyster soup (I could not eat that) boiled potatoes, bread, cranberry preserves and ice cream. All was cold. It could not be otherwise when the table was so large. Each man had his dinner piled on his plate. Our ward got their dinner about four oclock, being among the last they were obliged to wait for the second table.
>
> To speak of my punctuality in writing. During the campaign I did not carry a knapsack and consequently no writing materials. I could not easily carry a load buckled on my shoulders; instead of that I carried my bundle in a sort of a roll passing over my right shoulder and under my left arm which was far easier.
>
> During the next campaign I am going to carry writing paper folded up and put in the cover of a small book. Think I can easily carry that. You speak of being anxious about Alex. I do not see why you should be if he has only been absent two weeks. You do not know how well a soldier boy can take care of himself. Last night there was news that three tons of mail was sent around by sea to Savannah for Sherman's army.
>
> Enclosed you will find a ring. I made several while on my way last Spring to the regiment, but they got broke in my pocket book. About a furlough; they do not give a single one now because there are so many out. I am so near well that the chances would be small anyway.
>
> Did you see the list of officers wounded in the 153rd? Did Lt. Colonel Strain lose a leg? Some say he did — others that he did not.
>
> James

## Editorial Notes

James at Chestnut Hill Hospital is recovering well — wound nearly healed, walking around, ready to go back. The Thanksgiving dinner is the best meal described in the collection: turkey, gravy, oyster soup, potatoes, cranberry preserves, and ice cream. "All was cold" — the ward ate at four o'clock, last in line. Even abundance comes with army bureaucracy.

The ring made on the march — "I made several while on my way last Spring" — is a small act of craft from a teenager walking from Louisiana to Washington. They broke in his pocket book. One survives to be mailed home.

"You do not know how well a soldier boy can take care of himself." — James reassuring Mother about Alexander. The youngest brother, shot through the thigh at Cedar Creek, reassures his mother that soldiers are self-sufficient. The confidence of a man who walked five miles on a wounded leg.
