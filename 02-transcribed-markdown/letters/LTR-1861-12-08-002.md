## Full Transcription

> Dec. 8, 1861
> Camp Jackson
>
> My dear Brother:
>
> I have at length heard from home. Four letters all in a bunch. Doing pretty well — don't you think so? I feel about five hundred per cent better. You have no idea how much good they have done me. It took me nearly two hours to read them all. It seems to me that I never spent that space of time so pleasantly before.
>
> So you thought I had forgotten you all at home. Well, well, well, I thought you knew me better than that, but then I don't wonder at it. It was no fault of mine that you did not hear from me sooner. You may be sure of that. I began a letter when we were at Kalarama Heights. When we arrived at Camp Jackson it was quite late in the evening. We were obliged to stack our arms and knapsacks prior to pitching our tents. I was detailed for guard duty that night, and Luther as one of an escort to the Major as he was oblige to go back to the Relay House on business — so that I had no one to look after my knapsack. A sergeant in Company C took it by mistake and I did not find it until quite a number of days afterward. The letter and my writing materials were in it. Now you see that I was not to blame.
>
> Charley in his letter (how I should like to see him) asks me to "write all the minute details of my life" in Camp. I will do my best. Reveille beats at half-past five. As soon as I hear the drum (almost the first tap wakes me) I spring up, put my cap, jacket and shoes on (I generally sleep with them off) turn out and fall into line. We are allowed only five minutes to do all this in. At the expiration of the five minutes the Orderly proceeds to call the roll. If any are absent they are placed on guard duty with their knapsacks on. It is no small thing to carry a knapsack for seventy-four hours.
>
> After roll call one of the men from our tent goes to the Commissaries and draws the ration for the tent. (one loaf of bread, one and a fourth pounds of beef or three fourths of a pound of pork or bacon per each man, and a certain amount of sugar and rice or hominy or potatoes and some tea. We also have coffee, but as it is green it is not served out to the company. We then set to work to get breakfast (except the Commissary (he tents with us) and those on guard duty.) Some get the water, another cooks the meat, another makes the tea, etc., etc., etc. We get along "first rate". At half past eight comes guard mounting. It would take up too much room to tell you how it is done. I will finish the synopsys of the day in another letter as I have not much more room.
>
> I am very glad to hear that you are to attend Mr. Partridges convention. Improve your time. Learn all you can. Now James, don't you never give up the idea of getting an education. Never — never, never. Don't be discouraged. You CAN do it if you have health. Now you must not be vexed at me for saying what I have, a little fellow like me. I feel very anxious that you should do well. If my life is spared, when I return won't I put in, eh?
>
> I understand from one of the letters that you could have had the position of Orderly Sergeant if you could have got five men. I was almost sorry at first that you did not accept of it, but on reflection I think you have done well. You now stand a chance of getting into West Point, whereas if you had enlisted that is forever done for besides to take three or even two years out of your life now, would make it useless to try to get an education. There I am talking like an old man, well, well, well, what next.
>
> Your affectionate Brother,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Alexander's long letter to James is one of the most compelling in the collection for family dynamics. The eldest enlisted brother passionately urging the younger to pursue education over enlistment — "Never — never, never... You CAN do it if you have health" — is prescient given the war's eventual toll. His acknowledgment of the Orderly Sergeant opportunity and his approval of James's refusal shows strategic thinking: West Point outweighs a non-commissioned rank. The detailed camp routine (reveille to breakfast) reads like a junior anthropologist's field report, prompted by Charles's request for "minute details." The lost knapsack narrative provides the explanation for the 25-day gap between Alexander's Nov 13 and Dec 8 letters. Alexander's self-description as "a little fellow" is the second height reference after the "tuck up Henry's overcoat" request.
