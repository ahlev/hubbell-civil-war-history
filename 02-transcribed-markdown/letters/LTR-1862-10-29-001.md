## Full Transcription

> Oct. 29th, 1862
> Champlain, N.Y.
>
> My darling precious pet:
>
> Yours of the 20th I recd. this morning. I congratulate you on your promotion. It is very gratifying to me to see that you merit the rank of Sergeant, for I take it for granted that you could not have it, did you not deserve it. Dear, dear Henry was never dealt justly with. He was acknowledged by all to be a brave and upright soldier. Capt. Rich — I will not write what came near slipping my pen. He is now Capt. of the Co. to which Charles belongs in the 153 Regt.
>
> I see B. Misner this morning and he has dashed any hopes with regard to Henry. He says that he and Albert are not incarcerated at all, and that they saw all the prisoners taken at Antietam, and Henry was not among them. Says if he was a
>
> [page break]
>
> prisoner he was left at some of their hospitals.
>
> O, I fear that he was one of the "unknown" buried, or one of the number found in the bushes, who had died of their wounds and could not be identified. They did not cross beyond the lines of the 34th and he might in trying to escape to some place of safety have gone over. By hope that he is living is a forlorn one, but still I cannot give him up. I must hope against his end that he may be a prisoner. My darling, darling Henry. How I long to write him, & receive letters from him. If there is a possibility of getting information from any source as to his being a prisoner, do not fail to do it. You may hear through returned prisoners. Pray for him, daily, as though we know he is a prisoner — peradventure he may be. Pray that he may be returned to us soon and that God will raise him up & find a sympathizing friends in his bondage, heal his wounds & restore him to health. My prayer is, if he is living that God "will be a very present help to him at this his time of trouble." He must have him in the hands of the "Father of the fatherless," trusting that He will fulfill his promise to him, and be more than an earthly father could be.
>
> The 153d Regt. left Fonda last Monday for North Carolina. I do not know when I shall hear from Charles. It saddens me sad, to have him so far away. My heart aches about Henry that I cannot endure, to have either of you any longer in the army. B. Misner talks very discouragingly about the war closing. Says there is not the least reliance to be placed upon the papers — the reporters are all liars! They write to produce sensation, not to keep up the spirits of the government, of the country rather. They told him in Richmond that they would fight to the bitter end! Surrender never!
>
> If the papers are to be believed, there is another great battle impending. O, I hope you will not be in it. If you are — do not go in without your name upon your person. You are in peril — and I trust you realize the importance of being ready, of always having your "lamp trimmed." You must consecrate yourself anew daily to the Saviour. "Watch and pray." Commit yourself into the hands of God, as this living or dying you may be the Lords. Let his word be the man you counsel and guide of your life. Resist temptation, resist the devil and he will flee from you. Be sure he has a minister in the army, if all I hear is true, and he has a tremendous influence too. His examples are not scholarly and may almost vie with the General. O how rapidly he is peopling his regions from the army. Let all who love Christ then unfurl His banners — the rules of prayer. He hears then as well as privately. For mercy. Be a Christian soldier.
>
> For my sake, take good care of your health. Had you not better get some competent person to measure your foot and send home for boots. Write what kind of ones — tell us just what you need — everything you need — and we will endeavor to send them. Do not suffer for anything.
>
> Ben is Capt. Ransom not Luther. I have not heard from James since I wrote you last. He commenced studying yesterday and has not ceased yet. A o'clock this afternoon it is dismal enough. Charles has sent home his bounty money to be let. He can get no one to take it nor my interest. There are such fearful times ahead that people are keeping their stocks very snug. The Rail road is a success by and by.
>
> Mrs. R. leaves next week and then we are alone. We still have little Joe stay here nights. If I could only hear from Henry, I could endure anything, every thing. Frances? friends in Longeurit[?] and Grand Ligne heard from Poussee[?] point that Henry was dangerously wounded. I am afraid he was wounded more seriously than was reported. There are several in the Regt. from Poussee "point." I can not learn that any one had spoke with him after he was wounded. You have not told me what your sensations were when you went into battle. God grant that you may never go into another.
>
> [page break]
>
> Kind regards to Capt. Ransom and Luther. Write me soon, very soon. God bless and preserve you from all evil.
>
> Your aff.
>
> Mother
>
> Clark Copeland is sick of the typhoid fever. It is prevailing here quite extensively. I expect to hear from Charles in the coming week. I will then write you again and send his address. Tom called here the last week. He thinks Charles is not robust enough to endure the hardships of the soldiers. He was discharged for not being able bodied. They must be in North Carolina by this time.

## Editorial Notes

This is the letter where hope dies. B. Misner walked into Champlain that morning and told Mother the truth: he saw all the prisoners, and Henry wasn't there. The prisoner theory — which Charles constructed, which Mother clung to, which even Alexander endorsed — is finished. Mother responds with a passage of extraordinary lucidity and grief: "I fear that he was one of the 'unknown' buried, or one of the number found in the bushes, who had died of their wounds and could not be identified." She is imagining Henry's body decomposing in the Maryland underbrush. And then she pulls back: "I must hope against his end."

The letter is also remarkable for what Mother nearly says about Capt. Rich. "I will not write what came near slipping my pen." This suppressed accusation will need tracking — was Rich negligent? Did he fail Henry in some way? The fact that Rich is now commanding Charles's company in the 153rd makes this personal.

The identification request — "do not go in without your name upon your person" — is devastating in its practicality. Mother has learned from Henry's disappearance that unidentified bodies are buried as unknown. She does not want Alexander to be another anonymous grave.
