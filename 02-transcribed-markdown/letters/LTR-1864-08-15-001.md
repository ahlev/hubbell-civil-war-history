## Full Transcription

> Aug. 15th, 1864
> Baltimore, Md.
>
> Dear Mother and Fannie:
>
> It is a long while since I have written you. I think my last letter was from Morganza. Since that time our regt. has seen considerable hardship. We left Louisiana the last of June for the North — this, I think Charlie has written you. As soon as we arrived at Washington D.C. we were sent out to the defenses and placed in the rifle pits. The next morning there was heavy skirmishing near us, but the Rebs retreated and we saw nothing of them. The next day we were sent as a reserve for the 6th Corps and followed the Rebs out to the Shenandoah Valley, the 6th Corps following close and skirmishing, but the Johnnies did not want to fight. We crossed a day and the next we crossed the river and lay in a belt of woods all day expecting a fight, but no, the enemy only wanted to get off with their plunder and keep us off, and they did it. At night we received orders to report at Washington within 24 hours. "Fall in!" ran along the lines and off we marched, many with light eyes and gloomy feelings — the prospect of marching all night does not lighten the feelings of many. Just as we were starting four men came in with a large dressed porker but it was goodly big, for a soldier cannot carry more than his usual load. Pig hitting had gone on pretty briskly that afternoon. Some were fortunate enough to get their pork for supper. Hardtack and coffee was all most of us had to eat. Do you know what hardtack is! Bake a flour and water cake with no salt or saleratus on a griddle till it is hard — throw it on a shelf for three or four days and see what kind of a meal it will make.
>
> We waded the river and with an occasional half hour rest marched until four the next day. I stood it until about eight the next morning and then the want of sleep and food, a blistered foot and fatigue compelled me to fall out. The wagon train soon came along. I put my gun and bundles on one and found I could get along. I kept with this train until we arrived at Chainbridge within the defenses of W. There we consumed. Orders were soon issued to prepare for field service; shoes and clothes were given out, and after a day's rest off we started. Where we went I did not know or care. After several days hard marching the two corps pitched camp about two miles beyond Harper's Ferry. Then came a counter march. Our Generals marched and counter-marched without seeming to come to any determination, the men in the meantime becoming worn out. At Frederick City I was taken to a hospital where I am now and have been two weeks. I was quite sick but am now pretty well and expect soon to go to a convalescent camp. My sickness was generally unwell and played out. The hospital is pleasantly situated on a hill overlooking the city. Papers are brought in every morning with war and city news. They do not apprehend a draft here — the wards are fast filling out their quotas. Milk is retailed at 16 cents a quart; peaches are selling at 80 cents a bushel. I had a little change and could buy fruit. We get tea four a cent here.
>
> There is a general feeling indignation expressed in these papers that our Generals should not have ascertained the actual force of the enemy and prevented them from coming so near our Capitol. They state that we had twenty thousand troops around Washington, part of them veterans, while the actual forces of the Rebels was 15,000, since ascertained. The fact that the army came up here with that force, harvested, plundered and got off with a wagon train three miles in length is rather irritating to us.
>
> About Charlie's health — he was quite thin and used up when I left; he finds the marches very hard. Charlie is one of the kind that'll go until he cannot go. I do not think that is the way. He remarked that if he was sick he would not have vital energy enough to recover. A good rest and something to eat would set him all right, I think. I hope he has been sent off. Charlie is pretty plucky and won't give up, but I think he ought to favor himself more.
>
> This paper was given me when we arrived at Washington from Louisiana. This I have carried since then which accounts for its being soiled. Our fare here is soft bread or pilot bread, meat twice a day, stewed apples at night.
>
> It seems to me that I should like to come up with the Johnnies and have a good hard fight with them instead of dogging them day and night and then having them get away marching and countermarching, accomplishing nothing, I do not like. What is the news in C? Will they be obliged to draft them in Sept.? Please write soon. Direct as before.
>
> James

## Editorial Notes

James's account of Early's Raid is the youngest brother's first real combat narrative — and it's a story of frustration, not glory. Sent from the heat of Louisiana to the defenses of Washington, placed in rifle pits, sent to chase Early through the Shenandoah Valley — and the rebels escaped with a three-mile wagon train of plunder. "Our Generals marched and counter-marched without seeming to come to any determination." The indignation is shared by the newspapers and the soldiers alike.

The hardtack definition is priceless — written for Mother and Frances who have never seen one: "Bake a flour and water cake with no salt or saleratus on a griddle till it is hard — throw it on a shelf for three or four days and see what kind of a meal it will make." The doughnut-maker of Morganza (LTR-1864-06-29-001) has graduated to hardtack.

"My sickness was generally unwell and played out" — James's laconic diagnosis. He was exhausted. The army wore him out with marching and counter-marching, and his body gave up at Frederick City. The hospital on the hill with its peaches and milk is a world away from the Shenandoah pursuit.
