## Full Transcription

> July 14th, 1862
> Camp on Pine Mountain
> Near Warrenton, Va.
>
> My Dear Sister:
>
> "Way down South in Dixie." That is where I am. And a beautiful country too if it were not cursed by the existence of slavery. I am encamped on the summit of a high hill or rather a mountain from which I can see a long distance and the view is very fine. Green fields, meadows, large farms, patches of woods, roads winding here and there, little villages in the distance, and all of this spread out before me like a beautiful painting.
>
> I was sitting on the hill just now watching the sun as it was setting behind the Blue Ridge. The soft notes of the bugle were floating through the air when I almost forgot I was a soldier. I seemed for a moment to be transported to the green fields and quiet scenes of old Clinton County. But then again I thought if the drum should beat to arms tomorrow morning, there will be a different scene. Troops will be forming in line of battle. The sun will be glistening on the bright steel of ten thousand breastplates. The artillery will be wheeled into position and the skirmishers will be thrown out, and then the earth will fairly rock.
>
> But enough of this. Frances, you have returned from Canada, and now I want to have a good long letter from you. I have been looking for one ever since I heard you were at home. I shall have to pull your ears when I come home if you do not write me a good letter soon.
>
> Now I want to tell you something about my plans. If the house can be sold and Uncle Henry's place purchased, I think it would be a good thing. When this war is over I want to go to college if possible. If not I shall go to farming. I must have some object in life and I am determined to make something of myself.
>
> The Signal Corps is encamped about fifty rods from us. Albert Cook is in it. I had a long talk with Lieut. Miner this evening. Please send me some postage stamps as they are hard to get.
>
> Evening. I have just had a cup of coffee with Cook. He is well and in good spirits. I received a letter from Mary Doolittle. Please direct your letters to Washington, D.C., Gen'l Banks Division.
>
> Your affectionate brother,
>
> Alexander F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

This is Alexander's most literary letter — and one of the most remarkable documents in the collection. The twenty-year-old corporal sits on a Virginia mountaintop watching the sunset behind the Blue Ridge, listening to a bugle, and writes a passage that moves from pastoral beauty to imagined apocalypse in a single paragraph. The "ten thousand breastplates" glistening in the sun, the earth that will "fairly rock" — this is a young man who reads, who sees things in terms of imagery and narrative. His post-war ambition for college ("I must have some object in life and I am determined to make something of myself") is poignant given that he left school at seventeen to enlist. The contrast with Henry's simultaneous letter from Harrison's Landing — sick, exhausted, calculating his bounty — could not be sharper. Alexander is dreaming on a mountaintop; Henry is surviving in a swamp. They are less than 100 miles apart.
