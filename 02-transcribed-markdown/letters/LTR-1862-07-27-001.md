## Full Transcription

> July 27th
> West Point Military Academy
>
> Dear Charles:
>
> I received your letter with the others and also a paper a few days since. I was feeling very anxious about Henry until I received your paper. A number of the Cadets take the Herald of which I can have the reading when ever I choose, so that I am enabled to keep myself pretty well posted on the movements of McClellan. I saw an account of the fight.
>
> I wish you would send me Henry's and Alex's address. Have you heard from them lately? What do they write? I should like to hear from you often, and in return I will write you of the things going on here, which I think will interest you. I will try and give you a little description of Camp McRae. It is situated on one corner of the plain and is entirely surrounded by shade trees, some of which are not more than three years growth. The camp is surrounded by six sentinels day and night. During the night the hours are called off by the sentinel. First the one at the guard tents gives the number of his post and the hour, the sentinel on the next post takes it up and adds "all is well" and so on in succession all around. The one at the guard tent adds finally "all is well". There are eighteen sentinels detailed for each day. Each sentinel has to walk two hours out of every six of the twenty-four. I have been on guard five times. You have no idea how the Cadets and even the officers of the guard would bother us. I think the latter, however, did it only to see if we understood our orders. Sometimes the Cadets would come out with 1/2 a dozen wheelbarrows, and sometimes with an old cannon. They would knock off our bayonets and get our guns away if they could. They dragged me around one night but could not get my gun away. They took one fellow one night (the most uncouth looking fellow I ever saw) and tied his hands behind him and marched him down through camp. He began to bawl and said "Oh please let me alone I don't know what to do. They thought him decidedly military, but I will go on with my description of the camp. The Corps is divided into four companies; each company has two Cadet Lieutenants and sergeant and two Corporals. The commissioned officers tents are in the rear of the encampment. The shoe black, barber and varnisher tents, back of the officers tents. We had to go down a flight of stone steps and through an underground passage to get to the sink. It is on the side of a hill overlooking the river. No one would ever suspect where it was. I never saw anything so nicely got up. It is kept very neat.
>
> You asked me if I took breakfast on the boat. I did. I forgot to mention it. Mr. Holcomb was aboard of the same boat. I did not know he was aboard until after I had (accidentally) sat down beside him at the table. He was going to New York. We kept together most of the way. Did you receive those clothes I sent? Please write soon.
>
> Your affectionate brother
>
> James
>
> Mr. Holcomb got on at Burlington and took a state room. That's the reason I did not see him before. I had a ticket given me for a berth when I got my passage ticket.

## Editorial Notes

James writes like a seventeen-year-old discovering a new world — everything gets described, everything is interesting. The hazing passage is remarkable: cadets attacking sentinels with wheelbarrows and an old cannon, knocking off bayonets, dragging James around (but he held his gun). The fellow who was tied up and bawled "Oh please let me alone I don't know what to do" — James reports this without judgment but clearly found it memorable. The underground passage to the latrine ("sink") overlooking the Hudson is exactly the kind of detail that makes these letters invaluable. James is tracking the war through the Herald; he saw "an account of the fight" (Seven Days) and was anxious about Henry. The communication network is clear: Mother and Charles are the hub, relaying news among the four brothers. James needs Henry's and Alexander's addresses — he can't write them directly without the relay.
