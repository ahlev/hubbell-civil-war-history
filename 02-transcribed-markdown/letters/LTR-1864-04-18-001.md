## Full Transcription

> April 18th, 1864
> Stevenson, Ala.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I rec'd. your letter of the 4th inst. last evening. I was amazed and vexed at your notion (Bonnett call it anything else) of James. He could not have enlisted, been sent to his reg't., drawn his arms, and been mounted in time to have participated in Kilpatrick's raid, and furthermore I very much doubt his enlisting in the cavalry. I think James is keen enough to look out for himself and do well. As soon as he gets settled he will doubtless write you. I think his chances of getting a commission of Gov. Seymour are rather slim. A person to get a commission has to be very strongly recommended by his commanding officers. He has doubtless enlisted and as soon as he reaches his regt. and corps, he will probably write. If James minds his P's and Q's he will surely rise in the service. I do not mean to stripes but straps. He would be a favorite with the men wherever he might be. You know he used to treat us well when we were little shavers. I hope James will go into the service with spunk to do well, and I believe he will. There are not many much better masters in these days than Uncle Sam. He is sure pay and cash at that.
>
> Am very, very much obliged to you for ordering those boots for me. They will be a great comfort to me. I trust we shall be paid soon. I shall be able, I think, to send home ninety dollars. I shall keep but little with me and if I should be in very great need of more I can send for it. I want to see that five hundred dollars all straight and you paid up. If I should ever come home again I should like at least a thousand dollars to start on.
>
> Now, Mother, I do not want you to be afraid to use my money. My greatest happiness is in seeing you comfortable. How does Frances flourish and is she preceptress of Champlain Academy? When I was home Mr. Bass very pompously told the boys that in Illinois they had strawberries in the middle of April. It is now the middle of April and I have as yet seen no strawberries and I do not think I am likely to in some time, though vegetation is quite forward here — the woods and fields are beginning to look quite green.
>
> I am glad that Mrs. B. is going to live with you in the old Stone Castle. You will not be so entirely alone. You will not have to wait for Frances to come home to disclose your "solemn conclusions" of which you generally have no lack. What have been the "conclusions" since the one of Kilpatrick's raid — or haven't you paper enough to write them? Shall have to employ Frances to record the most important of them for they amuse me vastly.
>
> Gen. Thomas and Hooker were around visiting the troops yesterday. Gen. Thomas is a tremendous large man — very much such a built man as Mr. Burroughs. A good part of his beard is snow white. Gen. Hooker is as fine a looking officer as you could wish to see.
>
> Ball is still the great game. The 78th N.Y. Vet. Vol. have challenged our regt. to a game of ball today and are now playing it out over at the 78th camping ground. We sometimes have grand dances in the company street. I participated in one the other night and had a gay time. As we have no ladies here some of the boys have to act instead. They are distinguished from the gents by having their caps off.
>
> Six new regts. from Indiana passed by here this morning. They had marched from Nashville and looked as if they had passed through a campaign — being poor and haggard — worn out, I might say. They left large numbers on the way unable to march. They will get used to marching after a while. I pitied the poor fellows. Few men at the north have any idea of what a soldier has to endure.
>
> I have no postage stamps and do not know what I shall do to send off this letter. Please send me some as soon as possible and please send some two cent stamps as I should like to send a paper to some of my friends occasionally. Tell Frances if she don't write pretty soon I shall pull her ears. If you have my furlough please send it on. If Marshall Dunn has it it will be sent on soon as the Orderly has written him to send all the furloughs he has immediately. Perhaps Mr. Averill has it. If he has tell him to get it and send it to me! Why doesn't he get my county bounty? I believe they are paying it in county bonds. Please write soon. Love to all.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
>
> April 19th — I commenced writing this letter some days since but have just been able to borrow a stamp to send it off. I now owe several stamps. I hope you will send me some soon. There is nothing of interest here to write. Everything is going on here about as usual.

## Editorial Notes

Three brothers now serve. Alexander assesses James with the eye of a veteran: doubts cavalry, thinks he'll rise to officer rank, compares James's childhood kindness to leadership potential — "he used to treat us well when we were little shavers." The elder brother's confidence in the younger.

Thomas and Hooker visiting the troops before the Atlanta Campaign is a moment of military pageantry — "Thomas is a tremendous large man, beard snow white. Hooker is as fine a looking officer as you could wish to see." These are the men who will lead them into Georgia.

The grand dances with boys playing ladies — "distinguished from the gents by having their caps off" — is the army's improvisational social life. The Indiana regiments passing through "poor and haggard" are a preview of what marching will do to men. "Few men at the north have any idea of what a soldier has to endure."

"If I should ever come home again I should like at least a thousand dollars to start on." The conditional — "if" — carries the weight of a man who has seen Gettysburg, Wauhatchie, and Lookout Mountain, and knows what's coming next.
