## Full Transcription

> June 17, 1862
> Onikers Ferry
>
> My Dear Mother:
>
> I received your very, very, welcome letter last evening. It did me good to hear from you — real substantial good. I have not heard from you in a long long time. The last letter I received from you was at Harpers Ferry a day or tow before we left.
>
> June 18th: I left off writing yesterday and went out on a pass with a squad of "the boys after cherries, and and — fresh or smoked meat. In other words, we went out after plunder. We have the privilege of taking whatever we please outside of a mile from the camp unless the proprietor has a written warrant from the commanding officer certifying that he is a good union man and that his property must not be touched.
>
> Well now about my expedition. We went out to a very flourishing looking place some three miles from Camp and very innocently inquired of a little darkie where their cherries were. "Out in the garden" he replied. To the garden we went and found two immense trees bending under their weight of splendid ripe cherries. Up we went and eat until we could eat no more and then had succeeded in making no perceptable impression on the trees. We then went to the smoke house, but as there was little meat there we took none. We went next to the hen house and bagged a half dozen fowls and then went to the pasture where we filled our canteens with milk and dressed our fowls. The sun was getting low so we started for home. We had started with the intention of bringing home a sheep, but as we came across none on our way back we contented ourselves with what we had got. However, some of "the boys" a little after brought in a sheep and a pig. We made an excellent breakfast off our chickens this morning.
>
> Now, dear Mother, are you not horrified to think you have a hen roost robbing son; are you not shocked. Dont believe you are for I know your good sense will come to your aid. All the provision brought to the large army under Banks is brought on wagons and you must know it is no easy thing to do this. We are now out on detached service and should suffer if we were not allowed to do this. And is it not right that those who precipitated war upon our country should be made to suffer for it.
>
> The day before we left Winchester I went out foraging with a train (of baggage wagons) under command of a Lieut. We took a large quantity of corn, some ham and oats for the horses and from fifty to sixty bushels of wheat. I suppose you would like to know how we came here. A company from each of several regts. in our brigade were detailed to come here, also quite a strong force of cavalry. The
>
> whole commanded by a Major of the Cavalry. We started about dark and reached here in the morning, a distance of about eighteen miles. We are here to prevent the rebels from crossing this ferry and flanking it. We, I understand, to be relieved soon by the cavalry. This ferry is on the Shenandoah River.
>
> I gave you in my former letters a very imperfect account of our doings at Harpers Ferry. When we crossed the river we advanced to Bolivar Heights and formed our line of battle upon it. The right wing was supported by a battery of six guns (rifled) and the left by one of four. A battery supported by infantry was sent out to reconnoitre. They returned towards night. I understand they had quite a brisk time with the rebels. On the third day after our arrival the rebels opened fire on us with their artillery. I was out on picket at the time. The pickets were called in and we marched back to camp. (We did not run). One of the rebel shots struck within about ten rods of us. I suppose they sent that to show us their good will. The rebels did no harm. Their shot all stuck too low. That night we were aroused up about twelve and ordered to fall into line with all our accoutrements on. We soon had the order to "forward march" and took the direction back to the river. I was afraid we were on the retreat and felt as mad as a hornet. We halted a short distance from the ferry and formed our line of battle.
>
> Our regt. was on the extreme right and supported one of the batteries. The next day towards night I went out with a squad back to the Heights to get some knapsacks left there by the pickets. We had not been gone from the hill more than a half or three quarters of an hour when the rebels appeared on the hill. We only went to the foot of the hill. If we had gone on top we should probably have had a few rebel pills thrown at us.
>
> During the forenoon of that day one gun supported by (I believe) one hundred and twenty five sharp shooters from our Regt. went on the hill and had a very brisk time with the rebels. They had a very lively time and some narrow escapes — dodging rebel shells. One shell struck the wheel of the cannon and damaged it considerably. That night the rebels began planting a battery of eleven guns on the hill. Our batteries poured a perfect tempest of shot and shell upon them. They "skedaddled" in fine stile and, I understand, with considerable loss. That night I was roused up by the simultaneous discharge of two of our cannon. I gave my blanket a kick and tumbled out in short order. We were in line ready for the rebels in "no time". Several volleys were fired on the left and I thought for certain that we were to have a good battle, but no, we waited in vain. I have since learned (whether correctly or not I do not know) that the rebel skirmishers were feeling their way along, and were almost on our lines. There was scarce a night but what we were called into line — perhaps several times, sometimes all night. We were often obliged to stay in line nearly all night in a drenching rain. It would do me good to ring Silas and Sids neck, it would. It is quite well that you have sold the old cow. They have been harping about her for the last five or six years.
>
> (Remainder of letter missing) From, Alexander

## Editorial Notes

Alexander's foraging letter is the most vivid domestic-scale scene in the collection — climbing cherry trees, raiding hen houses, filling canteens with milk, dressing chickens by a fence. His playful challenge to Mother ("are you not horrified?") is followed by a sophisticated justification: supply difficulty plus moral argument that secessionists deserve the loss. The foraging rules he describes — anything outside a mile unless the owner has a written warrant of Union loyalty — show the practical mechanism of occupation. This is war's transformation of a Champlain farm boy into a forager: the same young man who tracked every 3¢ loan is now systematically stripping Virginia farms. "Onikers Ferry" defies easy identification — may be a phonetic rendering of a local place name in the Shenandoah. Letter continues on page 6.
