## Full Transcription

> Feb. 24th, 1862
> Camp McClellan
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received a letter from you two or three days ago. Was very glad to hear that you were all well. The reason I have not answered before I thought I would wait and know what news there is to write. The probability is now that we shall make a move before another week goes by or transpires. Orders were read on dress parade last night to have our knapsacks packed and ready to leave at a moments notice. What clothes we could not carry, we are to send to Poolsville in a box. I have mine marked. When we go into camp again we can have them. I can not carry all of the clothes I have now.
>
> The roads are very bad now. We may not move this week, and yet we may. I was over to meeting last night. We had speaking. The text was the Duty of Patriotism. Very good. The Chaplain in his remarks said he thought we would not be here another Sabbath, but that we can not tell for certain. Feris left for the fleet week ago today. He is probably sailing before this time. He got into a fight with some of the boys and was broke from his office. He felt so bad that he left, with the Capt. permission. If he had staid he would have been court martialed and it would have went hard with him.
>
> We came off from picket yesterday, was on reserve, heavy firing has been going on across the river lately. It is raining today. Colonel Ladue has returned into our Regt. again. I heard that he has been promoted to Brigadier Gen. Nothing more this time. I may write again before we move. Do not worry about me, I have good health now.
>
> I remain your aff. Son,
>
> Henry

## Editorial Notes

This is a pivotal letter for the dating discrepancy resolution. Henry writes from Camp McClellan on Feb 24 saying orders were read at dress parade to have knapsacks packed. The Chaplain said they wouldn't be there another Sabbath. Two days later (Feb 26, per the Feb 28 letter), they marched. The "Duty of Patriotism" text at meeting shows the chaplain preparing men spiritually for campaign. Feris's story — broke from office after a fight, left for the fleet rather than face court martial — is a vivid example of how discipline problems were sometimes resolved by letting men leave rather than prosecuting.
