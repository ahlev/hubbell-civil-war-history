## Full Transcription

> **[Editorial header — NOT part of original letter; added by later family member, likely Fred Hubbell Jr. ca. 1996:]**
>
> *Letter to Mrs. Hubbell… from R.W. McDonald, a tent mate of Henry Hubbell. Letter was sent about 2 weeks after the Battle of Antietam. R.W. McDonald had seen a letter arrive for Henry from Champlain, and he was passing along what little he knew about Henry, who he thought was wounded. It was learned later that Henry did not survive.*

> ..... 30 .....
>
> Oct. 1, 1862
> In camp on Bollever Heights
>
> Misses Hubbell:
>
> Madam yesterday there came a letter to the company fr. Henry and I seen the postmark on it Champalin. I took the letter in charge and I will Forward it to Henry when I learn to what hospital he went. I did not see him after he was wounded but made all the enquiries about him I could, as he and I had tented together for some short time before the day of the battle and slept together the night before. I find upon all the matters I can obtain in relation to him that he was wounded in the leg / a flesh wound/ and not very badly. He had been ailing for a few days before with his bowels and did not feel first rate. We all hope that he is properly cared
>
> (Contd.)
>
> Continuation of Oct. 1, 1862
>
> for. Doctor Thurman went back from this place to see to the wounded and expect that he will return in a few days, and I will see him on his arrival and obtain all the facts from him and write you the true state of the case. I had thought that Henry would have written home immediately, so that you would have known where to direct your letters.
>
> The 60th Regt. are on Loudon Heights about 2 or 3 miles from here. I seen ~~inRe~~ LaBlue of Capt. Ransoms Company a few days ago and sent his brother word of what had happened. I thought he would have come here to see me, but he had not come. No doubt but he would have liked to have come but perhaps he could not as it might conflict with his duty and also contrary to orders, as it is necessary at this point to keep the members of each Regt. as much together as possible, as we are only but a short distance, 7½ miles, from the Headquarters of Stonewall Jackson.
>
> You may think it very strange and almost say neglectful that we know so little of your son Henry, but on due reflection when you come to take into account so many men congregated together as was on the very Memorable Day of the 17th ultimo and part in the woods, part in the clearance with the ever and constant explosion of shell, the noise of ~~xx~~ musketry, that of all sizes and the sharp crack and whizz of the Rifle ball falling as thick as hail, that it is utterly impossible to know about everyone that falls or is wounded. It is only after all is over when we call the roll of the Company that we can tell anything about them at all, unless perchance what comes under your own immediate observation. When a man gets wounded in action he falls to the rear and takes himself away to where he can take the best care of himself he can, and goes to the first ambulance Doctor or Hospital he can find. I therefore think that Henry went to Frederick, as a great many went directly there.
>
> Having now said all that I can as a neighbor sympathizing with you all in your deep distress and hoping you will pardon me for such intrusion. I remain your obedient servant -
>
> ~~xxx~~ R. W. McDonald
> ..... 30 .....

## Significance Flags

### Historical Significance: `major`

> **THE ONLY OUTSIDE VOICE IN THE COLLECTION.** R.W. McDonald, Henry's tentmate in the 34th NY, writes Mother Hubbell to report what he knows of Henry after Antietam. Henry has not written. McDonald has not seen him since the battle. He reports Henry wounded in the leg — a flesh wound, "not very badly" — contradicting Alexander's Sept 27 report of a hand wound. McDonald's vivid description of Antietam itself ("shell… musketry… the sharp crack and whizz of the Rifle ball falling as thick as hail") is the most detailed combat account in the collection so far. He believes Henry went to Frederick for hospital care. The editorial header — added decades later — delivers the devastating verdict: "It was learned later that Henry did not survive."

### Emotional Intensity: `high`

> McDonald writes as a stranger reaching across to a mother in distress. His tone is formal, almost courtly ("hoping you will pardon me for such intrusion"), but the substance is agonizing: he cannot tell her where her son is, he cannot confirm he is alive, and he knows she knows this. His explanation of WHY they don't know — the chaos, the noise, the impossibility of tracking one man in the carnage — is both an apology and an eyewitness account.

### Connections to Other Letters in Collection

- **CRITICAL DISCREPANCY — HENRY'S WOUND:** Alexander (LTR-1862-09-27-001, Sept 27): "slightly wounded in the hand, but still with the company." McDonald (this letter, Oct 1): "wounded in the leg / a flesh wound/ and not very badly" and Henry has LEFT the company — McDonald doesn't know where he is. These are irreconcilable secondhand reports. Neither man saw Henry after the battle. One or both are wrong, or Henry had multiple wounds (hand AND leg). Alexander's information came through the regiment grapevine at the 60th NY hospital; McDonald's came from "all the enquiries about him I could" within the 34th NY company. McDonald's account — from Henry's own company and tentmate — may be more reliable, but both are `secondhand`.
- **CRITICAL — HENRY'S SILENCE:** McDonald expected Henry to have written home "immediately." He hasn't. Mother hasn't heard from Henry directly. McDonald is intercepting her letters to Henry. This silence is ominous — it contradicts Alexander's report that Henry is "still with the company."
- **LaBlue and the 60th NY connection:** McDonald saw "LaBlue of Capt. Ransoms Company" (60th NY) and "sent his brother word of what had happened." This is the communication channel between the two regiments — someone from the 60th told Alexander about Henry. The secondhand nature of Alexander's information is now explained: he heard it through a chain (McDonald → LaBlue → LaBlue's brother → Alexander, or similar).
- **Henry's bowel illness:** McDonald confirms Henry "had been ailing for a few days before with his bowels." This matches the chronic diarrhea pattern — Henry has suffered from this since at least June 1862 (LTR-1862-06-22-001). He went into the Battle of Antietam already weakened.
- **The editorial header:** "It was learned later that Henry did not survive." This is the first explicit statement of Henry's death in any document in the collection. The header was added by a later family member (likely Fred Hubbell Jr., ca. 1996). The letters themselves have not yet revealed this — we are reading forward through time, and as of October 1, 1862, Henry is missing and wounded. The family annotation breaks the temporal frame to deliver the verdict.
- **Chronological position:** This letter falls between Alexander's hospital letter (Sept 27, LTR-1862-09-27-001) and whatever comes next on page 21 of the 1862 JUNE-DEC PDF. It is the only letter in the collection from outside the Hubbell family.

### Open Questions
- **Where is Henry?** McDonald thinks Frederick. Alexander thinks still with the company. Neither has seen him. The truth is unknown as of this letter.
- **When and how did Henry die?** The editorial header states he "did not survive" but gives no date or cause. Was it the Antietam wound? Illness compounded by the wound? A hospital death? Future letters should resolve this.
- **Who is R.W. McDonald?** A "neighbor" who sympathizes — does this mean he is from Champlain or the surrounding area? He is Henry's tentmate, suggesting a personal connection predating the war or formed in service.
- **Who is LaBlue?** A member of the 60th NY (Capt. Ransom's Company) who has a "brother" — is this brother Alexander, or another Hubbell, or someone else? The phrasing "sent his brother word" suggests LaBlue sent word to LaBlue's own brother, not to Alexander. But the information clearly reached Alexander somehow.
- **Who is Doctor Thurman?** Regimental surgeon? Contract physician? He "went back from this place to see to the wounded" — suggests he traveled from Bolivar Heights back toward the Antietam battlefield or hospitals.

## Editorial Notes

This is the most extraordinary document in the collection so far — and the most heartbreaking when read with the editorial header's foreknowledge. R.W. McDonald is a stranger writing to a mother he has never met, and he knows it. His formality ("Madam," "hoping you will pardon me for such intrusion," "I remain your obedient servant") barely contains the gravity of what he's trying to say: *I don't know where your son is.*

The Antietam passage is the finest combat writing in the collection. McDonald doesn't describe what he did — he describes what it was like to be inside it: "the ever and constant explosion of shell, the noise of musketry, that of all sizes and the sharp crack and whizz of the Rifle ball falling as thick as hail." And then the devastating logic: "it is utterly impossible to know about everyone that falls or is wounded. It is only after all is over when we call the roll of the Company that we can tell anything about them at all." This is how families learned their sons were gone — by the absence of a name at roll call.

The wound discrepancy (leg vs. hand) is significant. McDonald, as Henry's tentmate and company-mate, had direct access to the 34th NY's information network. Alexander, hospitalized with the 60th NY at Harpers Ferry, got his information through intermediaries (possibly through the LaBlue connection McDonald describes). McDonald's account is likely closer to the truth, but he is also explicit that he did NOT see Henry after the battle.

The "..... 30 ....." notation at top and bottom is a 19th-century convention for "end of transmission," borrowed from telegraph and newspaper practice. Its use here suggests McDonald may have had newspaper or telegraph experience, or that the transcriber added it as a document separator.

The letter's position in the collection is unique: it is the only voice from outside the Hubbell family, and it arrives at the exact moment when the family narrative is fracturing. Henry is missing. Alexander is hospitalized. James is at West Point. Charles has just enlisted. And a stranger named McDonald is holding Mother's letter to Henry, unable to deliver it.
