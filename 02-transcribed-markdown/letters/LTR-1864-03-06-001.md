## Full Transcription

> March 6, 1864
> Department of the Gulf
> New Orleans, La.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I came to this place March 3d, which is Gen'l. Banks Head Quarters. On the afternoon of the 2nd the Col sent for me and asked whether I would like to go to N. Orleans as clerk. After looking at my handwriting he asked me if I thought my Capt. would give his consent. I told him I thought he would. He told me to get my Capt. to send in a request to him to detail me and he would do it. I asked him, but he gave me no definite answer. I was then afraid he would not do it. The next morning Lieut. Col. Strain came to me and told me to get ready to go. He said my Capt. wanted A. Douglass to go, but he told him he could not spare so healthy a man. Lieut. Col. Strain told me that you had written the Surgeon and that his (Strain's) wife had written to him several times. That he had been waiting for an opportunity to get me a situation. He is a very pleasant man and you must thank Mrs. Strain for her influence.
>
> Now, Mother, I am much obliged to you, for you are at the bottom of this matter. It is a pleasant place here, a good deal better than being in the field. We are in the third story, where we can get the fresh cool air. There are a good many clerks here and we all mess together. It costs about three dollars a mo. for each of us to buy extras, such as butter, potatoes, etc. One of the older clerks acts as caterer for the mess, that is, draws our gov't. rations and buys the extras. The clerks draw extra pay, but I don't get know how much. About eight clerks are going in a day or two into the field along with the Gen'l.
>
> Our Regt. left Algiers for Franklin, La. the same day that I came here. I took the cars for that place. I think it will see some hard marching before long. The Col. asked me about my health. I told him it was pretty good. He said he meant if I was able to stand a long march. I told him no.
>
> I have received no letters since we left Wash. and there was not one due me at that time. Should like to hear how you all get along. Lieut. Col. Strain told me that I was detached from the regt. — a permanent detachment, — so I suppose that I will not go to the regt. again.
>
> Mch. 11th — A mail steamship leaves here this morning so I will finish this letter. I might have sent this sooner, but was waiting to hear from you. I have rec'd no detail from Head Quarters yet, so am not sure of staying here, but I guess I will stay. My writing is not as good as the other clerks, but I have to improve. My duty is to copy special orders, but I have not done much yet at it yet. If the smallest mistake in spelling or anything else is made, it will not answer but a new copy must be made. I wish I understood punctuation, but I will learn, I suppose. Wish you would correct my bad spelling.
>
> The 153rd is going to Texas, I Believe. The extra pay of the clerks is 40¢ a day which will make my pay about $25 a mo. The 4th of Mch. was a great holiday here when a free state Governor was inaugurated. You must have seen an account of it. 5000 school children sat on the seats and sang together. There was plenty of music and plenty of cannon firing, also musketry firing. In the evening fireworks, music, etc. Two days since the rain poured down and the streets were fairly flooded with water. Please write soon. Love to all.
>
> From your aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

The irony is exquisite: Charles, who has spent months urging Alexander to improve his penmanship, is now a clerk whose "writing is not as good as the other clerks" and who wishes he "understood punctuation." The very skill he preached to his brother is now his professional requirement.

Mother's behind-the-scenes campaign finally bears fruit. She wrote to the Surgeon; Mrs. Strain wrote to her husband; Col. Strain arranged the detail. "You are at the bottom of this matter." The Captain wanted to send the healthy A. Douglass instead, but Strain told him "he could not spare so healthy a man" — the delicate fiction that justified keeping the less robust Charles in a desk job. Mother's network of advocacy, operating through officers' wives and regimental surgeons, achieved what direct appeals to the army could not.

The Free State Governor inauguration — Michael Hahn, elected under Lincoln's 10% plan — is Reconstruction beginning while the war still rages. Charles watches 5,000 school children sing while his regiment marches toward the Red River Campaign.
