## Full Transcription

> May 6, 1864
> Camp somewhere in the
> State of Georgia
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I now hasten to write you a few lines to let you know of my welfare. This is my first opportunity of writing you since I left Stevenson. We have been marching hard — very hard for the last few days.
>
> I rec'd the boots when I reached Bridgeport, Ala. I was very much pleased with them, but most unfortunately they proved a little too small for me (as my used up feet will testify) and I have sold them to yours Lafountain from Champlain for $6.50. I have now an excellent pair of Govt. bootees and I find them much better for marching than boots.
>
> The sugar was most excellent and you can well imagine how much I enjoyed it. I am very much obliged to you for sending it. The stripes are very nice indeed.
>
> We are close upon the enemy. Our advance came upon them this morning but they fell back. I hardly think they are in much force. We have between thirty-five and forty thousand men here under Hooker. We are I believe on the extreme right of the army.
>
> I will write you as often as possible. Culver Bostwick is here. He reached the regt. yesterday morning and he wishes me to tell his friends that he would write with me but that he has drawn a new gun and is too busy cleaning it. The mail is going out in a moment and I must close.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
>
> P.S. Keep a stout heart.

## Editorial Notes

"Camp somewhere in the State of Georgia." The vagueness is deliberate — operational security and uncertainty combine. The expansive camp letters from Stevenson are over. This is a campaign letter — hurried, essential, stripped to facts. Boots, sugar, stripes, enemy contact, "keep a stout heart." The postscript is the message.

The Atlanta Campaign begins with rebel forces falling back — the opening of what will become a 100-day series of flanking movements, battles, and sieges from Dalton to Atlanta. Alexander estimates Hooker's force at 35-40,000. He doesn't know yet that the next four months will be the defining experience of his war.
