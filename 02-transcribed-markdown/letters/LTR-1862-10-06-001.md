## Full Transcription

> Oct 6th, 1862
> Champlain, N.Y.
>
> My darling pet,
>
> As Mr. Ransom leaves this evening I will inpose the opportunity in writing you a few lines, although it is only two days since I mailed a letter to you. Frances and I are at our "wits end" to think what "token of remembrance" to send you and we can think of nothing that would be acceptable to you. Gen. Halleck says it is no favor to a soldier to increase the weight of his knapsack. I know it. I can think of "lots" of things I would like to send you — a jar of preserves, a box of candy, etc., but I am told they cannot be taken. How I wish you had written what to send you, my dear pet.
>
> [page break]
>
> Continuation — Oct. 6th, 1862
>
> I have shed an ocean of tears over dear Henry's fate — I know not what it is — but there is something. Yesterday I was half deranged from young Fiser's report. He says, "Henry was seen to fall, that the rebels were within two rods of them, they fled, and know nothing more of him." Mr. Copeland says that he read a letter dat. from Henry and "some Corporal in which they say that they searched the field for Henry and saw only of Alexander[?] their Regt. who was put into the pit, and Henry was not among them. That comforts me. Mr. Copeland thinks he is a prisoner. B. Misner, I see is a prisoner in Richmond. Mr. Cook is very anxious about Albert. Has not heard from him for more than a month, fears he was shot in the drive at Shepherdsburg. Hopes he may hear something about him through you.
>
> What have you done towards ascertaining Henry's fate? There are so many conflicting reports. I shall mail a letter today to Col. Safin[?] of the 34th hoping I may learn something definite. You must write me often. It is so desolate. All the comfort I have is in hearing from you. Do let it be often. I want your description of the battles. I have seen several of the Canadian letters describing it. Therefore, I have had a description of the battles Henry has been engaged in — from his pen. O, how I miss his letters. He wrote me faithfully. Tell me what were your sensations during that terrible battle, how long you were engaged — and all about it.
>
> I have been out riding today. Tony is just as fat as he can be. I do not know how he will fare this winter under the little French boys care. How I wish you could be home to go to school this winter and attend to pony. He would be glad to see you, and I know you would him.
>
> Take good care of yourself. Do not expose yourself easily in battle. O, these terrible battles — when will the end come. God grant that it may be over — very soon. You must live near God in these perilous times — put your whole trust in Him. He alone, is able to protect you. "Read the 91st Psalm daily — and live up to its promises. The Saviour is near, near you — and can hear the hearts faintest sigh. In the din of battle no artillery can drown that sigh if it is breathed in faith. He will be near you to cheer you — to strengthen & support, guide and direct you. "It is better to trust in Him than to put confidence in man." Trust in Him, at all times.
>
> I am going down to Mr. Bummings to leave this letter. Mr. Ransom has gone to Chazy today. Made us a short call this morning. I am glad he is going back on your acct. Mrs. R. will leave the first of Nov. Mr. C, how lonely I shall be. How I wish the war was over and you home once more. I hope the day is not far distant.
>
> I pray God to preserve your life — to "cover your head in the day of battle" and restore you to us soon. Write, write, write, write, write, write, write, WRITE.
>
> Your affectionate & desolate
>
> Mother

> **NEW EYEWITNESS REPORT ON HENRY.** Young Fiser says: "Henry was seen to fall, that the rebels were within two rods of them, they fled, and know nothing more of him." ## Editorial Notes

This letter oscillates between two registers: the domestic and the apocalyptic. Mother is simultaneously trying to choose between preserves and candy for Alexander and processing the report that Henry was seen falling with rebels thirty-three feet away. The pony Tony is fat. Henry may be dead. Gen. Halleck says don't overload the knapsack. "I was half deranged."

The Fiser report is the most vivid combat detail connected to Henry in the entire collection: "seen to fall, rebels within two rods, they fled, know nothing more." This is the West Woods at Antietam in one sentence — the sudden flanking attack, the panic, the flight, the men left behind. Henry fell and his comrades ran. No one looked back. Two weeks later, the field has been searched and the dead buried, and Henry is not among them. This is the evidence Mother clings to.

The 91st Psalm recommendation is significant: this was THE Civil War psalm, the one soldiers carried into battle. Mother is not offering abstract comfort — she is prescribing a specific devotional practice that soldiers across both armies used. "In the din of battle no artillery can drown that sigh" — she is imagining Alexander under fire and telling him God can hear him even there.

The closing is one of the most memorable lines in the collection. "Write, write, write, write, write, write, write, WRITE." Seven lowercase repetitions building to one uppercase command. It is funny and desperate and it is a mother trying to hold her family together with ink and postage stamps while everything falls apart.
