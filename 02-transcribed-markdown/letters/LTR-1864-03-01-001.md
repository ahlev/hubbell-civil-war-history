## Full Transcription

> March 1, 1864
> Algiers, La.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> Here we are just across the river close to New Orleans. We left Washington Feb. 18th for Alexandria and stayed there in the Soldier's Rest till the 20th when we went aboard the steamship Mississippi bound for New Orleans. We reached N.O. about sundown, Feb. 28. On the morning of the 4th day about daylight we entered the mouth of the Mississippi River. It was Sunday morning and I passed a pleasant Sabbath on deck of the vessel looking at the beautiful sugar plantations all along both sides of the river. The buildings on a plantation made quite a village. The negro quarters were nice looking small buildings all in rows. We passed orange groves and at one place the darkies threw some oranges at the boat but they did not reach it.
>
> It is a beautiful country along the river but perfectly flat. When we arrived at N.O. we did not land that night but anchored in the river till morning when all the recruits from N. York were landed in N.O., but our regt. was brought to this side of the river which is called Algiers. About 70 of our regt. were detailed to guard the new recruits to a place in the city of N.O. I was part of this guard so had a chance to see N.O. It is a fine city and but little business is now carried on there. The cotton presses (and there are a great many of them) are idle and empty and are used to quarter soldiers in.
>
> Our regt. is quartered in a very large brick building formerly used as an iron works. There is no floor, but the ground, and the windows are nearly all smashed but it will do for warm weather. There is a great change in the weather since yesterday when we arrived here. This morning it is cold and rainy, but yesterday it was quite warm. We do not expect to stay here long, perhaps a month or so. I hear that there is an expedition fitting out for Mobile. I don't know whether we will go there or to Texas. I think we will have to rough it now but that was to be expected. Each man brought his half or a shelter tent from Washington. Two men put their half tents together and slept together. We were glad when our voyage was over. We slept in bunks in the hold, four or five in a bunk. As we slept so close we did not suffer from the cold. We had some rough weather but not a great deal of seasickness. I was not sea sick. Part of the way we used the sails. Our meat and coffee were cooked by steam, also the bean soup. The noon and the meat were boiled in a large barrel which had a steam pipe running down on one side and across the bottom. The steam made the water boil furiously. Coffee was boiled in a large iron tank by steam. We earned our meals, for although we fell in in a long string it was squeeze and mash. Starting on one side we marched around by the coffee tank, thence by the meat board for our meat and hard tack. I got along very well eating hardtack, but it takes me a good deal longer than the rest unless I soak the .
>
> Direct your letters to N.O. The Captain says that is the right way to direct. I am expecting letters from you every day. It is a long time since I heard from you. Should like to hear about James and Alex. Hope you will write often. My health is good. Oranges are in great plenty here at 6¢ each. Apples sold for 10¢ on Sabbath so were we and other things in proportion. Tell the boys to write to me and I will write to them. I still close by hoping to hear that you are all well and that you are taking the world easy.
>
> From your aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Charles's description of the Mississippi River journey is his best travel writing — sugar plantations, orange groves, darkies throwing oranges at the boat, the flat country. His eye for detail serves him well: the cotton presses "idle and empty," the iron works with no floor and smashed windows, the steam-cooked meals fed to soldiers in a long shuffling line. The ship's galley — a barrel with a steam pipe for boiling meat, an iron tank for coffee — is industrial-age military logistics.
