## Full Transcription

> Nov. 1st, 1863
> White Side, Georgia
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> Your letter of Oct. 21st rec'd. yesterday. It was the right thing in the right place for I was feeling particularly lonely. We are now holding a pass in the Cumberland Mountains about fifteen miles below Chattanooga. Our coming into this Gap prevented us from being in a fight. The rest of the brigade pushed on and camped about four miles from here. About one or two o'clock (A.M.), the rebels made a furious assault upon the camp, following so closely upon the pickets that the camp was barely alarmed before they were upon them and poured in a withering volley. Reports are so conflicting that I will wait until I can get an authentic acct. which I will give you in my next letter. Suffice it to say the rebs were defeated. Gen'l. George S. Greene, our Brigade commander, was seriously if not mortally wounded. Lieut. Geary, son of Gen'l. Geary, our Division commander, is reported killed and the Gen. himself slightly wounded in the toe.
>
> The rebs yesterday shelled one of our wagon trains, but their practice was bad and did no damage. The other night our pickets were drawn in a short distance, leaving a large fire burning upon their old posts. They had not been gone long before a squad of rebels cavalry dashed upon the fire with loud yells and sabres drawn, expecting, I suppose, to capture the post. Our boys fired upon them when they wheeled about and skedaddled. The pickets have not been molested since.
>
> You will be surprised, I expect, when I tell you I have been in the state of Alabama. Wonder what Aunt Wry would say if she knew my vandal footsteps had pressed the soil of Alabama. Do you suppose she pities us as much as she used to?
>
> You asked me to give you my views fully in regard to a discharge. It would be useless. There is no one, I think, but the chief magistrate of the U.S. has power to grant it. I am anxious to pursue my studies — very anxious — but — well, I don't think you can get a discharge and I will say no more. If you get me one I shall not demur, though it will seem hard to leave the rest of the boys to fight it alone.
>
> Tell Frances to write soon. I want to hear about the books. Tell her I know something about them. I am even with her now. I will write again soon if possible. Love to all.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Alexander
>
> Sid's wife and I will agree very well, I think, for we both think the same of Mrs. Ruth Hubbell.

## Cross-Reference Notes

### Connections to Other Letters in Collection
- **Gen. Greene wounded:** The same George Sears Greene who held Culp's Hill at Gettysburg and commanded the Murfreesboro garrison (LTR-1863-10-11-001) is now seriously wounded at Wauhatchie. He survived but never returned to field command — a devastating loss for the brigade.
- **Discharge discussion:** Mother has been trying to get Alexander discharged. His response is ambivalent: anxious to study, but "it will seem hard to leave the rest of the boys to fight it alone." Pride and duty hold him in the ranks.
- **White Side, Georgia:** The location heading says Georgia, but White Side (Whiteside) is actually in Tennessee, near the Tennessee-Georgia-Alabama border. The confusion is understandable given the mountainous terrain.

## Editorial Notes

The Battle of Wauhatchie was a rare night engagement — Confederate forces attacking Union positions near Chattanooga at 1-2 AM. Alexander was in a gap position that kept him out of the main fight, but his brigade took serious casualties. Gen. Greene's wounding removes one of the war's most capable brigade commanders from the field permanently.

The discharge discussion is revealing. Mother wants Alexander out; he wants to study; but "it will seem hard to leave the rest of the boys to fight it alone." This is the central tension of his service — intellect and ambition pulling him toward civilian life, while loyalty and duty keep him in the army. The President is the only one who can discharge him, and Alexander knows it won't happen.

The rebel cavalry charging an abandoned campfire with "loud yells and sabres drawn" — only to find empty ground — is pure dark comedy of war.
