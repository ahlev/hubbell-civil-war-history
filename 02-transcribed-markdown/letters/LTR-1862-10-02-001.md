## Full Transcription

> October 2, 1862
> Champlain, N.Y.
>
> My dear darling pet,
>
> I recd. yours of the 19th this morning and you may be assured I was rejoiced to hear of your safety, and we ought not to forget to thank God for his protecting care over you. You were preserved while thousands fell on your right hand & on your left. You ought to consecrate your self anew — to His service. Your life was spared amid the dreadful carnage for some wise purpose.
>
> The uncertainty of dear, dear Henry's fate — fill me with sadness — the deepest sorrow. Two or three letters a day have been recd. from the Regt. during the past week — no intelligence from Henry — all his regt. his wounded except McDonald who says he is missing. Charles writes me that one of their son, said he was reported in one of the papers wounded in the hip — and he thought he might have been taken prisoner as he could not very well make his escape. O, how my heart bleeds — and bleeds. Where, where is the dear, dear boy? Echo answers — where? I feel that he somehow got mixed up with another Regt., fell, and was buried unrecognized. DaValls writes that he was detailed to examine the field where their company fought and says Henry Hubbell was missing. All agree that he was wounded.
>
> Friday. Company prevented my finishing my letter yesterday. Capt. Ransom took tea with us and I was very glad to see him. Mrs. Doolittle came with him. I hope to see him again before he leaves. He intends returning to his Regt. next week. His health is improving, but he does not expect to be able to do duty when he returns.
>
> This has been a lovely day — but a very sad one to me. I cannot enjoy the sunshine or the singing birds while there is so much uncertainty about dear precious Henry's fate. O, how my heart aches. Alexander, do get leave of absence, and ascertain if possible something about him. I wish I could go. O how I wish I had the means — nothing would detain me from searching for him. I have been to the post office every morning this week — with the faint hope of some tidings from Henry, I discard — and have no heart to go again. O Alex you must write your poor broken hearted Mother every week. I fear I shall never receive another from dear darling Henry. I fear "he is sleeping his last sleep" — and that — "no sound can wake him to glory again." I feel that I must sink under this great grief. It is too much. My cup is running over. If he has gone, I must follow him soon.
>
> Do write me often. Henry was very faithful — he had a very tender regard for me — even in all his sickness — he begged me to take good care of myself.
>
> Charles did not come on the five train tonight as I expected. I am sadly disappointed — perhaps he will come in nine o'clock train. It is about ten weeks since he left home. I suppose I must say goodbye to him too — very soon. It is hard — hard. Gods will be done. My pilgrimage is nearly run — if grief shortens it a little — no one will suffer. Write me soon and may God bless and preserve you — and restore you to us soon — is the prayer of your affectionate afflicted
>
> Mother

## Editorial Notes

This letter is the emotional nadir of the collection. Mother has assembled a mosaic of conflicting reports — wounded in the hip, missing, possibly prisoner, possibly buried unrecognized — and the mosaic forms the image she fears most: Henry is dead. Her language shifts to past tense when she recalls him: "He had a very tender regard for me." She quotes what may be a hymn or poem: "he is sleeping his last sleep — no sound can wake him to glory again." And then she says it plainly: "If he has gone, I must follow him soon."

The contrast between the beautiful October day ("lovely day," "sunshine," "singing birds") and her interior devastation is almost literary in its precision. She cannot enjoy any of it. She is going to the post office every morning, performing the ritual of hope, and coming back empty.

DaValls is a crucial new figure: someone who physically searched the Antietam field for Henry and found him missing. This is the closest anyone has come to a firsthand investigation. Combined with McDonald's report that no one in the company saw Henry after the battle, and the fact that Henry has not written or been located at any hospital, the evidence is pointing toward the worst outcome.

Capt. Ransom's visit is a striking home-front detail: a 60th NY officer, recovering from illness, taking tea in the Hubbell parlor while Mother's other son from the same regiment lies in a hospital at Harpers Ferry. The war and the community are interpenetrated.
