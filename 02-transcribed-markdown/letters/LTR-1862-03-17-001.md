## Full Transcription

> March 17, 1862
> Baltimore
>
> "God is my refuge and strength: a very present help in trouble."
>
> My dear Mother:
>
> I received your very welcome letter on the 14th inst. You request me to burn the letter. No — no — I cannot do that. I have all the letters I have received since I have been in the army except a few of those I received while at Ogdensburg. This package will bear record of faithfulness of my friends in writing me.
>
> We are to have our dress coats soon and with them I understand a leather stock and dress hat and plumes and a pair of brass epauletts. Several companies in the Regt. now have the coats. They are very good and quite fine cloth.
>
> Relative to the movements. I know nothing, only that our Colonel is very anxious to get the Regt. off the road. A great many rumors have been afloat concerning us. When I get my knapsack packed and the Colonel gives the command "forward march" then I shall believe that we are to move.
>
> You say Mother, that you sometimes regret letting me come. No — no — you cannot. I do not at all regret coming. It seems to me that I love my country ten times better than ever. Ten times? No, not ten times, but ten times ten times.
>
> So Plummer has proved himself "a great scamp", eh? I was afraid of it. I never had any great opinion of him anyway. I saw enough of him when he thrashed for us.
>
> I find the great maxim of the world seems to be "take care of number one". "Every man for himself." I must now tell you how I have been "gulled". While I was at Camden Station a Sergeant in the Excelsior Brigade (Sickles) station at Budds Ferry, while hurrying on to join his Regt. was left in Baltimore without money, it having been stolen from him. The Capt. went with him to the Provost Marshals and then to a Major Belgor, who has the charge of the transportation of the troops, but could do nothing for him. He told him to wait at the Union Relief. He expected the Malone Regt. through soon and would manage to send him through to Washington with them. He, the fellow, was in great distress about it as he should lose a second lieutenancy if he was not there soon. I took pity on him and loaned him five dollars. He promised to return it as soon as he reached his Regt. I heard nothing of him until lately. One of our Co. received a letter from him. Among other things he asked why Corporal Hubbell had not written him. He said that he had written me some time since and had received no answer. I received no letter from him and have written him asking him for the money. (He left me his address.) I have received no answer to the letter. The boy, I understand, received the lieutenancy. I have learned wisdom. It was one of the most foolish things I ever did. I pitied the poor fellow and thought how bad I should feel to be left in a strange city without money. (I have just heard that Capt. Ransom says that our Regt. will have to fight. He has been to Fortress Monroe. He started last Friday. He is now back. The Lieut. Colonel and Adjutant, I understand, went with him. Our Colonel is determined to get the Regt. off the road. I shall not be at all sorry if we are obliged to fight.
>
> You remember that fellow I told you I chased through the city with a file of men, and who was caught by the policemen; well, he ran the guard the other night. A Sergeant and three Corporals went after him and did not succeed in catching him. He is now at headquarters awaiting his courtmartial.
>
> Now for answering your questions: I am enjoying excellent health. I attend church as often as I can. I attended last evening. It sometimes often happens that I am on guard duty Sunday. I often come off guard Sunday morning and am too tired and sleepy to attend the morning service. This was the case yesterday. I retire almost any time. If I am on guard duty I catch a nap the best way I can. Other days I retire at nine or half past. Generally I rise, if I am on guard duty, at one and take a three miles walk; the same at five. Other days at Reveille (six) when we have roll call. When I am on guard duty I take my blanket and spread it on the floor of the guard house and nap a little with one eye open. When I go out with my one oclock relief I often think of you at home. The last night I was on guard was one of the roughest we have had for some time. The very elements seemed to have conspired. I have a rubber blanket I bought of the Capt. (for five shillings) which kept my body dry, but the water ran off into my pants. Why I should think before morning I have at least a gallon of water in each leg. Capt. Ransom is with the first platoon. I am in the second. The first platoon is at Camden Station, about a furlong from us.
>
> The health of the regt. is at present, so far as I know, very good. We in Baltimore think the war will soon be over. What a wonderful display of Southern chivalry we have had at Manassas. That boasted Southern chivalry which was to accomplish so much. There is one thing they do particularly excel in — the rise of their heels.
>
> The McComb Regt. passed through here last Friday. I saw quite a number if knew. Joe Laforrd, the fellow who plowed our buckwheat field for Lumis, I saw. Ask Charley if he remembers my telling him of a fellow that wished to borrow three thousand dollars of Hiram Dudley to buy a farm. I saw him. His name is Hiram Pettice. I did not know that James Sweney was in the Regt. Luther saw him. I am sorry I did not see him. He (Sweney) is a Capt. (The drum is now beating for drill. I must go.) The drum did not beat for drill. The Company turned out without arms. The Capt. then brought the fellow I spoke of in this letter as at headquarters awaiting trial, before the Co. (us) and he (the fellow) made a confession. The Capt. feels bad to have any of his men court-martialed. He saw the Colonel and managed to get him out on condition that he would make a confession before the Company.
>
> This last letter of yours was one of the most interesting you have written me. My dear Mother, I have not written half I wish too. Please write me all about home matters. How many oats, potatoes, peas, buckwheat, etc. did the farm raise. Write about the hens, pony, heifer and old cow and all about home matters. You have done well this last time. I like to read the letter. Please write soon. Love to all.
>
> Your affectionate son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

This letter's most consequential moment is Alexander's refusal to burn letters: "No — no — I cannot do that." His instinct to preserve correspondence — directly opposing Henry's request that Frances burn his — is why this collection of 200+ letters exists 164 years later. The $5 swindle by the Excelsior Brigade sergeant is a perfect vignette of wartime exploitation: a man played on Alexander's sympathy, got his money and his lieutenancy, and vanished. Alexander's guard duty description is the most detailed daily routine in the collection: 1 AM relief walks, sleeping on the guard house floor "with one eye open," a gallon of water pooling in each pant leg during rainstorms. His patriotic declaration — "ten times ten times" — is among his most passionate statements. The McComb Regiment passing through gives us a window into the Champlain community's military service: Joe Laforrd plowed the Hubbell buckwheat field, Hiram Pettice wanted to borrow $3,000, Capt. James Sweney was someone Luther knew.
