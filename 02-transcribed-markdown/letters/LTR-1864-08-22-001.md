## Full Transcription

> Aug. 22, 1864
> 2d Div. Hospital — 20th Corps
> Near Atlanta, Ga.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> You perceive that I am again in the hospital. I am not wounded but am afflicted with what the surgeon calls genuine scurvy. He says I am very young to have it. By taking good care of myself and with proper nourishment (vegetables) I think I shall soon be fit for duty.
>
> I received your letter of August 8th yesterday. Dixon brought it down to me. You may be sure I was pleased for I had not heard from you since the 25th of July. I have received several papers since then. You did not send me a Tribune with an acct. of the battle of the 20th. I should like to see it. If you can, please send it or any other paper with an acct. of it.
>
> Nothing of special interest has transpired since I last wrote — yes, there has too, if the report is true. Very heavy artillery and musketry firing was heard a day or two since to the right of the line and the report of the affair is that a heavy battle came off there, the rebels charging and recharging our works, but were repulsed each time with terrible slaughter. Their loss, it is said, is to be counted by the thousand. The rebels are reported to have been very much under the influence of liquor. Reports are so conflicting that I hardly dare write them. Of a battle having taken place I have but little doubt as the firing was very sharp. You must bear in mind that our line is many miles long and we can only judge what is going on by the firing. There are so many rumors afloat that you can hardly tell what is reliable till you see the papers.
>
> Please send papers often and a magazine now and then if convenient. Please write soon and often. Lieut. Brookway has taken charge of my mail and will forward it to me wherever I am. Please send me some stamps. I will use my last when I send this. I found the two stamps I lost. Mrs Frances yet returned? Tell her I received her letter and didn't see my boots for a week after. I will endeavor to write to her soon. I wrote James and Charles a few days since. I hope that they will write me. I think your fears are groundless about their being prisoners. Love to all. F. A. Cook is at Champlain, and he is hot for Lincoln. I will hoist my colors soon. If I live I shall cast a vote this fall. You know I will have been in the service over three years when the election comes off, and according to the laws of the U.S. I shall be of proper age to vote, as no one is received into the U.S. Service under 18. By the muster rolls I shall be over 21.
>
> Your aff. Son
>
> Alex F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Alexander has scurvy — the disease of the age of sail, now the disease of the Atlanta Campaign. Three months of hardtack, salt pork, and no vegetables. "The surgeon says I am very young to have it." The campaign that ground him "bully thin" has now put him in the hospital.

The battle he hears from the hospital — rebs charging, repulsed with terrible slaughter, drunk on liquor — is likely the Battle of Jonesborough or related fighting in late August. Alexander can only hear it: "our line is many miles long and we can only judge what is going on by the firing."

"I will hoist my colors soon" — the McClellan man is shifting. The war has changed Alexander's politics. F.A. Cook from Champlain is "hot for Lincoln," and Alexander, who yearned for McClellan four months ago, is coming around. The voting age calculation is revealing: he enlisted under 18 but will be "over 21" by the muster rolls. The army made him a man, and now it will let him vote.
