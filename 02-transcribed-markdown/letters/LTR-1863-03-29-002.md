## Full Transcription

> March 29, 1863
> Alexandria, Va.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your welcome letter of Mch. 19th in due time. Am expecting a letter from you tomorrow. I received one from James dated Mch. 22, which I answered. He writes in good spirits. Cyrus Kellogg was in the squad on Friday that we took down to Aquia Creek. He told me to give his regards to my friends. Says his time will be out the 10th of June and that Hooker says he will give the two years men some hard fighting before their time is out. He looks well; he has been in the convalescent camp some time. He inquired about my friends; says he heard from Champlain
>
> [page break]
>
> that James 14 doing well. The men in the convalescent camp live in wooden barracks built this fall. The camp of distribution moved near; it is where we go for the men. Two nights ago our reg't. was called by the long roll and a couple of other reg'ts, lying near. I don't know whether Fort Lyons was any signal fires; at any rate she fired some guns (4) as a signal and the camp was roused. I heard that Lieut. Burdick wore a streak of blue lightning till every man was out of his tent. His men like him for all that. Our Co. being down here escaped all the fun of being routed us. I don't like this having to turn out in the night in such a hurry.
>
> The weather here is quite changeable. Yesterday it rained hard; today there is a cold north wind which keeps us around the fire.
>
> I think that you made a good arrangement about the cow and horse. I suppose you can buy your milk. I read a letter from Champlain which says hay is $2,500 a ton. I guess letting out the farm for grazing will be the best thing you can do. How much did you receive for sugaring last summer? Did Silas find out from that man at the Point about Henry. I am afraid that he was killed. Emerson Smith is at the convalescent camp. Cyrus K. says that Mink and Cook are at Georgetown. I think we will not leave here very soon. We are not very anxious to leave. I have answered Uncle John's letter but did not ask him to write. I don't like to write letters except home letters. That kind of clerks do Jim Morrison and Albert Contreras make? Frances knows how she likes them, I suppose.
>
> There were a lot of deserters here a day or two ago that were going back to their reg'ts. They were dressed in citizen's clothes and were good looking men. One man from our Co. has had a furlough to go to Plattsburgh and has been gone about double his time. I don't know as he will come back. His brother is a soldier in our Co.
>
> Where is Henry Gregory and what is he doing? He is the man with too big a heart as Dr. Coyt said. I am glad you are going to have some maple sugar. Think it is a good bargain if he gives you 150 lbs. and doesn't cut any good wood.
>
> Mch. 30 — I was disappointed this morning in not receiving a letter from home. Have not received any papers for some time. If Alex likes the Tribune you had better send him some of them. I hope you will not send either of us any papers before you have read them through. I sent Alex two Tribunes some time ago.
>
> Our regt. has shoulder scales for the men. It makes the men look better. Last night I went to the Methodist church. It was crowded. Part of the church was filled with soldiers marched there in a body. Had a good sermon. I had rather attend the Episcopal church. As the mail goes out in a few minutes I must close.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Two Hubbell brothers write on the same day, 50 miles apart, to the same mother. Alexander is indignant about being asked to hide in battle; Charles is quietly saying he thinks Henry is dead and asking about maple sugar prices. The parallel letters capture the war's emotional range — one brother burning with honor, the other pragmatically preparing for loss. The night alarm with Fort Lyons's signal guns and Burdick's "streak of blue lightning" is vivid — the war is coming closer even to Alexandria.
