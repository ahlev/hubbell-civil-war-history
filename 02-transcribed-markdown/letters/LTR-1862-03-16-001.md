## Full Transcription

> March 16th, 1862
> Harpers Ferry
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I know you will be very anxious to hear from me. Our Regt. and two brigades besides ours left Harpers Ferry some time ago for Winchester, with the expectation of driving the rebles away from that place, but after we had marched within three miles of Winchester we heard that twenty-five thousand of our troops were there, and of course we were not needed. The rebles have left that place probably for good. The distance from Harpers Ferry to Winchester is about thirty miles. All that distance we marched on foot with our knap-sacks and, back again, but I have become use to marching now and do not mind it much.
>
> We camped out two nights on the way. We had our tents. A regt. of Cavalry went before us and tore down a reble flag at Berryville, ten miles from Winchester. Our Co., after marching twenty miles, went on picket and also out skirmishing. I was very much fatigued that night. To tell the truth I have gone through a good many hardships since I have left Camp McClellan, but I am none the worse for it. We expect to leave Harpers Ferry very soon, probably for Washington. I do not know where we will go then. The report is now that we leave in the morning at eight. We are having very mild weather now. The roads between here and Winchester are very good.
>
> It is now nine months since I left home. I think this rebellion will be over by next summer, at least I hope so. I received a letter from Uncle John last evening. He is in Mich Genesee Co. He is not well he writes. I have answered his letter.
>
> Please excuse this writing and all mistakes. Take good care of your health. Dirrect your letters the same as before. Write often.
>
> I remain, your aff. Son,
>
> Henry

## Editorial Notes

Henry's march toward Winchester and back (60 miles round trip with knapsacks) coincides with Banks's occupation of Winchester on March 12. By the time the 34th NY arrived near the city, 25,000 Union troops were already there. The skirmishing and picket duty after a 20-mile march shows the physical demands on infantry. Henry's rare admission of hardship ("gone through a good many hardships since I have left Camp McClellan") and his continued optimism about the war ending by summer are poignant given his September death at Antietam.
