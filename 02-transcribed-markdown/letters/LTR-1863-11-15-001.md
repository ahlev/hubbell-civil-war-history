## Full Transcription

> Nov. 15th, 1863
> Champlain, N.Y.
>
> My Darling Child:
>
> Yours of the 1st of Nov. was rec'd. yesterday, and quite overwhelmed us with joy, for I had almost despaired of hearing from you again. We were beginning to have our forebodings. Sometimes we had you in Libby prison, sometimes all mangled in a field hospital, and at others, with our blessed lamented Henry among the "unknown" dead. You can imagine the set of your letters upon us. All our friends are rejoicing with us for they thought we had reason to be anxious. Mrs. Brinkerhoff came to me in church today and whispered "Have you heard from Alexander?" Her countenance beamed with joy when I answered in the affirmative. We know that Geary's Division was engaged in the last battle, although we are no member of the 60th, but we feared most that you were among the prisoners captured with the wagons. You must write us often for this anxiety of mind tells upon my poor shattered nervous system. I read a letter from James with yours. He will write you soon, so I will not transcribe his letter. He has had little leisure for writing, not as much as he had last year. I hope you will be here next summer to meet him. I can but hope that this terrible war is nearly over, that the rebellion is in its death throes. I rejoice that Charles was not in the last battle in Virginia. His reg't., fortunately was not among those sent out from Washington to the front at that time. I expect every mail to hear that they have been ordered away for I see it announced that the "Invalid Corps" only will be retained in Washington.
>
> I am sadly disappointed in not obtaining your discharge. Your Col. wrote me that he "would do what he could to restore you to me, but feared the attempts would be futile." I am gratified at the good character he gives you and I hope you will continue to merit the approbation of your officers. I feel as your former Col. D. "that my claim upon you is stronger than country, even." I hope to have you yet. In the meantime take good care of yourself for my sake, as well as your own, physically, morally, and spiritually. Do not neglect the cultivation of your mind and morals. The habits you form in the army will attend you through life. I warn you against the "yellow covered" literature. I need not repeat to you what I have so often said about reading. Use no language that is in the slightest not pure. Never indulge in unworthy phrases. Be gentlemanly in your conversation and manners. Remember your promise to me. I think of it with pleasure. Never forget for one instant that you are a professed Christian. "Go and tell Jesus" all your trials. His ear is ever open, and more than that. His kind sympathizing heart is ready, to comfort, to heal. Go to Him for guidance, for wisdom, for submission, for every needed grace. Cast all your burdens upon him and He will sustain you, for he never fails. Keep near Him in prayer. Like the great and good Havelock, be a Christian soldier. Do nothing to bring reproach upon the blessed name of the Saviour. And may He keep you, my darling boy, in the hollow of His hand and restore you soon to me, is the daily prayer of your affectionate
>
> Mother
>
> I hope you will get a Thanksgiving dinner somewhere. I wish I could send you one. I trust you will be with us next year. I shall not go out this year. How can I when all my boys are away! I cannot.

## Cross-Reference Notes

### Connections to Other Letters in Collection
- **Mother's voice:** Only the third letter from Mother in the collection (after April 30 formal inquiry and May 4-14 grief letter). Her tone has shifted from grief to anxious joy — relief that Alexander is alive, but the old fears remain.
- **"Remember your promise to me":** Almost certainly the temperance pledge Alexander made during his February 1863 furlough — the same promise that kept him from whiskey rations (LTR-1863-02-28-001).
- **"Yellow covered" literature:** Cheap sensational novels — the dime novels soldiers consumed voraciously. Mother warns against them; Alexander has been reading Welky's histories of Rome, France, and the U.S.
- **"How can I when all my boys are away!":** The desolation of a Thanksgiving table with no sons present. Henry dead, Alexander in Tennessee, Charles in Washington, James at West Point.
- **Col. D.** — likely the regiment's original colonel who had claimed Mother's family claim was "stronger than country" — a remarkable statement from a military officer.

## Editorial Notes

Mother's second substantial letter in the collection is a document of faith, fear, and moral instruction. She has been imagining Alexander in Libby Prison, in a field hospital, among the "unknown" dead alongside Henry. The relief at his letter is physical — "quite overwhelmed us with joy."

The spiritual counsel is extensive and reveals Mother's deepest values: read no "yellow covered" literature, use no impure language, be gentlemanly, remember his promise (temperance), be a Christian soldier like Havelock. She is fighting for Alexander's soul as anxiously as he fights for the Union.

"How can I when all my boys are away! I cannot." The Thanksgiving refusal — she will not celebrate alone — is the collection's most compressed expression of wartime maternal grief. Four sons gone from the table, one permanently.
