## Full Transcription

> May 12, 1862
> Monday — 34th Regt.
> Camp Alton
> West Point, Va.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I know that you are anxious to hear from me every week and oftener. I shall write a few lines to you just as often as I can. You must not be to anxious about me or Alexander, for it will do no good. I hope you will take good care of your health.
>
> I am very anxious to know where the cows and horse are pastured, but I forgot that vegetation is not so fast up home as it is here. The weather is very warm here, but the nights are cold.
>
> I have not received an answer to the money or from the money that I sent. I expect a letter tomorrow. There is a good deal of card playing going on in out Co. for money. I shall never take a fancy to card playing nor gambling, or anything else. I expect every day we shall move, probably to Richmond. Since I wrote last we have moved three miles from where we were. We are now near a river called the ———. It empties into the York River. I should judge from the looks and from what I have heard, there are about one hundred thousand troops encamped around here. I have heard that the 60th Regt. was on its way for this place. Alexander will get tired of it when he comes to march as much as I have. I shall be very glad to see him. If the war continues as successful as it has lately, seems to me it can not last a great while. McClellan is working slow but sure, which I think is the best way.
>
> Write and let me know who is working the garden. I am very glad that Mrs. Brinkhoff is coming back. It will be much pleasanter for you. What time does James leave for West Point?
>
> The sutler charges 50 cts. for fresh butter — everything else in proportion, but then it costs him a good deal to bring his goods here. He, of course, must make something. I do not go in very deep. I don't care much about it anyway. Write soon.
>
> Your aff. Son
>
> Henry
>
> (On same letter) 13th The weather today is very warm — as warm as it is at home in July. I do not like the South as well as I do the North thus far. The river that we are on is very crooked. Steamboats are passing and going all the while.

## Editorial Notes

This is the last letter in the 1862 JAN-MAY PDF. Henry's hope of seeing Alexander — "the 60th Regt. was on its way for this place" — will not be fulfilled; Alexander goes to Harpers Ferry instead, and the brothers' paths diverge further as the war splits them between the Peninsula and the Shenandoah Valley. Henry's "McClellan is working slow but sure" captures the Army of the Potomac's collective faith in their commander before the Seven Days Battles shattered it. The unnamed river ("I should judge from the looks") is almost certainly the Pamunkey, which is indeed notably crooked. The 100,000 troops estimate is accurate — the Army of the Potomac's concentration around West Point and the York-Pamunkey corridor was one of the largest military assemblages in American history to that point. Henry's moral abstinence from card playing and gambling, like his Sabbath reflections, shows a quiet rectitude running through all his letters.
