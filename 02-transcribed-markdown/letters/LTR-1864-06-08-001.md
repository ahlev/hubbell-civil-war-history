## Full Transcription

> June 8, 1864
> Marietta, Ga.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I had, oh such a jolly good time this morning. What do you think? I received no less than six letters from home. It did seem so good to hear from you all again. The letters were from April 27th to May 18th inclusive. I am very glad to hear that the Mr. Bass is convalescent. Guess he thinks flirting dangerous business. A man of his age ought to know better. He had a taste of bullets without going to war. I was vastly amused at your account of the matter. I expected when I got your letter I should have a horrible account. So Mr. Bass bled a painfull of blood. Oh, Mother, that is most as bad as the boy and the "hundred foxes." I have seen thousands of men wounded but hardly ever say anything come up to the pail of blood. You will carry off the palm this time certainly.
>
> You will probably hear long ere this reaches you that the Johnnies have "dug out" and fallen back toward Atlanta. We have been resting for the last two days, I suppose in order to bring up supplies. We start on, I believe, after the Rebs in the morning. May God be with us and grant us the victory. I am very glad that you have got my county bounty. Mr. Averill's charges were more moderate than I supposed. You say that my $500 is all taken up and drawing interest. Have you taken out the money I borrowed from you? And does that leave my five hundred clear! I had in Mr. Savage's hands (including what you had deposited before I came home) when I left home four hundred dollars. The State bounty amounted to seventy dollars and the National to $190 making a total of 660 dollars, and now taking out what I owe you — nearly $100, would leave it all right, I think. If I live to be paid I shall be able to send home over $600. The mail is going out immediately so I must close. Culver Bostwick sends much love to his friends. Says he has written ten letters home and has received no answer. Feels always discouraged from writing. "Wished me to tell his friends that he is in the best of spirits." Please send me a silk handkerchief. I need one very much. Love to all.
>
> Your aff. Son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Six letters at once — the mail drought breaks and Alexander is "jolly." His teasing of Mother's account of Bass's shooting is pure Alexander: "I have seen thousands of men wounded but hardly ever say anything come up to the pail of blood." The veteran's amused perspective on civilian drama. Bass's shooting was a sensation in Champlain; in Georgia, Alexander has been writing under fire with bullets spelling out "b-e-a-u-t-i-f-u-l" overhead.

The financial accounting mid-campaign is extraordinary — bounties, interest, loans to Mother, deposits with Savage — all tallied while the army rests before pursuing Johnston toward Atlanta. Alexander manages his finances as precisely from Georgia as from a desk.
