## Full Transcription

> May 10th, 1862
> Camp Niles Relay House
>
> My Dear Mother:
>
> I received your very welcome letter a few days since and immediately wrote one in answer, but did not mail it and on looking it over today I tore it up on acct. of something I stated as certain and have since found it to be a mere rumor.
>
> This railroad will be given up to the Company the fifteenth or twentieth of this month by the Government, and consequently we will no longer be needed here. One of the Lieuts. offered to lay a wager of $25 to $1 (I believe) that our regt. would be discharged within a month. One thing is quite certain, we will not be in service much longer. I am now very anxious to get home as James leaves so soon. I don't mean for you to understand that I am at all sorry I enlisted. I have never for one moment regretted it. I do not wish to return home until this war is over; until this rebellion is crushed and completely crushed. I want to see these Southern braggarts humbled. The Rebels will soon be driven to their "last ditch". I expect there will be great news on Monday. McClellan seems to be pressing pretty hard.
>
> Wonder what sort of weather you are having up there. We seldom have very much warmer weather there in July than we are having here today. We have no drill or inspection here on Saturdays, but are obliged to turn out for dress parade at half after four P.M. How strange it will seem to me when I return home not to be obliged to be present at roll call; not obliged to get a pass when I wish to go anywhere away from the door yard. We are kept in very close, but it does not trouble me at all as I have very little occasion to go outside the camp. One of our men received the sentence of a court-martial yesterday. (He was absent twentyfour hours from camp without leave.) It was this — "That said John Pickle having been found guilty of the charges brought against him be sentenced to lose one half of two months pay (13 dollars)".
>
> Mother I am sorry you worry so much. There is no use in it at all, at all. You must not do it. We are all in Gods hands. He will do as He thinks best. Do you think I have forgotten my Mother, my brothers and sister? No! never, never, never. Forget you indeed — I am astonished, shocked to hear such a thing. — Well, I guess you had the blues when you wrote that, eh? I shall try and write oftener. I have but very little leisure. Our time is so fully occupied with the drill and guard duty. Still I can always find time to write you. Now, Mother, why don't you write me oftener? There were nine days interval between your last two letters. Never fear, Mother, I have not forgotten you. McClellan is giving it to the rebels "right smart" and I expect I shall soon be home.
>
> So you have let out the "plowed land" to Portray, eh? Pretty good plan I guess, only I don't like the pony part of it. Perhaps my fears are groundless. Tell J. and C. to keep a sharp lookout and see that he is not abused. Now Mother don't you let out the cows or garden or any of the land around home. Get a boy by all means and immediately and when you get one get a good one if you do have to pay him higher wages. I will pay him and am only too glad to do it. I shall in all probability be home soon an then you will need the boy no longer. I shall take matters into my own hands and see how nice I can make everything look.
>
> You and I must go and see Grandmother McNiel, and Aunt Jenny. Wonder why she don't answer my last letter. I shall always keep the letter she wrote me. I prize it very much. Wish she would write me another. Grandmother is a real patriot and well worthy of her daughter who has sent two sons to the war.
>
> Secure pasturage, Mother, if you can at Dodds. Don't have anything to do with the "gentleman" who is afraid of a gun. He is a very dangerous fellow, certainly. Wonder if he isn't afraid of a firecracker. Suppose he means to errify people with one of his awfully ferocious looks. If he gets to be very troublesome just put the word "Gunpowder" on your gate and you won't see him again very soon! I don't believe. How does S. P. Hubbell, attorney-at-law, Champlain, get along? Has he got to be justice of the peace yet? Those are two noble patriots, eh? Wonder when they will be ready to get out of their mothers lap?
>
> I have just returned from "dress parade". Tell Charley to add to what I mentioned in my last letter 75 cents for tapping boots, one dollar for the band, 25 cents for a brass drum for our little drummer. We have a splendid band. Their instruments cost over nine hundred dollars. There are probably very few bands in the field superior to "the band of the Sixtieth." They have serenaded (how do you spell it) quite a number in Baltimore, and were very highly praised by the papers. One night while playing in Baltimore a secessionist stuck his head out of a window and began blatting. This made them furious and they immediately struck up with all their might Yankee Doodle. He said no more. They offered the rebel a large sum if he would only come down. As it was they took up stones to hit him if he showed his head again, but the fellow thought it was not quite prudent. A very wise conclusion.
>
> We have battalion drill every day except Saturdays and Sundays. We were drilled in firing yesterday. Each man was supplied with ten rounds of blank cartridges. We made a little noise and smoke. (Drawings of soldiers appear) What am I about, making pictures on my letter. Well, well. I get to thinking and forget what I am about.
>
> You ask me in your letter if I read the 91st Psalm. I do and take great comfort in its precious promises. "God is my refuge and strength; a very present help in trouble." I try to commit all my ways to him. I have not forgotten my promise to you. I love to read the first psalm. Fathers psalm.
>
> Tell James and Charles to write often. Love to all. Do not think I have forgotten you. Do not worry.
>
> Your affectionate son,
>
> Alexander F.
>
> P.S. Look out for pony. Tell James to put in kind regards to Mr. Copeland.

## Editorial Notes

This is Alexander's most expansive letter — covering military rumor, court-martial, farm management, family visits, sarcasm about shirkers, band exploits, firing drill, and religious devotion in a single sitting. The secessionist window incident is the best anecdote in the collection: the band responding to heckling by blasting Yankee Doodle, then offering to pay the rebel to come down and fight while gathering stones. Alexander's drawings of soldiers on the letter — "What am I about, making pictures on my letter" — are a rare artistic detail; if the original 1947-49 transcription preserved them, they would be invaluable. His contempt for S.P. Hubbell and the "gentleman afraid of a gun" reveals deep scorn for those who didn't enlist. The "Fathers psalm" (Psalm 1) suggests their father was either deceased or a strong spiritual influence — this is the first direct reference to him in the collection. The Psalm 91 / Psalm 46 conflation is a small but telling detail about how these young men carried Scripture in approximate, living ways rather than as exact citation.
