## Full Transcription

> August 8th, 1864
>
> Capt. J. M. Ransom —
>
> Dear Sir:
>
> I received your kind letter of July 10th some days since, but knowing as you do how we soldiers are situated on an active campaign, I trust you will excuse my tardiness in answering. My Mother was right in telling you that I would be very much pleased by the receipt of a letter from you. I was indeed pleased — and sincerely trust they will be less like "angel's visits" in the future.
>
> I have so much to write that I hardly know where to begin — and for that very reason had a good will not to begin at all — but nevertheless I will do my best. Everything seems to be going on here most favorably. The theatre of the most active operations for the last few days has been the extreme right. I will not weary you with a detailed account of its late movements as you will have seen a full acct. of them in the papers long before this reaches you. Suffice it to say that the combined attack of the forces of Howard, (formerly McPherson's) and Schofield was very successful, and that the last remaining railroad to the rebs is severed. I hope we shall be able to bag the Johnnies here, for I am heartily tired of chasing them.
>
> They are bringing us, I understand, a heavy siege train — so I expect the Johnnies are in a pretty tight place. The hardest fighting of the campaign has occurred since we crossed the Chattahoochee and all the Johnnies have to show for it is a long list of casualties that would frighten a man just to look at it. How comforting such a document must be to Jeff Davis & Co. The fight of the 20th of July was an awful one. The rebels threw their men upon us in solid columns and seemed bound to drive us into the Chattahoochee. But we were not to be driven. We had come all the way from Chattanooga to see Atlanta and were bound to see it and are now knocking pretty loudly at its doors.
>
> We changed front during the battle no less than three times. I thought matters looked a little "scaly" when I saw the rebs coming up in our rear, but for all that we whipped them. Our regt. and the 147th Penn. have the credit of saving the two Division batteries. The rebs had the use of the guns of Bundy's battery when we came up. Our regt. was the only one of the Brigade that was not broken during the fight. At the fight at Lost Mountain our regt. was the only one of the Brig. that got up to the top of the hill and held the ground. Capt. Delaney was wounded in the fight of the 20th. I have reached nearly the end of my sheets and have not said half what I wish to say. Shall have to leave it to another letter.
>
> Yours truly,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell.
>
> P. H. Brookway has got a commission and is in command of Co. H. Capt. Elliott is now Major and Thomas is Lieut./Col., Goddard is Colonel. We are to be consolidated soon with the 134th N.Y. Hobart is Capt. of Co. I. A good many of the officers will be mustered out when we are consolidated. The surplus non coms are to be reduced. P. H. Brookway has a first Lieut. commission.

## Editorial Notes

This letter to Capt. Ransom is Alexander's most detailed battle account. Writing to a fellow officer rather than his mother, Alexander gives the Peachtree Creek narrative he couldn't write home: "The rebels threw their men upon us in solid columns and seemed bound to drive us into the Chattahoochee. But we were not to be driven. We had come all the way from Chattanooga to see Atlanta and were bound to see it." The pride is earned — the 60th was the only regiment not broken during the fight.

"I thought matters looked a little 'scaly' when I saw the rebs coming up in our rear" — the understatement of a man who saw his regiment nearly flanked and overrun. Changing front three times during a battle means the regiment was nearly surrounded and had to fight in multiple directions.

The consolidation with the 134th N.Y. is ominous for Alexander: "surplus non coms are to be reduced." As a sergeant, his rank is at risk. The army's organizational efficiency collides with individual soldiers' careers.

"I have reached nearly the end of my sheets and have not said half what I wish to say." — the frustration of a natural writer who has too much war to describe and too little paper to describe it on.
