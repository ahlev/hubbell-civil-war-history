## Full Transcription

> April 16, 1863
> Aquia Landing
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your letter of the 7 inst. a day or two since and was filled with great wrath when I read your acct. of your trouble with your sugar-maker. I fairly hopped up and down, I was so provoked. If there is such a thing as get satisfaction out of old Rye I will have it if ever I return. Wonder if he can find no better employment than persecuting a helpless woman. Shame on him — though I very much doubt if he has either shame or soul. I cannot find words to express my indignation.
>
> We are now under marching orders and are to take eight days rations in our knapsacks and haversacks. We have five days now packed in our haversacks and will take three more in our knapsacks, when we move. The "Army of the Potomac" was on the move day before yesterday, but I presume we are pretty well stuck in the mud by this time as it has rained furiously all day yesterday. Nevertheless I do not think this will detain them long. General Hooker means to give the two years men an opportunity to pay a parting salute to the rebels. The Army is in fine condition and spirits and please, for my sake, box the ears of the first person you hear say they will not fight. Gen'l. Hooker so far as I know, is in high favor with all the troops.
>
> Your intelligence about Henry fills me with sorrow, though I still hope. It is singular that we can hear of no one who saw him dead upon the field and it is upon this that I base my hope that he is yet alive. Your fears that he was wounded are groundless and (excuse me) very foolish. Love to all. Remember me to the Captain and tell him that the company has drawn twenty-eight new rifles. The old ones without bayonets have been turned over, and say to him I am sorry he is not to march and fight with us this summer.
>
> Mrs. Hoyle still sends papers. She is very kind. Upon the wrapper were written some very patriotic sentences in English and Latin. I will delegate Miss Fannie to make a fitting reply — my appreciation of the Latin — my high exalted patriotism — valor and determination to die in the last ditch — ahem! Tell Miss Fan I expect she will do it up brown. Miss Laura's initials were prefixed to the sentences.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

The Henry passage is the emotional center: Alexander and Charles have reached opposite conclusions from the same evidence. Charles says "I am afraid he was killed" three times; Alexander says "I still hope" because no one saw him dead. Alexander calls Mother's fears "groundless and very foolish" — the word "foolish" is almost cruel, but it's the cruelty of a man who cannot afford to grieve while marching toward his own battle. In two weeks he will be at Chancellorsville, hearing the cheers of the boys charging, and he will need every ounce of hope he has.
