## Full Transcription

> Jan. 11, 1865
> Champlain, N.Y.
>
> To — Alex
>
> Praise the Lord — Bless the Lord O my soul — all within me bless His holy, holy name for preserving the precious life of my darling child. Your letters of the 24th & 25th are this moment received, the first which we have received from you since the battles before Nashville. Language is too feeble to convey the feelings of my heart, my joy, my gratitude to God for His wonderful mercy to me and mine. You are among the living while my fears had numbered you with the dead.
>
> I supposed that you were in the terrible battles before Nashville, and I feared was among the slain as we did not hear from you. Could it be possible that you have recd. not one of the letters which I have written you every week since you left home and directed as you directed me to do, Nashville — long, long, letters. I suppose they are with the mass of letters, and not readily found, and will be forwarded to the bond letter office. It is too bad. Your letter written at Chattanooga the 20th of Nov. I did not receive until the 4th of Jan. Your letters from Nashville were recd. in due time. You write me not to direct again to Nashville. I hope I shall have your address soon. I shall write you every day until I hear from you. I did before I recd. yours — your first letter. I had, I think, three sheets in my first letter to you — waiting for your address. It was mailed immediately on the receipt of yours.
>
> In my weekly letter to you, I informed you of all that was transpiring in our midst & on our borders. You know this through the papers that we are under military rule — that we have troops here — one hundred and fifty soldiers. We are to have a company of cavalry in a few days. Stables are being built for one hundred horses. Our streets are blue with soldiers. We have reveille in the morning & tattoo at night. There are some of the handsomest soldiers — from Ft. ——, strictly from good families. The Capt. says there are "about 25 hard customers" among them. The Indian woman then a second New Yrs. dinner at the barracks. They are to give us a concert in return this week. There are some splendid musicians among them. Frances is busy making costumes for them. Their concert is to be a gorgeous masquerade. They are delightfully delighted with Champlain. The people make a great deal of them. A little Corporal told me the other night that it was like coming out of prison to leave Ft. ——. The young ladies are running wild over the officers. There are five & only one of them married. S——. has made a conquest of one of the Lieuts. They keep a picket of fifty out. There are five on this road, north of us. Mr. Hoyle's store house is used for a guard house. One is kept on the hotel & one at each bridge. Every package from Canada is guarded — and not a person is allowed to cross. Mr. Bass went to the Capt. to get a pass for his scholars — but he did not get one. Mr. Rye started to go to his uncles — but was sent back. I don't think there is on our side any trouble with England. The most intense excitement prevails here. It is painful. People stand aghast and say "What does it mean?" "Why are the bars put up against Canada?" Any person who is suspected of coming from Canada is arrested & escorted by the soldiers to the Marshalls office.
>
> Jan. 16th: I am anxiously awaiting a letter from you — for I cannot mail a letter to you until I receive your address. I think of you every day and hour, and dream of you at night. You speak of being nearly starved. Dear child — how I do wish I could send you a box of good things. I can but hope that the rebellion is in its death throes. I have thought so many times before & am afraid to allow myself to hope too strongly. I know our enemies are about sending two hundred thousand more slaves South — that appalls me. I see that Shermans army is resting and recruiting for another campaign. I fear you will have no rest this winter.
>
> Our last letter from James was written on the 9th of Dec. I have written him repeatedly and sent him stamps, thinking that want of them might be the reason of his not writing. Charlie has done the same — but neither any progress. He cannot is at the hospital. You remember — an order was issued from the War Department about the middle of December ordering Surgeons of hospitals to send able soldiers to the front who were fit for duty. I fear James was sent at that time, and fell into the hands of guerillas. There was nothing for those soldiers with several hundred guerillas killed & captured a vast number. I very much fear poor James was among them. I have written the Surgeon of the hospital inquiring what time he left there — & whether for the regiment or a convalescent camp. When he has changed his quarters he has never called to inform me & give his address. I am in the deepest anxiety about him. Some evil has befallen him. He would not cause me so much anxiety — he is too good a boy. In his last letter he sent a very pretty ring — made of bone by himself.
>
> The snow is so deep that I cannot go to the P.O. this morning. I hope Frances will bring me a letter tonight from the Surgeon. You did not write me from New York. I suppose you had not time. I shall look for a letter from you this week. He visited Mrs. Denys last Sat. They made no particular enquired about you. Little Miss Denys is getting to be quite a pretty girl. There are eight soldiers stationed near their house. She is making them little bags for threads, buttons, pins & needles. Mrs. & and I went into the hospital to see some of our side soldiers day before yesterday. The ladies have made them very comfortable. One of them is spitting blood. We have stables ready for two hundred horses. They have not come yet. It is said they are in Plattsburg. The Co. at drilling there. Joe Moore is five hundred dollars a year for the stables he has furnished for one hundred.
>
> There is nothing talked of but the masquerade which is to come off next week. They have engaged a Boston band. The officers are going into it and all the young people here, even P. Ransom's wife — (Frances will not.) We are working for the soldiers again, making "housewives" like yours — only of calico soldiers.
>
> The "Appleby Glee Club" has given their complimentary concert. I will leave it for Frances to describe to you. They wear scarlet instead of blue, like you. They belong to the heavy artillery. Many of them belong to the first families in Boston & enlisted under the order that they should not be sent out of Massachusetts and they have not been until now. They are not in much danger here. One poor fellow, for getting intoxicated at his post (the bank) was compelled to march three days in front of the barracks with 70 lbs on his back. The punishment was made more severe from its refusing to tell who it was that orated it to him. His diet during the time was bread and water. This happened during one of the most severe storms we have had this winter. The weather was extremely cold. Tom Moore says he bore it will until the third day. He was about broken down then.
>
> A smart looking corporal sat just above us last Sabbath — who had his chevrons taken off Monday morning for taking too much of the ——! The officers and soldiers met on duty attend church & prayer meetings regularly. We have meetings every evening. They attend singing school and enjoy themselves very much. They have become acquainted in many families and are having sociable times. Many of them are educated men. I wish you could be as pleasantly situated. You are cut off from all the comforts and pleasures of civilized society. You need to be the more watchful and prayerful. The influence of example is powerful for good or evil. Let your example be such the others say he made better by it, and shun everything that may have a hurtful influence upon your mind or morals. Rule your spirit. You know what Solomon says — that one who does not rule his spirit is like a city without a wall. Let that purity of character which your dear father has so justly assigned — be your aim. Be not come short of it. I have read of many beautiful instances of soldiers in this war preserving their moral and Christian integrity unsullied. Be chaste and pure in your language — be gentlemanly in your deportment — dignified in your manner — a kind of courteous to all. Let your Bible be your most intimate companion. I beseech you not to indulge in reading the vile literature which is circulated in the army. You had better take coals of fire to your bosom. I warn you against it. I charge you not to read it.

## Editorial Notes

Mother's longest letter in the collection — and the home front's most detailed portrait. Champlain, New York is under military rule: 150 soldiers, cavalry stables, pickets on every bridge, the Canadian border sealed. Boston artillery in scarlet coats give concerts and attend masquerade balls. The young ladies "are running wild over the officers." A corporal loses his chevrons for drinking. Frances makes costumes but won't attend the masquerade.

And underneath the social whirl: "You are among the living while my fears had numbered you with the dead." Mother thought Alexander died at Nashville. She's been writing every week, long letters to Nashville that were forwarded from Chattanooga and lost in the system. Now she writes daily until she gets his address. And James — no letter since December 9. The War Department ordered hospitals to send able soldiers back to the front. Mother fears the worst: guerrillas.

The moral counsel is a mother's last weapon: "Rule your spirit." "Let your Bible be your most intimate companion." "I beseech you not to indulge in reading the vile literature." She cannot protect her sons with her body, so she protects them with her words. "I charge you not to read it." The charging, the beseeching, the warning — the language of a woman who has lost one son and nearly lost three more.
