## Full Transcription

> Alexandria, Va.
> Feb. 22, 1863.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your welcome letter of Feb. 9th and also the Tribune and the Observer. I found the Tribune interesting. I'm sorry that you think I did not receive your letters. I assure you your letters are very welcome. Today is Sunday and we are blockaded by the snow storm. There is about six inches of snow on the ground and it is snowing hard and blowing. I am glad that our men have not to go off today. This snow will all go off at once and make lots of mud which we will have to wade through to go to the distributing camp for the stragglers. A few days since we had a hard snow storm. The snow quickly went off and left us some awful roads to travel. I found then that shoes were not the thing. I wish you would send me two dollars as I want to buy a pair of boots. You can get that $20.00 check cashed and use as much as you are a mind to. I would prefer greenbacks as Uncle Sam's money passes the best here. I bought a pair of boots some time since but after wearing them two or three days sold them as they hurt my feet and will wait until pay day for my pay. We expect to be paid next month. Boots that reach up to the knees or above are all the fashion here among officers and men. The mud dries up very quickly here, but is bad while it lasts. We do not have any as cold weather here as at the North.
>
> I went down to Aquia Creek a few days since and came near going to see Alex. I intend to see him before long if possible. We came back on the mail steamer, which we went on in the evening, and slept there on the floor of the cabin around the store till morning. We started at 9:00 A.M. and arrived here at 1:00 P.M. We do not always get as good a place at that to sleep in. The boat was filled with officers and men going home on a ten days furlough. There were also two sick boys and one man and some more ladies taking care of them. Their mothers, I am sure. A. Douglas has gone to Fortress Monroe with some of our men as a guard, also Lieut. Knapp. They took four days rations with them and we expect them back today. Two or three hundred men have left here nearly every day
>
> [page break]
>
> some time back from the convalescent camp and other camps for their regiments.
>
> Lieut. Col. Armstrong has had some difficulty with our Col. and has resigned. The Col. was restoring under arrest, I hear. Armstrong was down here and bid the Co. goodbye. I told him to tell you I was all right if he saw you. He said he would make it a point to see you. I hear that Capt. Rich will be Major.
>
> Yesterday a man was buried who belonged to this Co. Four have died in this Co., but nearly all the men are well now. How do you and Frances get along? You must both try to enjoy yourselves. I hope Frances will not work too hard teaching French. Would not you both like to make a visit to Charlotte? You wanted to make a visit there last winter with me. I have just overheard that the Adjutant is to be Major, so Capt. Rich will remain as he is. Who is the newest? How did Silas' lecture go off? I have not been to church for some time on account of the weather and being away. Our 1st Lieut. goes to High Mass at the Catholic church, but I think it does not do him much good as he swears hard sometimes. Does Sydney work any this winter or his sister or has he a boy? Has she got along with his officer? I must write slips and send some tomorrow.
>
> I saw one soldier going home on a furlough who said that his Co. drew lots to see who should go. I have not heard from Alex. Do you think he will get a furlough. We have potatoes quite often. We have no dried apples.
>
> Your aff. Son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

February 22, 1863 — George Washington's birthday, and Charles is in Alexandria, Virginia, Washington's home city, within walking distance of Mount Vernon, and doesn't mention it. The letter is consumed with boots, mud, officer politics, and the question of dried apples. The mundane overwhelms the symbolic.
