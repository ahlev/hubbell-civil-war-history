## Full Transcription

> Dec. 1, 1863
> Capitol Hill
> Washington, D.C.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> There are no more signs of our leaving this place than the day we were relieved from duty. We on duty but are waiting for marching orders. Some think that we will stay here all winter. Others say that we are going to Alexandria and are waiting till the barracks now being built there are completed. We have a camp fire. We are stringing wires and stuff and that's all the barracks they are building, so I reckon not as I spoke.
>
> guard and dress parade every day and battalion drill in the afternoon. Yesterday there was a stinging cold wind and it made us look blue at drill, but as we drilled on the double quick we did not freeze.
>
> I should like to hear how Alex came out of Hooker's fight, but I don't borrow trouble as it does no good. He may not have been in the fight but may have been detached for other duty. If Meade is successful in this campaign the war will be short. The last ditch men of the rebels will soon have a chance to fight.
>
> I don't know whether I can get a furlough this winter, and if I can, think I had better not take one. It don't pay to go home on a ten days furlough. I have seen some men back from home who said they were glad to get back here. We are having an easy time and ought to get fat. Once in a while we have johnnycakes and butter, the butter bought with Co. savings on rations. We have had baked bread pudding two or three times. Milk is cheap here, ten cents a qt. Milk women come twice a day. How did you pass Thanksgiving? Did you have a good dinner? Our Co. had mashed potatoes and milk gravy for dinner, as much as could eat. It is seldom that we get potatoes.
>
> I must tell you about that pocket book. Don't know but the person that sat next to me at dinner picked my pocket. Still I might have pulled it out of my pocket. I did not expect to lose the check but got another one for it. It lost on Saturday between 10 A.M. and 2 P.M., and on Sunday evening the man returned me the pocket book and check and said there was no money in it when they found it. Perhaps they kept the money, but they said somebody must have found it before they did. There is quite a trade in checks here, the soldiers selling a $20 check for $18, and the buyer getting the full value. The soldiers sign the name of the person to whom it is allotted on the back. I intended to have gone into the city early Monday morning and seen the paymaster, to find out the no. of the check, then me an affidavit before a justice of the peace and have the Adjutant write on to the Treasurer in N.Y. to have payment stopped on it, and get another check. Some time since one of our Co. lost his pocket book with his money, also a check. He did as I have said and the treasurer in N.Y. replied to the Adjutant that if it was not found 3 mo. from date of loss he would on application at the end of that time, send him a certificate entitling him to draw another check.
>
> I have just seen Mr. Steais who came here to see his brother Pet. He looked seedy and the boys thought he was a hard looking fellow. He belongs to the 113th N.Y. and came from the convalescent camp. Said he was going to get his discharge next week, also said he had not been home for 1½ years.
>
> The Tribune news is old by the time I get it and as it costs 2¢ to send it, I think you had better not send it as I can buy the daily papers for 5¢. I rec'd. butter and R. Vinegar. The butter and cheese are gone, but the vinegar I have not touched, though I think that I had better drink it up as it can't be carried. The short-in berries I tried last night. The cook gave me some sugar so they went first rate. James Lucas had quite a lot of them sent to him.
>
> (Incomplete — from Charles)

## Editorial Notes

Charles and Alexander write on the same day from opposite ends of the war — Charles worrying about his pocket book in Washington, Alexander listing his own name among the wounded at Lookout Mountain. "I should like to hear how Alex came out of Hooker's fight, but I don't borrow trouble as it does no good." He will borrow plenty of trouble when Alexander's letter arrives.
