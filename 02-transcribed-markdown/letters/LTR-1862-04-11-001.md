## Full Transcription

> April 11, 1862
> Camp Niles, Relay House
>
> My dear Brother:
>
> Pretty near time to answer your letter. Don't you think so? Well, I came to that conclusion some time since, but we have been moving about so much of late that I have barely found time to write a few short letters home.
>
> Well — now for the news. I expect our Regt. will not be guarding this road much longer. Lieut. White said this morning that he thought we should have a chance to see the rebels. I hardly know what to think about it. I hope we shall, but from these late great victories I have been almost led to think that the war is about over. Memphis is certainly done for and New Orleans, Mobile, Yorktown, Norfolk and Savannah will soon be. I don't see what the rebels have to hold on to. Their capital, Richmond, will probably soon be in McClellan's hands. It seems strange that Yorktown, once invested by the forces under Washington and DeRochambeau, should again be invested by American troops. The natural supposition would be that if it ever was again beseiged it would be by foreigners. Who would have thought at that time that these same Southerners from whose cities we were endeavoring to drive out the enemy would ever turn against their Northern brethren. The curse of God it seems to me must be upon their undertaking.
>
> How do home matters progress? How does James get along with his studies at West Point? Do you think he will pass examination? Oh! how mortified I should be if he did not. He must put his "best foot" forward. How much sugar have you received from your sugar maker? I hope he will not prove to be a rascal though Mother seems to think he is.
>
> It will soon be time for Company drill, after which comes dress parade. We have a very fine band of music. Do you remember "Gussy Acorn"? I presume you do. Well, he is a member of the band. His real name is Augustus Hammel. Guess I had better bring my letter to close, as I have not much more time to write. I am very well. Luther also is well. I took a prisoner down to Baltimore the other day. Went down and came back on the cars. I had a pleasant little jaunt. Direct as usual.
>
> Your affectionate brother,
>
> Alexander
>
> P.S. We are to have Battallion drill instead of Company. (Battallion drill means Regimental.)
>
> ---
>
> April 15, 1862
> Relay House
>
> My Dear Mother:
>
> Received your very welcome letter Sunday and was very much pleased to hear from you. What do you now think of the "Rebel Cause"? I think it is pretty near "gone up". There is only one thing that causes me sorrow — i.e. I am afraid I shall never have an opportunity to show the rebels how well I can shoot, and how well I can use my sabre bayonet. It seems a pit to see such good weapons lying idle.
>
> We now have battallion drill and will soon be ready for the field. There are four companies here: two near Baltimore, two at Annapolis Junction and two on the road. All the Companies still do guard duty on the road. Between seventy and eighty men are sent our from here each day. The Companies at Annapolis Junction and near Baltimore also have battallion drill. It is the opinion of some that it will not be long before we will be sent to the field. I should feel rather cheap to come home without having been in one battle at least, but from present appearances I am afraid I shall be obliged to. Nevertheless I have this to comfort me. I have done all they have asked us to do, and we have probably done as hard duty as any Regt. in the field. It seems a pity to keep such a fine Regt. as this on a railroad. How I wish our Regt. had been at Pittsburgh Landing. We would have made the sparks fly I can tell you.
>
> One of the 10th Maine Regt. was killed by the cars the first day we were here. Some of our men went as an escort to the body. They fired three volleys, "the farewell shot", over the grave. Our band was also present. It was a very solemn sight. The band (I do not think I am mistaken) went first, after whom came the escort with arms reversed, then the coffin and lastly those soldiers who chose to go. If the 10th -------- had been here the whole Regt. would have probably been present at the funeral.
>
> Wonder how Mr. Timothy Hoyle is: hope he is better. It would be a public calamity if he should die. I think James owes a great deal to him and his brother. Hope he will "put in" and show himself worthy of the confidence placed in him. It is worth a great struggle. Tell James to "put in" if it is only to spite that little Doolittle. I can never forget what he said. Would like to flog him "first rate" and will when I come home if he says much.
>
> Evening    The Lieut. Colonel drilled the Regt. this afternoon (4 companies). I think we did pretty well. He put us through "right well" (as the people say around here.) It is real fun to hear them talk, "A right smart day, I recon" is a very common expression.
>
> My position in the Company is upon the extreme left; quite an important place. The reason that I am there is because I am the smallest Corporal in the Company. I go by the name, among the other Companies, of "the little Corporal". I don't think I am so very small. Do you?
>
> Wonder what you are doing this evening. Oh! how I should like to be there with you all. How my tongue would fly. I expect to be there in two or three months at the most. Oh how I long for my books. Nevertheless I am content. I expect the pay master will be here soon to pay us off. I must pay that pew tax and for those buffalo robes. Tell James to take extra good care of them.
>
> Our Regt. received marching orders, but General Niles wrote to have them countermanded. Our Colonel has gone to Washington, to prevent this, I expect. I hope we shall move. If we do I think we will go to Fort Corcoran. Good night. The drums are beating for roll call. Good night and pleasant dreams to you all. I sleep upon the soft side of a board and like it.
>
> April 18th. We are to be paid off today. Pretty near time I think as it is nearly four months since we received pay. We will be paid up to the first of March today. We will be mustered in again for pay soon as two months pay will be due us the first of May. I can send home but ten dollars this time as lending that five dollars has "played the dragon" with me. I never thought I was such a fool. I bought a pair of boots last winter (winter — I hardly realise we have had winter) of the sutler and paid four dollars for them. They have proved to be a very good pair. I have them yet and had them tapped the other day. (I pay for the boots out of the money we receive today. On account of lending that five dollars I have the little expenses of six months to pay. I have all my money matters out in "white and Black". The Lieut. has just handed me a letter from you I know. Now for reading it.
>
> We are still under marching orders and I should not be at all surprised if we went to Fortress Monroe. The 10th Maine formerly stationed here is now at Harpers Ferry. One thing is very certain — we will never stay here. Your letters do me real substantial good. I have a very formidable package of them. I received a letter from Willie Savage a day or two since. They seem to be prospering. Luther is quite unwell today. I hope is not going to be sick, if he is I shall take extra good care of him. The ten dollars I send home I give you. Pay the pew tax or do what you please with it.
>
> Your affectionate Son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell.
>
> ---
>
> On same sheet — Dear Brother James:
>
> Am very glad you have at last condescended to write me an anyways decent letter. What good spirit prompted you to do it? I hope you may often have such promptings. You say you will need ninety or one hundred dollars to get an outfit for West Point. You are welcome to all I possess. We shall be paid off again soo and I shall try and send at least twenty dollars to you. You need have no fears for me. If my life and health are with me I shall have an education. Cost money what it may I shall have one.

## Editorial Notes

Alexander's nickname — "the little Corporal" — is a Napoleon echo (whether intended or not). His position on the extreme left as the smallest corporal shows the precision of 19th-century linear formation. The DeRochambeau comparison (second siege of Yorktown, now by Americans against Americans) shows Alexander's historical consciousness. The military funeral for the 10th Maine soldier is the most detailed ceremonial description in the collection: band leading, arms reversed, three farewell volleys. Alexander's frustration at garrison duty is reaching its peak: "I should feel rather cheap to come home without having been in one battle at least." His financial sacrifice for James — "you are welcome to all I possess" and "cost money what it may I shall have an education" — shows an 18-year-old corporal prioritizing his younger brother's West Point future over his own needs.
