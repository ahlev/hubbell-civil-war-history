## Full Transcription

> March 11, 1862
> Baltimore
>
> My Dear Mother:
>
> I received your very welcome letter this morning. By each mail we hear long accounts of the "snow blockade". Have you supplies enough in the "old castle" to last you a month or two. I do not think "the blockade" will last much over that time. If it does write and let me know and I will send up several regiments of our sunshine, on the approach of whom I think "old Winter" will withdraw his forces.
>
> I am on guard duty today. Among other duties I generally have to see to the wood chopping (for the guard house. The guards are obliged to do this). One of the men was handcuffed for refusing to do his share of this work the other day. I am not obliged to chop, but only to see that it is done. We are making some pea soup for dinner. It will be the first I have had since I left home. We generally have very good appetites, very good, very good.
>
> Thousands of troops have passed here during the last week, (three of four thousand on Sunday) and thousands more will probably pass. Nearly all the troops are from N.Y. Two Regts. are now at the "Union Relief." They will probably be sent to Fortress Monroe by water. It is said that as many as thirty steamers arrived here the other day for troops. I suppose the reason is they can not transport them fast enough by railway. The Malone and Potsdam Regts. passed through here the other day. A large number of the "Sixtieth" were there to see them. All the N.Y. troops have the Enfield rifle. It was reported last night that four hundred thousand Union troops were on the move yesterday for Manassas and Richmond. I expect some hard fighting will be done. I wish our Regt. was there. I do not like the notion of Henrys fighting there alone. Not at all, at all!
>
> I received the long expected letter from Aunt Laura and Cousin Nellie day before yesterday. I am very, very, very, very, very much obliged to you for those postage stamps.
>
> Mch. 12th: You told me, Mother, to keep at a safe distance from drunken soldiers. That I cannot do and do my duty as Corporal. I have not much fear of my being hurt. Yesterday one of the men ran the guard (he was a little sprung). The Sergeant ordered me to take a file of men and bring him in. After him I went on double quick. The fellow ran towards the glass works and jumped in a door and found himself covered. He started to run back and came "hat upon me". (immediately drew my sabre.) He came to the conclusion that it was best for him to stop, but refused to "budge" an inch. A thrust or two in his stern from my weapon brought him to terms. When we reached the guard house he refused to go in. Myself and men immediately seized him and gave him "a shove" that sent him in "in a trice." He threatened all things. It gave me very little concern. I suppose he can go home now and say he has shed at least a little blood in his countrys cause.
>
> The other day the Sergeant ordered me to take hold of him of a drunken fellow. The fellow seized me by the hair, but nevertheless he went in. One of the men some time since (he was "tight") seized a rifle and snapped it at the guard. Fortunately it was not loaded. Such things as these do not happen very often. I hope you will never mention these events as the boys hold it as a point of honor not to mention anything disparaging to men or company and it would make me trouble should it be known that I wrote any such thing.
>
> Our Company is one of the most orderly in the Regt. A Corporal with a file of armed men is comparatively safe. I think I shall get a revolver. I do not know what may happen. I do not apprehend ever being seriously harmed — if ever. The men are afraid to touch a Corporal as they know it would be a sore job for them should they be courtmartialed. I charge you never to say anything concerning these things.
>
> I understand that there is upward of sixty boats here awaiting troops. I suppose you will hear ere this reaches you of the wonderful display of Southern chivalry at Manassas. They have evacuated the place without firing a gun. The combined armies of the rebel generals Price McCulloch and another Major General (I have forgotten his name) have been defeated by our troops.
>
> I expect to be home soon. I will not get the revolver if you need the money at home. Keep a boy by all means. I will pay him. Please write soon. Tell James I shall write him soon and Charles too. I received an Observer also yesterday. It seems good to get hold of a home paper. I wonder if Charley could send a county paper to send occasionally without too much trouble. Love to all.
>
> Your affectionate son,
>
> Alexander
>
> P.S. I am very very very sorry to hear of Mr. T. Hoyles illness. I hope he will recover soon. He has been a good friend to us.

## Editorial Notes

This is one of Alexander's most action-packed letters. The March 12 continuation contains remarkable NCO narratives: drawing his sabre on a drunken soldier, bayonet thrusts to force compliance, being seized by the hair, a man snapping a rifle at the guard. Alexander's explicit request for secrecy — "I charge you never to say anything concerning these things" — shows the tension between honest family communication and military honor culture. The irony: 164 years later, these private incidents are being transcribed as historical documents. The troop movement data is extraordinary: 30 steamers, 60+ boats, 3-4,000 troops on a single Sunday, 400,000 reported moving. Baltimore was the funnel through which the Peninsula Campaign was being staged. The Pea Ridge reference (Price and McCulloch) shows how fast news traveled — the battle was March 6-8, and Alexander knew by March 11-12. McCulloch was killed at Pea Ridge, but Alexander only knew of defeat, not death.
