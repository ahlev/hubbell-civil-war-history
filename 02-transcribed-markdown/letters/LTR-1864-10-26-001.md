## Full Transcription

> October 26th, 1864
> U.S.A. Hospital No. 3
> Nashville, Tenn.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> You can have no idea how anxious I am to hear from you all and I have at length determined to have a letter directed here. So please, immediately on receipt of this, write me a long letter telling me all the news since last August — the date of the last letter I received from you, and do not fail to send it by return mail. I think I shall be here ten (and perhaps more) days longer.
>
> I would not take the oath prescribed in the New York voting law and have consequently lost my chance of voting. Could I have voted I should have cast it "square and fair" for Abraham Lincoln. The majority of soldiers will vote that way.
>
> Direct your letter simply to Nashville, Tenn. In case I am not here when it arrives I can write to Postmaster and have it sent on.
>
> Love to all.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

"Could I have voted I should have cast it 'square and fair' for Abraham Lincoln." The political journey that began with "my heart still yearns" for McClellan in April is over. Alexander Hubbell, the man who admired McClellan's generalship and the Democratic convention's "right spirit," will not vote — but if he could, it would be for Lincoln.

The NY voting oath he refused is a footnote to a larger story: a man who will fight but won't swear on someone else's terms. The oath of September 21, 1861 — "three years or the war" — was enough. He doesn't need another one.
