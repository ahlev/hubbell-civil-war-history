## Full Transcription

> June 13, 1862
> Camp within five miles of Richmond
>
> Dear Charles:
>
> I was very glad to hear from you and know that all is going on well at home. The weather here is very warm when it does not rain. I wish we had more of your pleasant weather and less rain, but we have to take it just as it comes. It very often happens that we have to be on picket when it rains.
>
> You must have heard before this of the battle of Fair Oak. It was warm work there for a time. You can have no conception of how exciting it is to be in a battle. No one can except those that have been in. A perfect streak of reed placed all along the line. The firing reminds me very much of a number of men felling trees, only much faster. The ground shakes all around. All I thought of was to keep up in the ranks and load and fire. We very often had to load while advancing. I never knew till after we came out of the woods that any one was wounded or killed. There was a rebel Colonel and Gen. killed that night. Some say that there were three hundred of the rebles killed by our firing. There were two other Regiments engaged at the time besides ours. I did not go over the battle ground afterwards, but the ground was covered with the dead. I fired 40 rounds.
>
> A secesh prisoner said that the road between here and Richmond was lined with carriages with ladies and gents. It was told them that Beauregard was going to capture six thousand Yankees and they came to look at them, but instead of looking at the Yankees they saw their own wounded pass and took the carriages to carry them in. They found it rather hot work. But I must stop, I do not feel well. I have the disentery very bad. I have not received a paper for some time. The secesh have thrown a good many shells over here this morning. One man was killed by a shell this morning at the hospital. I get perfectly disgusted with so much swearing in the Company, but I have to put up with it. I get lonesome now and then. Excuse all mistakes as I do not feel much like writing.
>
> Yours truly,
>
> Henry

## Editorial Notes

This is the collection's most significant combat document. Henry's account of Fair Oaks achieves what he says it cannot — giving someone who wasn't there a conception of battle. The sensory details are extraordinary: fire "like men felling trees, only much faster"; ground shaking; loading while advancing through woods; only learning afterward that men had fallen. His terse "I fired 40 rounds" is the most powerful sentence in the collection — a 23-year-old farmer from Champlain, New York, fired forty times into other human beings and doesn't elaborate. The Confederate spectators anecdote — ladies and gentlemen in carriages expecting to watch Yankees captured, instead carrying home their own wounded — was a story that circulated after both First Bull Run and Fair Oaks. Henry's dysentery is ominous: the swamps of the Chickahominy were breeding grounds for disease that would kill far more men than combat. His closing — "I get lonesome now and then" — is heartbreaking in context. He is five miles from Richmond, sick, under daily shelling, and the war he expected to be over by now is growing worse.
