## Full Transcription

> Feb. 23, 1862
> Baltimore
>
> My Dear Mother:
>
> Today is the holy sabbath. An unusual quiet prevails throughout the quarters. I attended divine service this morning at the Sharp St. M.E. church. Heard a very good discourse on death. Was there before service had commenced and went into the sabbath school. I enjoyed this part of the service very much.
>
> I received a letter from Henry today. He seems to be in pretty good spirits. Also one from Corporal Perkins of the same Co: a messmate of Henrys. I should like to answer these letters, but cannot for want of a postage stamp. When I received those stamps Charles sent I was indebted for quite a number of letters and 'used up' my stamps pretty fast.
>
> I feel some delicacy in sending home for more for I remember when I came home on furlough you said the reason you did not write me oftener was because you had no stamps. The next time I send home money I shall appropriate a certain sum for you to purchase stamps for you and myself. Be a good plan, won't it?
>
> I wish Mother, you would write Aunt Laurie and Cousin Nellie and tell them for me that she says in her first letter to me 'I most sincerely hope you will be punctual and Nellie and I will do our part. Try us Alex.' I have tried them and 'found them wanting'. They generally wait four — five — six weeks after they have received my letters before they answer them. When they write, they write good long letters and apologize plentifully and promise to do better in future. You tell Aunt Laurie I have been holding an 'indignation meeting' with myself for some time, but have finally concluded that she has 'turned rebel' and will no longer own her 'soldier nephew'. I am afraid that nose of hers (you know she says it is crooked — I never could see it) has led her astray.
>
> My dear Mother, you complain of the shortness of my letters. I hardly know what to write. I wish you would ask questions for I hardly know what would interest you. Let some of the questions be standing ones; and I will report concerning them every time I write. I wonder if Charley could send me a Plattsburgh paper now and then.
>
> If I had stamps I could send him Baltimore papers very often. All of you at home please write me often. Mother, if such a thing be possible, can you send me some stamps. I am sorry to ask it.
>
> So Albert Savage is dead is he? I never was more astonished when I read in your letter that Henry had thought a great deal on Albert Savages death. When did he die? I received a letter from Willie a few days since and he said nothing about it. Nelhinch wishes to write and as I can think of nothing more of interest to write I will close.
>
> Your affectionate son, Alexander.
>
> P.S. Love to all. I will write Frances soon. Sent "blos up" Aunt L. too hard. She might get vexed and never write me again. Luther sends his respects to you all. I am enjoying excellent health. Luther says I am getting fat.

## Editorial Notes

Alexander's sabbath observance at Sharp Street M.E. Church shows his maintained religious discipline at 17. The postage stamp shortage reveals real home-front hardship — Mother couldn't afford stamps to write, and Alexander proposes allocating his next pay specifically for stamps. Corporal Perkins (Henry's messmate, Co. D, 34th NY) writing to Alexander shows inter-regimental correspondence networks. The Aunt Laurie "turned rebel" joke is Alexander at his playful best. Albert Savage's death — news from home — astonished Alexander; Henry had "thought a great deal on" it. The P.S. shows Alexander's self-awareness: he worries he "blos up" Aunt Laurie "too hard" and she might stop writing entirely. Luther saying Alexander is "getting fat" — at 17 — suggests adequate rations in Baltimore garrison duty.
