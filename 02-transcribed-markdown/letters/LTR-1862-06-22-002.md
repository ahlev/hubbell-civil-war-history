## Full Transcription

> June 22, 1862
> Camp near Richmond, Va.
>
> Dear Charles:
>
> I was very glad to hear from you. We are suffering a great deal from the warm weather. Have not had much rain lately. We have a very good spring half a mile from Camp. The water is very cold. Also a very good place to bathe, but not to swim. I sometimes hardly know what to do some days. I get very lonesome — lay around in my tent. I would rather be at home hard at work, be sure of coming home at night and not be routed up, but I shall have to make the best of it.
>
> I gave a description in my last letter about the battle. You may be shure it was very exciting, but I was not frightened for we were pushed right in head first. We came to a bayonet charge just as we came to the woods. The rebles, it seems, got on to their knees. It was their intention when we came on to them to fire into us, but we halted after we had gone some ways and fired into them. We all fired very low. Our Colonel said, from the effects of our fire. Three hundred of the rebles lay dead and wounded. I should not wonder if it was so. I did not go over the field after the fight for I did not feel like it. I saw one rebel who was shot through the head. Our boys went over afterwards and said the ground was covered. I felt to tired myself. I never thought of the danger till after I was out.
>
> I forgot to mention in Mother's letter about Capt. Reich. He had a dishonorable discharge, that is it was read so on dress parade, but I do not take it as such, but so it was read, so do not mention it around. I will just state how it was. On dress parade the Companys were changed by order of the Gen. The Colonel told Capt. to take his Co. in such a place, but he would not, when his sword and belt was taken and he himself put in irons so that he has his discharge. I do not think myself that he was sorry to leave the Co. and the service. I overheard him say to Lieut. Ransom sometime before that he wished he was out of the Company. I for one and most of the Co. wishes that he was back. If you see him tell him I wish he was back again for I miss him very much. I am almost tempted to desert sometimes. I feel so lonesome. If it was not for the name of it, besides Mother being all alone, but I must keep courage a while longer. I must stop for this time. Write often.
>
> Your aff. Brother,
>
> Henry
>
> P.S. I shall be obliged to send this letter without a stamp as they are not to be had. The old Dr. has left that used to frank. Please send me a few stamps. There are not any around here. In the battle our Company was the third from the head.

## Editorial Notes

This is the most psychologically revealing letter in the collection. Henry's confession — "I am almost tempted to desert sometimes" — written to his younger brother, not his mother, is extraordinary. Civil War soldiers rarely admitted this in writing; desertion was punishable by death and shameful. That he qualifies it with "for the name of it" (meaning the disgrace) and "Mother being all alone" shows how thin the thread holding him is. The Fair Oaks bayonet charge adds new detail: the rebels kneeling for a close-range volley that never came because the Union troops halted and fired first. "I never thought of the danger till after I was out" — the classic combat experience of dissociation. The Capt. Reich story — refusing an order, sword taken, put in irons — shows how the army punished officers while enlisted men like Henry had no such option. Reich wanted out and engineered it; Henry wants out and cannot. Three days from now, the Seven Days Battles begin.
