## Full Transcription

> July 26th, 1862
> Camp near Washington, Va.
>
> My Dear Mother, Sister and Brother:
>
> I received your letter yesterday and was glad to hear from you. I also received one from Frances a few days ago which I have not had time to answer. I was very glad to hear that she had returned home and was still in "good spirits."
>
> Now as to Silas and Sid, I have a very poor opinion of such men. They are what I call fireside patriots. They are brave enough at home but let them come out here and you would see them whine. The idea of men at home finding fault while we are doing the fighting! There is a great deal of talk now about drafting. I hope they will draft every man that is able to carry a musket, and I believe they will.
>
> Charles asks me if the military life is as miserable as it is represented. Well, I will give you my opinion. It depends entirely upon the disposition of the individual. If a man enlists and is determined to make the best of it, he will get along very well. But if he enlists and goes to grumbling about everything, he will be miserable. For my part I make the best of everything and am contented.
>
> July 27th. Our First Lieutenant died last night at ten o'clock. It was a heavy blow to us all. He was a Christian Man and died like one. Sergeant Sterns said he "never saw so happy a death." Two of the regiment are being buried this morning. Luther is in the hospital, sick. I am in excellent health and am one of the hardiest men in the company. "Little Doolittle."
>
> I was very glad to hear good news of Henry's conduct. Mother has no reason to blush at conduct of at least two of her sons. Love to all. I will try and give you an account of my adventures in my next. Remember me to Mrs. B.
>
> Yours with affections,
>
> Alex
>
> P.S. I saw Luther this morning. He said he felt better. I hope he will recover soon. He is not dangerously ill.

## Editorial Notes

Alexander addresses all three siblings at home — Mother, Frances, and Charles — in a single letter, the first time he's written to all at once. His contempt for "fireside patriots" Silas and Sid is fierce: "They are brave enough at home but let them come out here and you would see them whine." His philosophy of military life — "depends entirely upon the disposition of the individual" — is both Stoic and slightly self-congratulatory, though earned. The death of the First Lieutenant on July 27 is the first death in Alexander's immediate company circle. Sergeant Sterns's remark — "never saw so happy a death" — is a common 19th-century trope: the "good death" of a Christian soldier, peaceful and resigned. Two more men buried the same morning. Disease, not combat, is killing the regiment at Washington, Virginia. Alexander's "Little Doolittle" is enigmatic — possibly a soldier nickname, possibly connected to the Mary Doolittle whose letter he received on July 14. The P.S. about Luther — "He said he felt better. I hope he will recover soon. He is not dangerously ill" — is Alexander's daily vigil. He checks on his sick comrade and reports home. "At least two of her sons" — Alexander counts himself and Henry as the ones who've proven themselves. James is at West Point, Charles hasn't enlisted yet. The pride is qualified: two out of four.
