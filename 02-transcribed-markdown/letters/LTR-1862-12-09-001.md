## Full Transcription

> Dec. 9th, 1862
> Champlain, N.Y.
>
> My darling precious pet:
>
> I could have cried when I found no letter in the office from you this morning. You must remember my loneliness and write often. My heart is breaking for Henry. I fear I cannot long bear up under the crushing sorrow. My hope of ever hearing from him is expiring and I am consumed with grief.
>
> Now, Alex, tell me who of "the boys" saw him after he was wounded. I often hear that he was seen, but have never heard who saw him. You have seen the company and know. McDonald didn't see him. I am afraid that the reports are not reliable. He may he was mortally wounded. Scott is here. He says they tell him that he was seen & two rods from him on[?] one knee. He might have been wounded in a vital area and fall in that way. Write me all they said — what they said — and who. It turns out that McDonald has heard false rumor. With regards to his being alive.
>
> The prisoners are nearly or quite all released before this and there is no Henry
>
> [page break]
>
> among them. Scott told me today that the missing were probably mortally wounded. Dennis Hayes was buried by strangers before the Company returned to the pit — and Henry may have been. Oh — Alex — can you get a discharge and come home. I cant live alone — I must have one of you. In my old age it is hard to be left alone. How much I think of you this cold, cold weather. I never retire at night to my worn room and set without thinking of you, my darling boy, and pray God to protect you. You must have suffered during these cold days. Have you good flannel shirts & drawers. The Carter boys are having good heavy shirts made[?] here to be sent on next week. They are made of two or three widths[?] since. You must be nearly clad. Your health is precious — take care of it. Charley must feel the cold as he has not yet become (?).
>
> Tomorrow I intend to get pony shod. The price for shoeing now is $1.50. I must get a whit (?). Fastened in the sleigh as some one has stolen the one on it. Examples of this kind that I intend to, bring Henry painfully to mind. These are things that he always saw to. Monsieur Cushas days are numbered, and I have all this to do and no to. I found Joe amusing himself this morning whipping pony while he was tied up in the stable. He got a lecture this he will remember awhile.
>
> Alek Douglas writes that it is thought they are going to North Carolina. I have not. I should be very sorry to have Charles go so far away. Do you hear from Charley. I want you to tell me more about yourself — what your new duties are and all about yourself. I wish the South would be wise ant accept the proposition for "gradual emancipation and end this terrible war. I want you home. I must have you. Has Luther returned? Remember me kindly to him. How soon do you expect this war will be over. In his years? Less than thirty. I have no opinion in the mat [?]. Pollard is going to be married this week to Mary Jane — a Mrs. Brinkerhoff old girl. Do you attend church & prayer meeting? I hope you will avail yourself of the priviledge whenever you can. "To avail all the means of grace we can get. You are surrounded by temptation & and have a great deal to Mrs. buffet and need grace in no small degree and you must go daily to the fountain for a supply.
>
> This is the last month of the year 1862. You know what Henry wrote you on New Year day. He says, "who knows Alex, whether you and I will be here at the close of this year." O, how my heart bleeds. It was almost a prophecy. I cannot endure to look at his letters or pictures — it is too much for my poor shattered nerves. Don't forget to answer my questions relative to him.
>
> God bless & protect you, my precious boy, is the daily prayer of
>
> Your aff
>
> Mother

## Editorial Notes

The year 1862 is ending, and Mother knows it. She quotes Henry's January prophecy — "who knows whether you and I will be here at the close of this year" — and the words have become an epitaph. She cannot look at his letters or pictures. The pony needs shoeing and there is no one to do it. Someone has stolen the whip from the sleigh. These are the small failures that mark a household falling apart.

The demand for information is now urgent and specific: "tell me WHO saw him." Mother has reached the point where she needs names, testimony, direct evidence. She is conducting her own investigation from Champlain, cross-referencing reports, identifying contradictions, and now explicitly rejecting McDonald's account as "false rumor." She is also, for the first time, asking Alexander to get a discharge. "I cant live alone — I must have one of you." This is no longer about the war. It is about survival.
