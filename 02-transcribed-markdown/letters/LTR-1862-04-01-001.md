## Full Transcription

> April 1st, 1862
> Relay House
>
> My dear Mother & Brothers:
>
> Wonder what you are doing this evening? How I should like to be at home with you. Wouldn't I have a jolly time, eh? But that cannot be. Here I am and here I must be. Nevertheless, I am content. I expect the war will soon be over and then hurrah for home. Oh! how good it will seem to see you all.
>
> I see by today's papers that a great battle is expected at Corinth in the northern part of Mississippi (state). That the rebels are concentrating at that point and are commanded by General Beauregard. He and Grant will now have an opportunity to prove which is the greatest general. I wonder if we shall have another display of "Southern Chivalry" like that at Manassas. That "one Southerner is sufficient for five Yankees" has been clearly proved, hasn't it? (vice versa). I wonder if Toombs doesn't think it is time for "every woman and child" to apply the torch. Wonder if Aunt Mary still "pities us". Wonder is she is as ready as ever to go out and assist her valorous husband. Wonder if he has been drilling on the "double quick time too. O Tempora, O Mores. Guess this rebellion will end considerably like the great Catiline conspiracy, only I think Reff instead of perishing in honorable fight as old Cat. did, shall probably take to his heels.
>
> April 2 (11 o'clock) — This is generally the time for our inspection, but our commissioned officers are on duty today and therefore we will have no inspection, much to my gratification as I have been drilling since nine and am rather tired. I have but very little leisure to write our time is so fully occupied. Our boots, belt buckels, rifles and clothes must be faultlessly clean. At six (AM) roll call, (seven) breakfast, (eight) guard mounting, (nine) uncommissioned drill, (ten) squad drill, (11½) inspection, (12½) dinner, (2½) company drill, (4½) dress parade, (6) supper, (9 PM) roll call, (9½) "taps" (when every light must be put out and every tongue still.
>
> Master Charley, you ask me if we eat on tin plates. Well — we eat on tin plates and drink out of tin cups. Eat pork and beans, beef, potatoes, soup, &, &, and have a good time generally. Our old Colonel is very strict. He sent in seven this morning because themselves or accoutrements were not properly cleaned. We soldiers earn our money.
>
> April 5 — Received your very welcome letter yesterday. Had a hearty laugh over your upset in one of those "Cahoes". Don't wonder James felt mortified. Suppose he felt like "charging bayonet" on the laughing occupants of the other sleigh. I should. So Grandmother made a speech, eh? Well, Well, well, what did she have to say? Something about her two "dear lambs". They seem to have been lambs a good while and I think always will be. Wonder if "Sidy" has got over his fear of a gun yet. Wonder how he would like the locks of our sabre bayonets. Guess he would need the "smelling bottle", eh? So he is about to carry on both farms. I verily believe the fellow lacks common judgment. It seems to me it would take a very extraordinarily smart man to do that, and make any profit.
>
> I see by a letter that Fry Clarks daughter wrote a fellow in our Company that Silas and Sid were sold some two hundred acres of land near Riders to Clark, and he (Clark) thinks he has made quite a bargain. He paid them eight hundred dollars and what he had left of the Rogers farm. Eight hundred dollars won't last them long. I feel mad when I see what belongs to me going in this style. Guess Grandmother will find in the end that she made a bad bargain when she cheated us out of our rights. It don't pay to marry widows and orphans or anybody else. There is a just God who regards such things.
>
> April 6 — Wonder what you are doing now. It is now a little after five. Well, guess Charley is reading the Observer, Mother some book from the parish library, and Master James I guess is off in some corner reading Harpers magazine, little scamp. Oh! how I should like to spend this Sabbath evening with you. But I trust I shall soon be home to spend a great many with you. I have heard today that the 34th has gone to New Orleans. Oh! how I curse the fortune that keeps our Regt. Here. It is wrong for me to talk so. It is all for the best.
>
> So Professor Bass is coming back, eh? Well, well. I should like to see the old croaker. Wonder what he thinks of the war now. I believe the old fellow is half rebel. Well — guess the Champlainers can do no better than to get him. He is a pretty good fellow after all, though he does like money pretty well, a little too well. I have just been reading your letter of the 30th inst. Oh! how vividly it brought before me the many pleasant Sabbaths I have spent with you all. You spoke of the dinner table — wonder when I shall be there to take dinner with you again. Soon I hope. I feel anxious — very anxious — about Henry. I don't like the notion of his fighting there alone. I think I could draw sight with my rifle on Octove or Julio with a very steady hand, and I don't think conscience would trouble me much when I pulled the trigger. Oh, how I should like to have our regt. sent to Mobile and have an opportunity given us to charge upon the "Southern Chivalry". These Southerners don't seem to like our "cold steel" very much. I should like to see Uncle Knapp leg it and if Aunt Mary was with him I guess she would leg it too and bless him because he did not run faster, eh? What do you think about it? I don't believe I can every forgive our Southern connections. I shall always be ashamed of them.
>
> I shall try and have my likeness taken as soon as we are paid off. Cousin Nellie has sent me hers. It does me good to look at her. You wish me to have my picture taken with my cap off. Don't believe I can as I have had my hair cut close to my head and don't look remarkably well with my short fur.
>
> I have just heard that our Regt. has received marching orders. Joy, joy, joy.
>
> April 7 — Guess I had better send this off. My health is very good. I think I have received all your letters. Please write often and you, Master James, why can't you write me often. When you do write your letters are short and sweet. Write longer ones.
>
> Yours affectionately,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

This sprawling multi-day letter is the most revealing of Alexander's personality. The classical Catiline reference (comparing the Confederacy to the Roman conspiracy) shows his debating-club education. The Southern relatives — Uncle Knapp, Aunt Mary, and the mysterious Octove and Julio — are the Hubbell family's Confederate connections, and Alexander's rage is unfiltered: "I could draw sight with my rifle on Octove or Julio with a very steady hand." The land swindle (200 acres sold for $800 by Silas and Sid, with Grandmother complicit) reveals a family property dispute simmering beneath the war correspondence. The daily schedule is the most complete in the collection, documenting every hour from 6 AM roll call to 9:30 PM taps. And the final line — "Joy, joy, joy" at marching orders — is Alexander's most ecstatic moment. After months of garrison duty, the 60th NY was finally moving.
