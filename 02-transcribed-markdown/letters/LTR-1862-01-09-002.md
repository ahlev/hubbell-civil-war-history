## Full Transcription

> Jan. 9th, 1862
> Camp McClellan
>
> Dear Frances:
>
> As I was writing to Mother I though I would add a few lines to you. Jan. 10th: It is very warm and pleasant today and very (??). It can not last long. I burnt all the letters that I received from home. They took up to much room in my knap-sack. Besides if I should go over into Virginia I should not want all of those letters with me. I received a letter from Nellie and Aunt Laura. I am glad for your sake that you are going to teach in Montreal this winter.
>
> Please excuse this writing and all mistakes. I have forgot all about the Journal. I do not think I can make it very interesting. I hope to write another and a longer letter next time.
>
> I remain your aff. Brother,
>
> Henry
>
> P.S. Please burn all my letters.

## Editorial Notes

The most consequential sentence in this letter is the postscript: "Please burn all my letters." Had Frances obeyed, the entire collection we are now transcribing would not exist. Henry's reasoning — knapsack space and not wanting letters "if I should go over into Virginia" — suggests both practical concerns and an awareness that letters on a body were a vulnerability (personal information, morale-damaging family details). The fact that the family preserved his letters despite this request is the foundational act of archival preservation in this collection. Henry's own burning of incoming letters means the home-to-front correspondence is lost — we have only the soldiers' outgoing side. The confirmation that Frances is teaching in Montreal (not just "going to Canada") resolves the earlier ambiguity and explains Alexander's later address to "Miss Fannie Hubbell, Longale, Canada."
