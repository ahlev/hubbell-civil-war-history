## Full Transcription

> Oct. 10th, 1864
> U.S.A. Hospital No. 3
> Nashville, Tenn.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> You perceive I have again changed my quarters. I was sent from Chattanooga last Wednesday and reached here Friday morning. The hospital I am now in is a very good one and I am getting along famously. I asked the doctor this morning to let me go to my regiment. He said he would see about it. I think I shall go in a day or two. My eyes are much better.
>
> The political excitement is running high. Lincoln seems to be the favored one. When we were coming from Chattanooga a vote was taken in the car I was in. Thirty-seven voted for Lincoln and five for McClellan. I tried to get a furlough while at Chattanooga but did not succeed. The doctor did not do right, I think. He gave a furlough to a fellow that had the scurvy not near so bad as I did, and when I asked him he said "he would like to but, but, but, etc."
>
> I hope this war will be over before long and then I shall need no furlough. I am anxious to get back to the regt. so I can get hold of the lots of letters there are there for me. I expect there must be a lot. James and Charles have written me. There is little of interest to write. There is to be a flag raised and a political speech to be made at Fort Gillam today. If possible I shall go. Please write often. Love to all. Give my regards to Mrs. B.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

37 to 5 for Lincoln on a hospital train from Chattanooga to Nashville — the straw poll of a war nearing its political resolution. Alexander reports it without the anguish of his April McClellan letter. The shift is complete. The "war man" votes with the war president.

"He said 'he would like to but, but, but, etc.'" — the doctor's refusal of a furlough, rendered in Alexander's sardonic shorthand. The army's unfairness is a constant theme: a man with less scurvy gets a furlough, Alexander gets "but, but, but."
