## Full Transcription

> April 18th, 1862
> In Camp within three miles of York Town
>
> Dear Charles:
>
> I was very glad to receive a letter from you. I believe I owe you two letters, therefore I shall write a long one this time.
>
> We are beginning to have pretty warm weather now. I find it so when marching with my Knap Sack. A good many have thrown their over coats away, and some threw blankets, shirts, pants, but I have kept mine thus far and mean to. We have no Co. cook, but each one has his ration dealt out to him. A great many would not have it otherwise. Half of the Co. went in for having their rations cooked, the other wanted it cooked. For my part I would rather have a Co. cook, but we all do our cooking, boil our coffee in our tin cups and cook our rations the best way we can. I do not like it at all.
>
> Last Sabbath I worked all day fixing roads. 25 were detailed from our Regt. It was for the purpose to have the artillery and rations to come through. Last night our Regt. was called up into line. I expected as much as could be to have a little skirmishing and have a chance to discharge my piece, but disappointed, but we may have before long. I hear so many stories I do not know hardly what to believe, but as far as I can understand the rebels tryed to capture our battery but were repulsed with some claughter. I think this must be true for a person that was in the action gave the report. One of the Vermont Regts. was in the fight. I heard that out of one of the Co. only twenty- were left, but I do not know how true that is. I also heard that the rebels lay all around dead. I have been keeping a Journal now for some time — have about thirty dates. I shall send you a copy one of these days. Nothing livens me up more than to hear the firing cannon and musketry. The rebels will ketch it before the war is over.
>
> I have received two pair of shoes from the Government, not counting what I received in Albany. I have had bill of clothing but that was not put in what I received in Albany. My clothing and everything that I have received since I left Albany amounts to $20.58. Of course what we do not receive now we have when the war is over — also rations. I believe every Co. has a fund, as far as I understand it.
>
> I get very lonesome sometimes and also very tierd, and I should but wonder, lazy now and then. It is a very idle life for me. All I have to do is to look after myself. I find it difficult to keep clean when marching. Yesterday I had a good wash all over — felt first rate after that. I enjoy pretty good health now. The cannon is fired every half hour at night to let the enemy know, I suppose, where we are. Last night we lay out with our rifles ready for them any time. I should not wonder if you would like soldiering for a while, but you would soon get tired of it. Look out for the Sugar man. I almost wish you were a soldier. Write often. I remain your aff. brother,
>
> Henry
>
> Could you send me a few postage stamps. I have no franked envelopes.
>
> April 24th — I shall have more to write when we get into York Town. I did not have a chance to see the Fort at Fortress Monroe. I saw a steamer that the rebels disabled last spring called the Cheese-box. I saw no very large ships.
>
> Saturday — the 26th: The rebels made an attack this morning quite early, but I think they must have got the worst of it. Our sharp shooters pick off the rebels pretty fast. Lately one of our sharp shooters got wounded pretty bad. There have been several of our men wounded. Rains about all the while here. Makes us quite cold.

## Significance Flags

### Historical Significance: `major`

> Writing his brother Charles from camp three miles outside Yorktown, Henry relays his first secondhand battle reports — a repulsed rebel assault on a Union battery and a Vermont regiment so badly hit one company was left with "only twenty." Carried across eight days, the letter closes with a rebel dawn attack and a sharpshooter shot down. "Nothing livens me up more than to hear the firing cannon," Henry confesses — a green soldier still hungry for the fight he hasn't yet faced.

## Editorial Notes

This letter spans 8 days of the Siege of Yorktown — the most sustained combat-adjacent writing in Henry's correspondence. The Vermont Regiment fight (one company reduced to ~20) is the first real battle report, and Henry's reaction is telling: "Nothing livens me up more than to hear the firing cannon." The April 26 rebel attack with sharpshooter activity shows the siege intensifying. The "Cheese-box" (USS Monitor) sighting connects Henry to the most famous naval engagement of the war. His clothing account ($20.58) documents the government supply system. The journal is now at ~30 entries and Henry promises to send a copy — a tantalizing document if it survived.
