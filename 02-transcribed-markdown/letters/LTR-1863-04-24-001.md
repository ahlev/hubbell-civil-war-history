## Full Transcription

> April 24, 1863
> Aquia Landing
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I mailed a letter to you this morning and forgot to mention a very important matter in it. That is about a watch. Will you get me one? I need one very much. Get one with a double case. Any other would be a nuisance.
>
> I saw one of the 34th yesterday. He inadvertently said something about Henry that pleased me very much. He said that when the boys went off and plundered anything he would not take any of it and would reason in this wise: "You stole that, and if I should take any, I would be as bad as any of you for the partaker is as bad as the thief." He said he was very quiet, seldom spoke to anyone unless spoken to, and a great deal of the time, and was a real good fellow. Sometimes they used to try and bother him, but he would pitch into them with a will and soon make them desist. Said he himself had got some hard knocks. Love to all. Remember me to the Captain and to Mr. and Mrs. Copeland and all the boys.
>
> Your Aff. Son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
>
> P.S. Sutler wrote Frances a short time since. Wants to know if she got the letter. He sends regards. Is fat and husky. Tell Frances if she don't answer it soon, I shall tell her when I come home again. Tell her that I am waiting patiently for the spirit to move her to write me.

## Editorial Notes

The Henry portrait is the letter's gem — a brief, vivid sketch from someone who knew him as a comrade, not a brother. Henry the non-plunderer, the quiet man, the principled reasoner, the unexpected fighter. "You stole that, and if I should take any, I would be as bad as any of you for the partaker is as bad as the thief." This is the voice of a man shaped by the stone castle in Champlain, by his mother's morality, by whatever schooling produced a farmer-soldier who could argue ethics in an army camp. Alexander treasures it — "pleased me very much" — because it confirms that his brother was the man he believed him to be.
