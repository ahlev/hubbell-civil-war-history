## Full Transcription

> May 23rd, 1864
> Camp on the Bank of the Miss. River
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I was much surprised yesterday to see James walking into my tent. He came up from N. Orleans on a transport. He has been in the Camp of Distribution about a week. He enlisted in N.Y. for this regt. It will be pleasant for us both to be in the same regt., together. We will tent and draw rations together and can see each other.
>
> I rejoined the army May 2d and it was March 3d when I left it. By being absent I was saved a long March which I think I could never have stood. We left Alexandria, La., on the march from Red River May 13th and I can tell you I missed this marching more than once. This being obliged to march when you are so tired that you can scarcely move your legs does not suit me. There was no chance to ride because the ambulances were full of sick and one of them were mounted on brokendown skeleton horses. I kept up all the way here till within two miles of this place when I fell out and took my own time. I reached camp about half and hour after the regt. We have rested two or three days since we started. On the way here for half a day we marched in line of battle to meet the rebs, the cavalry skirmishing with them in the front. We halted for a short time while the heavy cannonade was going on and expected every minute to be in a fight, but the rebs skedaddled and we came on. They say we are to take transports on the river to move from here. I hope this is true for I have marched enough for the present. I have rec'd two newspapers lately from Cousin Mary. Alex Douglass is commissary sergt. and received $21 a month. He issues the rations to each company. On our way here we passed parts of our letters that were in the mail that the rebs captured. Your letters were scattered all around. When I rejoined the regt. I wrote you. Did you receive the letter?
>
> James has not shaved since he left West Point and has quite a pair of whiskers. He looks well and healthy. I asked James why he had not written you. He said that he wanted to get to his regt. first. Says that you knew he was going to enlist because he told Alex.
>
> I took a swim in the river today and felt a good deal better after it. The weather is very hot. "We are going to have hats this summer instead of caps." We arrived here yesterday forenoon. Will leave here soon, I think. Hope we will go on boats. Our Col. told his regt. when drawn up in line that he had news that Richmond was taken and he believed it to be authentic. We gave some lusty cheering them. There are rumors here of terrible fighting up north with large loss of life on both sides, but that we were victorious.
>
> Hope you and Frances are well. Please write soon and all the news. Alex Douglass told me that something was the matter with Sid. Either someone was dead, or something burned up, he forgot what it was. What is it?
>
> From your aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

James walks into Charles's tent — the reunion is understated in Charles's telling but profound. James hasn't shaved since West Point and has whiskers. He enlisted "because he told Alex." The youngest Hubbell brother, with his demerits and his mother's anguish, is now in the same regiment as Charles. They will tent together, draw rations together, share the war.

The captured mail scattered along the road is one of the collection's most poignant images — Mother's letters to her sons, torn open by Confederate soldiers, left in the Louisiana mud. Charles walks past the fragments of his own mail.

The false rumor of Richmond's capture and the lusty cheering — the army's hunger for the end, seized upon and believed because they need it to be true. It wasn't.
