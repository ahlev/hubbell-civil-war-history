## Full Transcription

> Dec. 26, 1862
> Camp near Alexandria
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I was surprised yesterday to receive a visit from Alek. Day before yesterday Capt. J. B. Ransom visited our Co. He told me then Alek stood in his first place. I asked him if he could let Alek come & see me. He said he couldn't if they did not leave, but said I must come up & see him. I told him I would if I could. His reg. is at Fairfax station, 15 miles from here. The whole army corps is there. I did not think of the railroad as a means of going there & thought of but two ways of my going there on foot — or hire a horse. But I had no money to hire a horse & would have to get a furlough of two days to walk there, so I was afraid I would not see Alek, but he came here on the cars free of expense. He arrived here at 4 p.m. yesterday & left at 11 a.m. today. He looks well, though the eruption he had on his face has not entirely cleared out & also a little whiskers. He is also rather fat. I intend to get a pass & take a railroad ride to see Alek if I can. I gave him half of the butter & currants, some of the g. snaps, a little
>
> [page break]
>
> yarn & a d. needle with the two fingered mittens & a towel as he had nothing to wipe his face with, also a testament. While at Fonda I bought a matt of kid gloves so I have both mittens & gloves. Alek says he does not have enough to eat always. Says he each man cooks for himself. Says he lives on fat pork & hard crackers, though if they have a tin like they fry it on the plate. Also says that he is in a log barracks coffee drinker though they do not get much sugar to put in it. They now have dried apples. He says they live in the open sir as their tents are only large enough to sleep in. Two sleep in a tent.
>
> We went down to the city & went through it, but not a thing could we buy as neither of us had a cent of money. There is nearly 6 months pay due Alek & nearly 4 months due me. I dont know when we will be paid. Uncle has poor I guess. The men in our reg. are being trusted by the sutler. I shall not trade with him as he sells to dear. He has good butter, but it is 40 cts. a pound. Some of the men in our tent buy it. Alek says he owes the sutler $11.00 — had to buy as they could not get enough to eat.
>
> He received the box Dec. 22nd. Alek & I are much obliged. The currants & butter will go well & the snaps have & will go well for a short time. Quite a number of boxes have been received in our Co. & a great many in the reg. One box in our Co. staid 3 or 4 weeks in Washington & some of the eatables are spoiled. Did you pay my charges on the box ? if so how much. Snakes stuff filled up about half of the box. I have sent quite a number of acquaintances since I have been here. Albert Cook has been here. He M.& N.M. there are at the parole camp at Georgetown. Albert looks well, was going to try to get a 20 days furlough to go home. He rode a horse which was one of forty he & Bright took care of, as he told it. I was on guard & did not see much of him. J. Douglas & Albert went into the city. Cyrus Kellogg has been out of the hospital only a short time & is not at the convalescent camp. He has been here several times to see his uncle Norman. The convalescent camp has been moved to about 3 miles from here. There is a shanty there dug out the side of a hill. We had a good fire obtained by asking for a few sticks from each wagon that passed. I hope you and Mother went to Grandmothers as I think you would have had a good time.
>
> Col McMartin said to us the other day that he thought we had no more months here among 800 men that[?] there could be at home among the same number of men. Said we a great deal better off here than we would be at the front in smaller tents. Thought we would stay here this winter. Alek said it made him feel bad to see how comfortable we were & then think of their own camp. Lieut. Burdick came with Capt. Ransom into our Co. I see Burdick quite often. He is a pleasant man. Frances old friend S. Tombe is going to be discharged. He was to be drummer, but has not drummed any. I suppose she remembers what a handsome man he was. Well, he looks the same as ever. Made some particular enquiries about Frances said she went to school when he did & did him a favor when boys were reciting to Mr. Dixon in the teacher class. Tombe became confused & could not answer his question when Frances went on with the lesson as if he had answered right. He told me he was invited to Mrs. Nodiah Moores to a party when C. Walkerton boarded there. Mrs. Vanberton[?] urged him to come & see Charles as he was very polite. He & his brother are both married.
>
> Where does Mrs. Brinkerhoff stay this winter. Has she said you the rent? Mount Vernon is about 8 miles from here. Some of the men have been there. I dont know as I care enough about it to walk there. We had no drill Christmas, nothing but dress parade which we have every day unless it rains. Some of the men went to the Catholic & some to the Episcopal churches on that day. Our 1st Lieut. McGuire is a Catholic & takes a squad of men every Sunday to the Catholic church.
>
> [page break]
>
> At dress parade we have to have shoes blacked, clothes brushed & look as nice as we can. I staid in camp Christmas & at 4 p.m. Alek came. We have 3 men standing on a barrel (each on a barrel) to punish them for having to much of a Christmas. The slave men in Alexandria is a terror to our men. The worst ones are showered. One of our men was on city guard & put down his gun for a few minutes to get a drink when an officer came along & took it his gun. He was shut up in the slave pen & showered.
>
> Washington is about 8 miles from here. It is difficult to get a pass to go there. I think we can get none unless it is on business. How do you & Frances get along? You know I suppose your ears are not jarred by the noise in the stone castle unless it is by the mice. You had better keep money going. It still do you good to take sleigh rides. How does Frances like going to the post office? I wish she would write & tell how the examination went off & all the rest of the news. I hope you still feel well.
>
> Savagham did not write that letter that you read. It was written by Tromby. There are 12 men in our tent & only three can write. Sergeant Welky, Jim Lucas & myself. Those men that cannot write send more letters then I do & receive letters very often. I have written some letters for them. They think as I do that I am not a very rapid letter writer. Some of them all give you a letter to read & tell you to answer it. They dont tell you anything to write so you write what you have a mind to. The majority of the ignorant men are in our tent. I think that the other tents have not so many ignorant men in each tent as there is in ours.
>
> Please write soon & all the news.
>
> Your Aff. Son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell.
>
> ..... 30 .....

### Connections to Other Letters in Collection
- **Brothers reunited:** Alexander and Charles see each other on Christmas Day 1862. Alexander came by train, stayed overnight. This is the first meeting between any surviving Hubbell brothers since the war began (Henry being the only one who met Alexander, and is now missing).
- **Alexander's deteriorated conditions:** Charles describes Alexander's deprivation in detail — fat pork and hard tack, no sugar, $11 sutler debt just for food, tents barely big enough to sleep in. Alexander's own letters are more stoic; Charles reveals the reality.

This is a window into how Civil War letters were produced — many were dictated to or composed by literate comrades. ## Editorial Notes

The last letter of 1862 closes the year as it opened — with brothers trying to stay connected across the war's geography. Alexander rides the train to see Charles on Christmas Day, arriving at 4 PM, and they share butter and ginger snaps like boys dividing a picnic. Charles notices Alexander's face is still broken out, that he's "rather fat" (a charitable interpretation of illness-related bloating?), and that he felt bad seeing Charles's comparative comfort.

The literacy passage is the letter's sociological gem: "There are 12 men in our tent & only three can write." Charles, the self-conscious speller who once asked his mother to "send me a list of my bad spelling," is now the letter-writer for nine illiterate men. "They dont tell you anything to write so you write what you have a mind to." This is the 19th-century version of ghostwriting — Charles composing other men's love letters, family updates, and requests, filling in the content from his own imagination because the men "dont tell you anything." The majority of Civil War letters may have been produced this way, and this passage is one of the few that documents the process explicitly.
