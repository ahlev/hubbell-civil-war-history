## Full Transcription

> June 30th, 1862
> Camp near Sharpsburgh (?)
>
> My Dear Mother:
>
> We have just been mustered in again for pay. On looking over my accounts I feel a little blue. My expenses have been quite heavy. When we left Harpers Ferry we took only our overcoats and blankets in our knapsacks and did not receive the baggage we left until a few days since. How long I wore my shirt I am ashamed to tell you. Only I would say that I threw it away when I took it off. I purchased two shirts and two prs. of socks which cost me three dollars and a half. All my writing materials were completely spoilt by the wet and I have been obliged to supply myself anew from the sutler. A Corporal has often a need of a watch. One day while in Winchester I was sent with a guard of twelve men to the Academy Hospital to guard the sick rebels, some of whom were getting to be pretty strong. I borrowed a watch from one of our company with a cracked crystal. It fell to pieces during they and two hands were knocked off. I was obliged to have it repaired which in Winchester cost three dollars, of which I payed two, etc. etc. etc., all of which count up. When we are paid off I shall be able so far as I can now see to send you at least ten dollars to go to Charlotte with. I feel mad when I think how small it is. A truce to this subject as it is one not very pleasant for me to contemplate.
>
> We are now encamped within about three miles of Strausburgh in a small fine woods. We have made ourselves little shanties of boughs which answer very well to keep of the sun and wind, but when it rains we fasten up a blanket which keeps off the greater portion of the wet.
>
> July 1: July 1! I can hardly realize it. I thought last spring that I should be home by this time, certainly. Well, well, I am content to stay. There is the bugle for drill. The capture of Richmond has just been reported here. I hope it is true. I hope and pray that Henry has again come out unscathed. One of the boys heard the dispatch (to Genls. Siegel and Slow) read. It said that our forces had captured Richmond after three days fighting and that thirty thousand men were left on the field. I only wish our Regiment could have had a share in it. From present appearances I should judge that the whole army in this valley will move on soon. Our Capt. thinks we shall have some fighting soon now that Richmond has been probably captured. He thinks they will try and hold this valley. I shall be ashamed to meet Henry unless I have been in two or three battles.
>
> I am enjoying excellent health. When we are paid off again I hope I shall be able to send home at least twenty dollars. Love to all. Remember me to Mrs. Brinkerhoff. I feel very anxious to hear from James.
>
> Your affectionate son,
>
> Alexander F. Hubbell
>
> P.S. Do not forget to remember me to Frances and tell her I hope to see her before long.

## Editorial Notes

The dramatic irony of this letter is devastating. On July 1, 1862, as Alexander writes excitedly about Richmond's "capture" and prays Henry has come through unscathed, the Army of the Potomac is fighting the Battle of Malvern Hill — the last and bloodiest of the Seven Days Battles — in a desperate retreat from Richmond. Henry's regiment is in the thick of it. The false dispatch that Alexander heard was a garbled version of the fighting around Richmond; the casualty figure (30,000) was roughly accurate, but the outcome was the opposite of what was reported. Alexander's shame at not having fought enough battles — "I shall be ashamed to meet Henry" — is painful in light of what Henry is actually going through: sick, dysentery-ridden, tempted to desert, under daily shelling, now being driven back through swamps.
