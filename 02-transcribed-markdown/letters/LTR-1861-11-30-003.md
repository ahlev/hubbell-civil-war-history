## Full Transcription

> Nov. 30, 1861
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your letter last eve. It is so cold now that I can not write very good, but I shall do the best I can. I am sorry to know that you have been worrying about me. You must not. If I am sick I shall let you know, you must let your mind rest at perfect ease about me. I admit we suffer with the cold some, but that cannot be helped. Some of the boys have fireplaces in their tents and others have stoves. Have not any in our tent. The stoves cost between three and four dollars. When we go into winter quarters we shall probably have log houses, but we shall have to build them.
>
> I received two papers from home, one was the Observer and the other, New York Times. Very glad to receive them. In regard to my having a furlough to go home. I do not think I can get one at present. There are three or four before me now. I understand that two can only go at one time. I should like to go home if I could. The war may be over before I go. I hope it may. I came off from picket Thanksgiving day at two o'clock in the afternoon.
>
> I am, of course, perfectly willing to pay my portion in regard to the taxes, or anything else. Very glad to do so. I had my picture taken, but could not get it standing. It cost me a dollar, the case and all. You wrote about sending me some mittens, but if I had a pair of woolen gloves they would be very acceptable. I bought a pr. of buckskin, but they are not very warm.
>
> I sometimes think that I did wrong to leave home, knowing that you will need me this winter, but when I do get home I shall stay. I think that you got along very well last summer in regard to farming. That is, I think the boys worked it very well although the crops did not turn out very well.
>
> I have no more time to write now. More next time. Write often.
>
> I remain your aff. Son,
>
> Henry

## Significance Flags

### Historical Significance: `routine`

> Writing from Camp McClellan in cold so sharp he can scarcely hold his pen, Henry begs his worrying mother to "let your mind rest at perfect ease," reports he stands third or fourth in the furlough queue, and came off picket Thanksgiving day at two o'clock. He confesses he sometimes thinks he "did wrong to leave home" with the farm needing him that winter, yet pledges that "when I do get home I shall stay."

### Emotional Intensity: `moderate`

## Editorial Notes

Henry's most emotionally complex letter — guilt about leaving the farm ("I sometimes think that I did wrong to leave home"), reassurance of Mother ("you must let your mind rest at perfect ease"), and the quiet promise "when I do get home I shall stay." The Thanksgiving detail (picket duty until 2 PM) is poignant. His willingness to pay taxes from camp shows continued responsibility to the household. The $1 photograph is potentially a surviving artifact.
