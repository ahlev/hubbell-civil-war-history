## Full Transcription

> June 3rd, 1863
> Camp near Aquia Creek Landing
>
> Dear Mother and Sister:
>
> I received your welcome letter of May (something) today, and am much pleased that you have at length heard from me. Tell me when you next write whether it was my first or second letter you received. I am glad you have heard from Lt. Col. Redington, and sorry for your disappointment. He is a very fine man, and I like him much.
>
> So, Miss Fannie, you are quite curious to know how Colonel Redington found out your name. I can explain it in no other way than that when looking over the mails he has seen your address on different letters I have written you. It is also quite likely that Capt. Ransom in his conferences with the colonel relative to me thought it necessary to mention your name. You ask me to describe Colonel Redington. He is about the minimum height, rather slender and very erect. He is quite a man of business — a great bargainer, which will go to make us a hero, you know. But what is most of all, he is a man of courage. In the last battle at Chancellor he was singing away at a great rate, using the screams of the shells as they passed over his head as a key to pitch his voice on. He did the same at Antietam as they were passing over a hill a key to pitch his voice on.
>
> So the young gents at Champlain are displaying their patriotism by burning boxes and powder (their mothers are very imprudent; powder is a dangerous thing), and ringing bells. How generous of them to do so much for their beloved country — still it is asking almost too much of them. "The evening is damp and the smell of powder very pernicious to the health — it would have a tendency to give them the ague."
>
> June 4th — We have orders to march with three day's rations. We leave our camp as it is. It is probably merely a reconnoissance. Luther's father is here.
>
> Yours in haste,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
>
> P.S. Will you now and then send me a stamp?

## Significance Flags

### Historical Significance: `major`

> **COL. REDINGTON PORTRAIT — SINGING DURING SHELLFIRE.** Alexander describes Col. Redington singing during Chancellorsville, using shell screams as a "key to pitch his voice on." Same behavior at Antietam. This is one of the most vivid character portraits in the collection. **ORDERS TO MARCH JUNE 4** — three days rations, leaving camp. This is the beginning of the movement toward Gettysburg (July 1-3).

### Emotional Intensity: `low`

> Light, teasing. The powder passage mocks Champlain boys' "patriotism."

## Editorial Notes

Col. Redington singing during shellfire — using the screams of shells as his key — is the kind of detail that belongs in literature, not letters. Alexander records it with evident admiration: "what is most of all, he is a man of courage." The image of a slight, erect man singing into the noise of battle is the collection's most vivid portrait of military leadership.

The June 4 march orders are historically momentous: Lee is moving north, and the Army of the Potomac is beginning the pursuit that will end at Gettysburg. Alexander calls it "merely a reconnoissance." In four weeks his regiment will be on the Gettysburg battlefield.
