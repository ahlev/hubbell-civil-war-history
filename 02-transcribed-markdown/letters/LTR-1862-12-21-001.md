## Full Transcription

> Dec. 21st, 1862
> Camp near
> Fairfax Station
>
> My Dear Dear Mother & Sister:
>
> I must "hurry up" and write or my fingers will soon be so numb that it will be wholly impossible. How I long to see you and when that will be God only knows. I wrote you a few lines the day before we left the Ferry. Since then we have done no small amount of marching. We went up Loudon Valley to Hillsborough, from there to Leesburg, thim to Fairfax Courthouse, from there to Dumfries, five miles from Aquila Creek and twenty-four from Fredericksburg. The orders were countermanded, I am one, and we marched back to this place, leaving a detachment of the Regts. there and another small force at Bolts ford over Bull Run Creek. We reached here day before yesterday and will probably leave soon.
>
> Please thank Mrs. Hoyle surely for me for her kindness in sending those papers. Tell her that they were the sources of the greatest enjoyment to myself and comrades. The Plattsburgh paper was especially welcome. Say for me that we had the abolitionists quite as badly as does the editor of that sheet, and that we would much prefer fighting them to the Southerners.
>
> You ask me, Mother, about my new duties: I could hardly tell you on paper, that you would understand, only that my responsibilities are much greater. Mother, did you ever receive the letter of two sheets I sent from London Heights telling you of my visit to the Camp of the 34th and all I learned there concerning Henry? I presume you did. I do not remember who they were that said they saw Henry. The boys (a number of them) said they saw Henry grasp his leg, take a step or two, and threw him self down. I think there is a strong probability of his being yet in a rebel hospital.
>
> The first of January there is six months pay due me and I do not see much prospect of my getting it very soon as the treasury department does not seem to be in very good order. I am sorry for I should like to send you some money to get winter necessities. Mother, if you can without inconveniencing and denying yourself send me a little — and only a little — money, it would be a great comfort to me, and when you write if you will see and then put it in a stamp it will enable me to answer your letters much more readily for it is very difficult to get them here.
>
> Love to all. I will try and write again soon. I am enjoying excellent health.
>
> Your affectionate son,
>
> Alex. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

The eyewitness passage is the letter's core: "the boys said they saw Henry grasp his leg, take a step or two, and threw himself down." This is the final frame of Henry's war — wounded in the leg, trying to move, going down. Combined with McDonald's terrain description (open woods, no undergrowth, visibility 1/4 mile), the picture is clear: Henry was wounded in the open, went down in plain sight, and could not be recovered because the regiment was overrun or retreating. What happened after he went down — whether he was captured, died, or crawled somewhere — is the question the collection has been circling since September.

Alexander's anti-abolitionist outburst is notable for its vehemence: he would "much prefer fighting them to the Southerners." This from a man who wrote poetry about mountaintop sunsets and quotes his mother's prayers under fire. The Emancipation Proclamation is fracturing Union solidarity, and Alexander — six months unpaid, sick for half the fall, his brother missing — is not in a generous mood.
