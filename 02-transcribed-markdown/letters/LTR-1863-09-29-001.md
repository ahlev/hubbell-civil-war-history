## Full Transcription

> September 29, 1863
> Capitol Hill
> Washington, D.C.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I wrote you a few days since, but will write you again to let you know I have seen Alex. He requested me to inform you about him. Last night while our Co. were drawn up to go out on dress parade who should I see but Alex. He told me that he had only a few moments to stay, so the orderly sergt. said I could fall out. But as I could not get a man to leave camp till after parade he staid in our barracks a short time and then I went with him to the depot, but we found the train had left. We were both annoyed but went to an office close by to get transportation. Alex was told to wait and jam on the next train which would be along in about an hour. Alex said this was the first chance he had got into, but his Capt. would make it all right. His Capt. gave him his permission to leave. Well, after waiting some time, who should come along but two fellows of Alex's Co. They said the train was about one mile down the track where it had stopped and they took the chance to leave and go into the city. The train left the depot to prevent the soldiers from going into the city. Well, we reached the train and I staid there some time before the train started. Alex was glad to get the take to his co. He looked healthy, but is much sunburnt. Has a moustache sprouting out. I saw Amos Luther. He was well and had not a vine in his mouth. He is now sergt. The train was very long, a freight car with benches in them. A sentry was placed before the door of nearly all the cars, but they were not strict, and a good many of the men went into the city. I bet several going back to the arts. Alex is on his way to Tennessee. The 11th and 12th corps and I don't know but another one are going to reinforce Rosecrans. I asked Alex how he liked going off there. He said that he did not care where they went, that while they were riding they were not fighting, and he had gotten sick of Va.; it was nothing but a waste. He was glad I was in so good quarters.
>
> I left Saturday A.M. with 75 of our regt. and we took 282 rebel prisoners from the old Capitol prison down to Point Lookout Out which is near the mouth of Chesapeake Bay. This Point L. is not far from Fortress Monroe. There are about 3000 prisoners confined there. We went on a steamer and had a fine ride down the Potomac and Bay. Arrived here Sunday night. All the N.C. Officers not on duty went on this trip as there were not men enough. Corporals were as thick as blackberries. The other squad of men started a little earlier in the morning than we did on a foot 350 deserters to Gen'l. Meade's Headquarters. They came back a little before me and I was told by some of them that the 11th and 12th corps were preparing to go to Tennessee.
>
> The rebels were poorly dressed and had no uniforms, dressing as they pleased. Some of them were quite ragged, others had on near gray jackets drawn from their quartermasters. One of them said they were allowed $160 a year for clothing, but I don't think this is much as their currency has depreciated so much. They don't think much of their money as they gave a man for our Co. a $10 bill. One of them said green-backs were good anywhere they went. Said they rec'd $24 a month wages. I heard them talking about a good many taking the oath of allegiance. They did not like it, but they said their army was all the better for getting rid of bad men. A good many of the rebs have taken the oath and are then sent to Philadelphia not to come south of that during the war.
>
> Lieut. Knapp of our Co. has gone home to Moorestown on leave of absence. Alex D. has not gotten his furlough yet. I heard not long since that Capt. Rich brought 1100 conscripts from N.Y. to Alex. Our Co. had just come off drill one day and were in line when Capt. Rich came and saw the Co. He did not have much to say to it. He then went back to N.Y. All the men like Capt. Rich.
>
> Have you heard from James and Alex lately? James has owed me a letter for some time, but I suppose he is very busy in the reason. My health is good and I hope that I will stand the winter. But we may not have much of one, but may ride. How is your health and Frances and how does the world go with you both? Hope that you have wood enough to keep warm this cold weather. The weather is warm and pleasant here now. The cold rains are to come.
>
> From your aff. son
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Cross-Reference Notes

### Connections to Other Letters in Collection
- **Tennessee transfer:** After Chickamauga (Sept. 19-20, 1863), the Union rushed reinforcements west. The 11th and 12th Corps were sent by rail — one of the largest troop transfers of the war. Alexander's 60th NY is heading to the Western Theater, where he will fight at Wauhatchie and Lookout Mountain.

## Editorial Notes

The brothers meet by chance at a Washington depot — Alexander is heading to Tennessee with the 12th Corps, Charles is at his Capitol Hill barracks. "He did not care where they went, while they were riding they were not fighting, and he had gotten sick of Va.; it was nothing but a waste." Alexander's exhaustion with Virginia — after three major battles there — makes a theater transfer feel like relief.

The Point Lookout passage provides a rare Union soldier's observation of Confederate prisoners: ragged, depreciated currency, some taking the oath of allegiance. "They said their army was all the better for getting rid of bad men" — the prisoners' own rationalization of desertion and surrender.
