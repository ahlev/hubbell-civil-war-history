## Full Transcription

> Sept. 25, 1863
> Capitol Hill
> Washington, D.C.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I read your welcome letter of Sept. 21 yesterday, also one at the same time from Mary McNeill. By the way, I don't exactly fancy corresponding with a cousin I have never seen. She says the three families there are all well. What families are they? Max John, Charles and Wm. McNeill? How near do these families live to each other?
>
> About that pension. I would not apply for one if I were you. With three sons in the army it looks bad if you should draw a pension.
>
> Part of the duty of the regt. has been taken off; since then, which is four or five days since, the regt. has drilled every afternoon and there is Co. drill in the morning from 6 to 7 before breakfast.
>
> Not long since the orderlies' call was beat and me soon learned why. It was made by our orderly detailing 12 men and 2 corporals from our Co. to go off somewhere. Each Co. furnish their proportion of men and they are to start at 6:30 A.M. tomorrow with three days rations, overcoats and rubber blankets. We guess that our men are going to guard some prisoners from here to somewhere else. As I just came off guard this morning it is not my turn to be on duty next, so I do not go on this excursion and I am glad of it, for the nights are cold and it might rain.
>
> Alex Douglas is still here, but expects to have a furlough soon. He has a 20 days sick furlough, but a good many here say he will never soldier in this regt. again. Think he will get his discharge.
>
> I don't know as I want anything except some woolen socks. Uncle Sam charges .35 for socks. I have had only one pair from him and they are not like home made socks. Two prs. will be a great plenty for me. I think that we will stay here this winter. A good many of the men think so. We have two stoves in our quarters and things are being fixed up comfortably.
>
> A young fellow's wife came the other night. He belongs to our Co. On being wakened up in the night he said to his wife when he saw her: "What in hell did you come for!" The Lieut. got up and gave her his bed. She is going to cook for the two Lieuts.
>
> James gave me a letter. I suppose that when he is not studying he does not feel like writing. I know I should feel so if I were in his place. You misunderstand me about demerits at West Point. James said in his letter 100 demerits in six months after the second half year, but 150 the first and second half years. Does Alex have any good reading? Then I was down to see him he had a .25 novel to read. Not very useful reading.
>
> I have learned to play checkers. Am quite interested in the game. But I shall not learn to play cards. It is a waste of time if nothing worse.
>
> I hope Mrs. Brikerhoff will live with you this winter. It will be a great deal more pleasant for you. I am afraid Frances will have a hard time this winter in getting to school. Have you got any fodder so that you can keep pony? I think Frances ought to have a resting spell. She teaches too constantly. She is a dear, conscientious girl. She ought to enjoy life as she goes along. Hoping that you both will take the world easy I will close. Please write often.
>
> From your aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell
>
> P.S. I leave in the morning on another detail. This makes two squads that leave tomorrow. I take two days rations.

## Editorial Notes

"What in hell did you come for!" — the soldier woken by his wife's arrival at the barracks. The lieutenant gave her his bed; she became the officers' cook. The anecdote captures the improvisational domestic arrangements of garrison life — wives, laundresses, and cooks living at the margins of military camps.

Charles's pension advice reveals the family's pride: even with Henry dead and three sons in service, applying for a pension "looks bad." The Hubbells would rather manage their debts than accept what they see as charity.
