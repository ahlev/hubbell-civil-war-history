## Full Transcription

> [Beginning missing]
>
> You see I am going to write you a long letter while I have the chance for we shall probably be "heels over head" in the din and turmoil of the fight in a day or two. The "Johnnies" don't like the way Sherman fights. They say he comes around on the blind side of them and they have to "up and git." One old lady down here expressed great wrath at our proceedings. She said the Yanks came and made three lines of fight in her yard and tipped over a barrel of ashes — she would not have taken two dollars and a half or any sum of money for those "ashes" "And that is not all" she said, "They had a man killed and went off and wouldn't kote him off with them."
>
> (Both)
>
> The first night we came here we (60th) were the skirmishing Regt. of the Brig. and were close to the rebel lines. They seemed to be in a terrible stare. We could hear them talking and shouting and coughing all night long. Once they called out "three cheers for Genl. Hardee" and you thought the setup a — mmh well, I cannot describe it. It was so different from the hearty cheer of our troops. It was something very like a wail — and a sorrowful wriggle. I suppose that old traitor Hardee was passing. If any of the 60th could have clapened an eye on him he would have fared ill, but the woods were too thick. Genls. Hooker and Geary were out on the skirmish line and brought out the 13th N.Y. Battery, but for some reason they did not seem to think it expedient to open on the Rebs and the battery was sent back. The next day they commenced fixing a place for that battery and several others, but we were ordered to another part of the line and the work was suspended. The 14th Corps (Genl. Logan) now holds that part of the line. We are further to the left. The right of our Corps connects with the left of the 15th. The 14th Corps (Genl. "Alonzo") connects with our left, I believe. The feeling of animosity between the eastern and western troops is dying away and we are getting to be pretty good friends. We, the 20th Corps, are known among the western boys as Hookers Regt. or Hookers star men. (You know the badge of the 20th Corps is a star.) Of all Sherman's generals, Hooker, I think, is the most feared by the rebels. They thought he was killed a short time since and great were their rejoicings. He was also reported to us to be killed, and you may be sure I felt pleased when I saw the old fellow riding along as sound as a dollar the next day. It was Genl. Barker that was killed.
>
> You can tell M. H. Cook that the Eastern troops are considerably above par in this department and that old "fighting Joe" is one of the biggest toads in the puddle.
>
> Please thank Mrs. Boyle for the papers she sent me. I got them more regularly than the letters. I was very much pleased with the speech of Genl. McClellan which she sent me. It has the right kind of ring to it. I was very much pleased with the way he spoke of the officers of the regular army who had proved traitors to their country.
>
> I hope James will write me soon. I have written him.
>
> Alex

## Editorial Notes

The old lady's ashes are Alexander's finest found comedy — she lost two dollars and fifty cents worth of ashes and the Yankees "had a man killed and went off and wouldn't kote him off with them." The collision of domestic economics and military death, told in dialect, is devastating.

The rebel cheer for Hardee — "something very like a wail — and a sorrowful wriggle" — is Alexander's most evocative description of the enemy's demoralization. The contrast with "the hearty cheer of our troops" is propaganda, but the wail is observed with a novelist's ear.

Hooker as "the biggest toad in the puddle" and the rebels' "most feared" general — the 20th Corps pride is at its peak. Within weeks, Hooker will resign in a dispute over the command of the Army of the Tennessee, and the 20th Corps will get a new commander. But for now, Alexander rides the crest of "Hooker's star men."

This letter closes PDF 6 — the first half of 1864. Alexander is near Kennesaw Mountain, fighting daily, writing between battles. Charles and James are at Morganza, Louisiana. The Atlanta Campaign has weeks of brutal fighting ahead.
