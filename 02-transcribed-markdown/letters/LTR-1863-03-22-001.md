## Full Transcription

> March 22, 1863
> Aquia Landing
>
> My dear Mother and Sister:
>
> To you, Miss Francis, I will first address myself. I received a letter a day or two since from Mother — and none from you, but then you are, I suppose, perfectly excusable, as I believe you generally write upon the inspiration of the moment, or as you say, "when the fit is upon you." Would that these inspirations or "fits" came more often upon you.
>
> Well, has S. P. Hubbell, counsellor-at-law, held forth in the old Academy yet? How he did not say his brother was afraid of a gun. He is certainly a mighty man of valor and a great orator. I have heard him make some wonderful speeches about "the destructiveness of your hens" and "the unruly propensities of your cow and heifer," etc., etc. They were highly entertaining. Did Silas have anything to say about "the hens and heifer" or "who tramped through his oats and grass?" But laying all jesting aside, I hope Silas will do well — and more than well.
>
> How does Mr. Simons and the Misses Crowly prosper? Hope Mr. Simons will soon have a better opinion of the Potomac army.
>
> And now, my dear mother, I will now say a word or two to you. I am just about having my hair cut. It makes me think of the times when you used to box my ears because I would not hold my head still, and tell me that I was perpetual motion. I am half inclined to believe you.
>
> I am very glad Lieut. Ingram has called upon you. I hope you were pleased with him. You tell me, dear Mother, to follow the rules of civilized life as far as possible. Would you insinuate that the U.S. army is an army of barbarians? Shocking! Horrible! I am astonished — shocked to hear such principles confessed and even avowed in the old stone castle. I understand what you mean, Mother, and will try and follow your directions. If you can get an 150 lbs. of sugar for the bush, I think it would be a good plan to let it out. If you see Miss Alma Mcyath, please tell her for me that she will soon have to fulfill her promise for I expect, if I live, to be there in the course of ninety days with an honorable discharge in my pocket, and the war over. How does Mr. "I know" get along? Now, dear mother, you tell me to write you good long letters. You must know this is a difficult thing for me to do. My head and hands fly in all directions and that is one reason why my writing is like hen's tracks or rather goose tracks. My regards to Mr. and Mrs. Copeland and the boys and little girls. Remember me to Mrs. Hoyle and thank as warmly as possible for sending me those papers. They are a blessing to the whole company. Love to all.
>
> Yours affectionately,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
> Company H, 60th NYO
>
> Luther is well. Regards the Captain. He (the Captain) gave me warning of the scolding I may expect from you. I received a letter from him yesterday and am very much obliged.

## Editorial Notes

Alexander's most charming letter — teasing Frances about writing only "when the fit is upon you," mocking Silas's oratory about hens and heifers, remembering Mother boxing his ears during haircuts. The 90-day prediction is heartbreaking in hindsight: he expects to be home with his discharge by late June. By late June he will be marching toward Gettysburg. The mention of Alma Mcyath and a "promise" suggests Alexander has a romantic life that the collection has barely hinted at — a future he's planning that the war may not permit.
