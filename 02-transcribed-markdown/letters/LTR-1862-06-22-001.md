## Full Transcription

> June 22, 1862
> Camp within Five miles of Richmond
> Sabbath afternoon
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your letter this morning, and very glad was I to hear from you. Nothing does me more good than to hear from home. I received the Obs. week ago today. It is very seldom that I receive a paper. I cannot see the reason as the direction is correct. I like the Observer better than any other paper. The New York Herald comes around every morning, but they are 10¢ a paper and I do not buy as I do not have the money and if I had I do not think I should buy them for they cost almost to much.
>
> In my last letter I wrote about my health not being good, nor is it very good now, but I am better than I was. I have not been able to do duty for over a week. I go to the Dr. most every morning. He gives quinine and pills. I have had the disentery very bad. A great many in our Regt. are sick. Our Co. is on picket today, but I did not go as I do not feel able. We have hard bread and salt junk and fresh meat, coffee morning and night, soup for dinner, sometimes rice, beans and vegetable soup. I do not drink coffee, and as for hard bread, I do not eat much of that. It is very seldom that I eat meat. It is what I call pretty tough living. We have potatoes now and then. I can make them go first rate, but we do not have many of them. I shall make up for lost time when I get home. I believe I shall want to sit down and eat nothing but potatoes. The army is provided as well as it can be I suppose. I should like nothing better than to have my discharge, for your sake if nothing more. I have had about enough of it and long to be at home, but you must keep up courage. This war can not last much longer. When we arrive at Richmond they will have to give up. I take a great deal of comfort from the advice you give me in every letter. I know I have your prayers which comforts me very much. I was just thinking what a good dinner I should have if I was at home today, but there are better days coming I hope.
>
> The rebles, from what I see in the New York Herald, are living on half rations and they are getting desperate. Most of them do not like Jeff or Beauregard and would desert if they could. There is a very large force in Richmond, not in Richmond but this side. We can hear their drums night and morning. Yesterday there was quite a skirmish between our pickets and the rebles. I have not heard the particulars yet. Our Regt. was all up in line. We very often are obliged to be up at midnight from their firing. Gen. McClellan is carrying a regular siege at Richmond now, starfing them out. I for one am about disgusted with soldiering, but I shall try to make the best of it. Take good care of your health. Do not go around to much. I am glad you dismissed the boy. He would be a great bother besides the expense. I remain your aff. Son,
>
> Henry
>
> I shall send extracts from my Journal next time and write to Frances.

## Editorial Notes

This letter, written three days before the Seven Days Battles begin, captures Henry at his most vulnerable. The farm boy who marched confidently into Virginia a year ago is now sick with dysentery, unable to eat the rations, unable to do duty, and "disgusted with soldiering." His faith in McClellan persists — "carrying a regular siege... starfing them out" — but the Union army is about to be driven back from Richmond in seven days of brutal fighting. Henry's ration list is the most detailed in the collection and reveals why so many soldiers were sick: hard bread, salt junk (salted meat), and fresh meat that was likely spoiled in the Virginia heat. The journal promise is almost poignant now — Henry keeps promising to send extracts but never does, and time is running out.
