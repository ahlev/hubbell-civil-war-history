## Full Transcription

> Nov. 25th, 1862
> Harpers Ferry
>
> My Dear Mother and Sister:
>
> I received your most welcome letter last evening and oh! how much good it has done me. There is nothing, it seems to me, that gives half the joy that a letter from home does. It is one of the strongest links that binds men from vice. My letters are read, and reread and reread before I get another. Please write often, very often. Your letters do me worlds of good. I feel them tho't although I am here among strangers yet I have those who love & care and pray for me; that I am not alone and forgotten in this world. I take great comfort, Mother and Sister, in the thought that you are praying for me, that I may have grace to withstand all the temptations and allurements I am so much exposed to. I felt when in the battle while the shot and shell were shrieking and whirring around me, as tho I had a Mother upon her knees praying for me and I took great comfort in the thought. It seemed as tho God's arm was wrapt around me and I felt that if I fell all would be well with me.
>
> I have just reread your letter. So Mr. A. Whiteside and E. Doolittle are "paying and hoarding substitutes", eh! I hope they will give them the slip just when wanted. Wouldn't it be rich. The miserable, craven hearted cowards deserve it.
>
> Nov. 27. Today is "Thanksgiving" at home. How I wish I was there. Wouldn't I have a jolly time, eh? Rather think I would. Guess the "old castle" would ring a little, and if the sleighing was good wouldn't "pony's" heels fly. What a nice ride we could have, wouldn't we? Wonder what you are about — believe I can guess. You are about starting for church. It is now some after ten a.m. How I should like to hear the "Thanksgiving sermon" with you and then escort you over to Grandmothers for dinner. That a merry, merry time we would have — but God has ordered it otherwise and I must try and be content. You tell me to get up a nice dinner here among ourselves. Such was our intention but Government has not seen fit to pay us off yet and consequently I must content myself with bean soup and boiled pork and "hard tack" today. Good substantial food but a turkey would be much more to my palate, I must confess. I hope, dear Mother, you have changed your mind and will go to Grandmother's for dinner. If I were there I should make you go or rather persuade you.
>
> You ask me if there is anything I need. There is nothing now that I can think
>
> [page break]
>
> of. We are just now in great need of money, but are hoping that Government will pay us off soon. We shall know definitely in a few days. I will then write. If we are not paid off money will be indispensable and Mother, if you can send me a little — and only a little — money, it would be a great comfort to me, and when you write if you will see and then put it in a stamp it will enable me to answer your letters much more readily for it is very difficult to get them here.
>
> Love to all. Your aff. son,
>
> Alexander

## Editorial Notes

The battle passage is the emotional peak: "I felt when in the battle while the shot and shell were shrieking and whirring around me, as tho I had a Mother upon her knees praying for me." This is Alexander answering Mother's question ("what were your sensations during that terrible battle") with a statement of faith rather than a battle report. He felt protected. He felt her prayers. Whether this was Antietam or another engagement, it captures something essential about how soldiers processed combat through the language of home and faith.

The Thanksgiving passage is the collection's best domestic-contrast writing: Alexander imagining the "old castle," the pony, the sleigh, the sermon, Grandmother's table — while eating bean soup from a tin plate at Harpers Ferry. The specificity makes it ache: he knows what time church starts ("now some after ten"), he can picture Mother getting ready, he can hear the pony's hooves. He is 300 miles and a war away.
