## Full Transcription

> March 3, 1862
>
> My Dear Sister:
>
> I received your very welcome letter today. It was not much of a letter I must confess, but then I take the will for the deed. I hope in future you will write longer — a little longer letters. You say in the first letter you wrote me from Long — (how do you spell it) that you were very busy and had but little time and little inclination to write. Now Miss Fannie — I — Corporal A. F. Hubbell, do hereby charge you that you take care of yourself, and do you, what Mr. Bass said, "he learned to do" after six years of teaching, — "favor yourself". It will not pay to learn French at the expense of health. Therefore, Miss Fannie, if you find the place too hard, I would counsel you (and so would every other sensible person like myself) to "resign your commission" and return home to comfort your "old Mama" as Mother styles herself.
>
> How does Miss B. get along. Wonder if she has recovered the use of her tongue yet. She seems to be an exception to the sex, for they usually have all the talking to do. You tell Miss Mix the next time you see her that I have not forgotten that of her yet.
>
> We have very pleasant times down here in Baltimore. A party of us "boys" went out duck hunting with our rifles the other day (the ducks are very plentiful in the bay.) I expect we made great slaughter among them, but then the fog was so thick we could not see to pick them up. Our old boat leaked pretty fast. We landed twice to dip the water out. During the last half mile the water increased upon us quite fast and to add to our enjoyment, when we had passed over about quarter of the distance one of the oarlocks broke. After considerable exertion we reached the shore in safety with our boat about quarter full of water. We brought back glowing accounts of the abundance of game (and so there was) etc., etc. The next day the Orderly and another Sergeant and several men went out in one boat and Lieut Fitch and Corporal Luther in another after "ducks", and came pretty near getting "ducked" themselves. While out a heavy gale sprung up, which rendered the small boats perfectly unmanageable. The one in which the Lieut and Luther were in swung round and "gave its sides to the waves". They sat one in each end of the boat casting very doleful looks at one another expecting each moment to be consigned to Neptunes care. But they were disappointed. The boat struck a pier right side up and both hopped on dry land without more ceremony. The boat was never seen again. It either sunk or was carried away by the waves. They were obliged to pay for it. (Luther's share was five dollars.)
>
> The Army of the Potomac is reported to be on the move. Nearly every engine and box car were in use the other day transporting troops. Not one word of all this has been suffered to be published as yet. It was reported here the other day that Gen. Banks division (the one Henry is in) was entirely cut to pieces and that the rebels had been crossing the river all day and that they would be in Baltimore the next day. The latter part of the report was perfectly foolish for it would be impossible for the rebels to penetrate as far as Baltimore on account of the great number of troops we have on the Potomac. When the rebels come to Baltimore we will turn out and "present arms" to them. After which we will "Charge Bayonet". It is said they do not like cold steel. The "Sixtieth" can give them plenty of that. McClellan is in great favor. The latest news is that Columbus has been evacuated. We may now safely say the rebels are routed out of the West. I shall try and send you some papers.
>
> Your affectionate Brother
>
> Alexander

## Editorial Notes

The duck hunting narrative is one of the most vivid recreational scenes in the collection — soldiers with rifles hunting ducks in Chesapeake Bay, fog preventing recovery of kills, a leaking boat with a broken oarlock. The next day's disaster (Lt. Fitch and Luther nearly drowning, boat lost to a gale) is told with characteristic Alexander humor. The false Banks rumor is important: Alexander's division was in Baltimore, Henry's was with Banks in the Shenandoah. Alexander was tracking his brother's military situation and dismissed the catastrophic rumor with strategic logic (rebels couldn't reach Baltimore). Frances is now identified as teaching French, probably at Longueuil, Quebec — a French-speaking area near Montreal.
