## Full Transcription

> June 16, 1865
> Near Washington, D.C.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> You have doubtless wondered at my long silence. I have been waiting to see if there would not soon be some news of our going home, but I still see no immediate prospects though I presume we will be discharged before a very very long while, as soon as the country gets more settled and the different new State Governs get into smooth working order.
>
> Our corps and division has been broken up. Gen. Geary has issued his farewell order and gone. A large number of veteran regts. formed into a division are commanded by Brevet Major General Bartlette. Our Brig. (3rd) is commanded by Brevet Major General Greene. He was formerly Colonel of our Regt. He was promoted to be a Brigadier and commanded our brigade in the White Star Division a long time. He was wounded in the battle at Wauhatchie, Tenn. and left us then and has not been with us since until now. We like him and are glad at his promotion. His son, Chris Greene (his aide de Camp) was wounded a few weeks after his father at the battle of Ringgold. His leg was taken off by a cannon shot. Two of our Regt. bandaged his leg (it hung by a few muscles and these they were obliged to sever with a pocket knife) and carried him from the field. He presented each of them afterward with a handsome Star with his and their names engraved on them. He is now on the staff of his Father.
>
> We have been paid. I expressed home yesterday three hundred and fifty dollars. Please say fifty to Frances and tell her I regret not being able to buy her before. The money is in fifty dollar coupon bonds. The Paymaster said the money was not invested as well as it could be. Ask Mr. Hoyle what to do with them. Use as much of the money as you want — all of it if you want it. Please write often. I will endeavor to be more prompt in future.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
>
> P.S. Please send lots of papers, i.e. if you have them. I have been obliged to buy a new watch and consequently could not send home as much as I wish I could. Perhaps I did not tell you I was obliged to sell my other watch to buy bread to keep life in me.

## Editorial Notes

Chris Greene's leg — "it hung by a few muscles and these they were obliged to sever with a pocket knife" — is one of the collection's most visceral images, told in a post-war letter as a historical detail, not a horror story. The boy's father was wounded at Wauhatchie; the boy lost his leg at Ringgold. He gave stars to the men who saved him. He's now on his father's staff. The war's violence becomes the war's decorations.

"I was obliged to sell my other watch to buy bread to keep life in me." — The watch Alexander coveted and obtained, the possession he carried through the Atlanta Campaign, sold in a hospital to buy food. The army didn't always feed its sick. $350 in bonds goes home; the watch is gone.
