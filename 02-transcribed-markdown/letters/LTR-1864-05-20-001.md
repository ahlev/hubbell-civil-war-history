## Full Transcription

> May 20th, 1864
> Camp near Kingston, Ga.
>
> Dear Fannie:
>
> "At length," I can hear you say, "at length he has found time to write me a letter. Right then, indeed he should, I should think. Not a word has he written since he left on furlough." Now, Miss Fannie, haven't I nursed a right? I will plead guilty to all charges and beg ten thousand pardons, but cannot under existing conditions promise to do any better in future, but will promise to do my best. Now won't you accept my poor apologies and write me a good long letter? You see your silence has brought me to terms.
>
> We are, I suppose you are aware, in close pursuit of Johnson's army. Our advance has had several little brush brushes with the enemy's rear since we started. Yesterday the rebs made a stand where I am now writing. We moved upon them about three P.M. in line of battle, but when we reached here the enemy were found wanting, having again fallen back. Their only object was, I suppose, to check our advance as long as possible. We are resting today awaiting the construction of a bridge which the rebs burned yesterday. You can have little idea how grateful this little rest is to us after being so constantly on the move for the last three weeks. We came late and rise while the stars are shining and march all day under a scorching sun with a load you would think would kill a mule. But then we do not always have such rough times. T
>
> The name of the late battle which we fought, our loss, the loss of the enemy, and all the little particulars I am entirely ignorant of having seen no paper of a date later than the battle. Rumors are in abundance but I have learned not to put much confidence in them. Of the battle all I know is what transpired immediately around me and that is a very small part of a battle. The line of battle was very long and I am thinking it will be a hard thing to settle upon a name for it. The "eastern men are learning to respect the Star Corps (20th)."
>
> I was astonished — shocked yesterday to read in a paper Mr. Hoyle sent that Mr. Bass was shot and probably fatally. Now sad! I think Dodds must have been deranged. Who will now teach the old Academy? Nor Mrs. Bass! His wound I have but little doubt is fatal. I have known men shot through the right lung to liver, but do not recollect of one surviving shot through the left lung. Mr. Bass will have one advantage that we poor fellows do not have out here — that is good nursing and the best medical treatment. One of our men the other day, a brutal fellow, when we were casting over the battle field picked up a rebel leg and foot and shook it in our faces with some disgusting remark. I wanted to knock the fellow down with the butt of my gun.
>
> My paper says halt. I send this letter with my last stamp. The 153rd has been engaged, I see. Charley, I believe, was not there. I rec'd a letter from him a few days since. He was then awaiting the boat to join his regt. Tell Mother to keep a stout heart. All our lives are in God's hands. Love to all.
>
> Your aff. Brother,
>
> Alexander
>
> P.S.— Bostwick is a bully soldier. Takes a battle very philosophically and stands the marches first rate. He is a great comfort to me. We mess together. He was somewhat astonished at the mode of proceeding in the late battle. Said he thought they came out in an open field and fought it out. The late battle ground was a perfect wilderness.

### Connections to Other Letters in Collection
- **Mr. Bass:** The schoolteacher Alexander has been sparring with throughout the collection — the Southern sympathizer who said three Northerners couldn't beat one Southerner, who Alexander was sure voted against soldiers' voting rights. ## Editorial Notes

The severed rebel leg is the collection's most visceral moment — a soldier picking up an amputated limb from the battlefield and shaking it in his comrades' faces. "I wanted to knock the fellow down with the butt of my gun." Alexander, who has killed men at Gettysburg, who has been shot at Lookout Mountain, who has watched deserters executed, draws the line at desecrating the dead. The moral compass holds.

Mr. Bass's shooting is reported as home-front violence erupting into the war — "Dodds must have been deranged." The schoolteacher who admired the South has been shot in Champlain while Alexander fights in Georgia. The war at home and the war at the front collide.

Bostwick's astonishment at battle — "said he thought they came out in an open field and fought it out" — is the recruit's discovery that war is not what he imagined. "The late battle ground was a perfect wilderness."
