## Full Transcription

> Mch. 6th, 1861 [*sic* — 1862]
> Baltimore
>
> My Dear Mother:
>
> I received your very welcome letter day before yesterday. I have been watching the mails for the last fortnight and had just about dispaired of hearing from you when Luther came in and handed me two letters, one from Charles and Frances and one from Mr. Copeland. You may be sure I had a jolly time reading them. I am very happy to hear from the hens and heifer and "old whity". So you make eight pounds of butter per week, eh? That is doing well — certainly. You must have enough and to spare. I am glad that the hens are doing so well. Where do you keep and what do you feed them. I am very particularly glad that you have allowed me to help pay the taxes. Charles says there is a pew tax of eight dollars and a half. I will pay it and am only to glad to do it. This is something I have longed to do for a good many years and am very glad that I am now able to do it. We have been "mustered in" and will have our pay soon.
>
> So James has had his appointment from the "War Department", has he? I think studying under Everest will be a first rate thing for him. I wish I could raise that 60 dollars. He is welcome ten times over to all the money I have. We shall be paid off again in May. I will try and send him twenty dollars then.
>
> You tell Dodds Beaumont and Bowen that all the poetry will be taken out of them and their uniforms when they come down here and begin to drill through the mud and water as I saw some cavalry doing yesterday. They will have to swing those swords till their wrists fairly ache. How strange it will seem to them to come down here and sleep in a building with several hundred others, and to cook their own provisions, take care of their horses, etc., etc. But then I respect a young man for doing it. They will probably like it after awhile.
>
> Now, dear Mother, don't worry about Mrs. Webb. She is a miserable old hag and beneath your notice. Do you remember when she use to come to our house to have a "talk" — a talk. How sanctimonious she used to look! "Well, now I know Lellies abilities are good, and — I don't see why — etc. etc. etc." Isn't that it, eh? Mr. Kellogg is an "old sputterer". Let things take their course. Don't worry.
>
> You tell Charles that our Regt. is indeed as brave as a lion and eager for fight. Is Dobbin going to remain another term. So you have as much snow as we had last winter. Fairly blocked in, eh?
>
> I have nothing more of importance to write.
>
> Your affectionate son,
>
> Alexander
>
> P.S. The 34th is now or was the last I heard from them, at Harpers Ferry. They form a part of the van of the advancing army. I saw a fellow from the Regt. yesterday. He carried a letter for me to Henry. One of the pickets of Co. C was nearly killed an hour or two since. He was standing looking at a train loaded with mules and did not see the one coming from the opposite direction. The engine struck him and knocked him off the track, breaking his leg and inflicting terrible wounds on the head. Please write oftener. Do not let so long a time elapse again without writing.

## Editorial Notes

The year error ("1861" for 1862) is the transcriber's slip — every contextual detail places this in March 1862. Alexander's farm interest (hens, heifer, butter production) and his pride in paying the pew tax ($8.50) reveal a young man shouldering family responsibilities at 17. The P.S. is critical: the 34th NY forms "the van of the advancing army" at Harpers Ferry, and a Co. C picket was nearly killed by a train — the third railroad accident in the collection (Lt. Porter killed Jan 9, Alexander's near-miss Jan 25, now this). Mrs. Webb and Mr. Kellogg represent small-town tensions that Alexander dismisses with characteristic directness. Everest as James's tutor may be connected to the West Point appointment preparation.
