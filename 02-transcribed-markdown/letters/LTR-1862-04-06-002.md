## Full Transcription

> April 6th, 1862
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your letter last night and was glad to hear from home. I have to take my chance to write just when I can. Ink is very scarce here. I wrote a letter some time ago but did not have a chance to mail it. I hope to mail this day after tomorrow. I shall write just as often as I can.
>
> Yesterday I had really a hard time. It was the Sabbath, our Regt. was detailed as pioneers with axes, picks and shovels. I used an ax. It was not the work that I did, but it was the marching four or five miles to the place where we were wanted. Between here and York Town the rebels blocked the roads up pretty well (in the woods we had to make new roads for the artillery and troops to march. We expected to have a fight day before yesterday and left Hampton with that intention, but we have not had it yet nor are we likely to. The rebels seem to be retreating everywhere our troops go. I believe there was some fighting though at York Town day before yesterday by the artillery. Have not heard much about it yet. I do not think the rebels will make much of a stand there. There was a great light off in that direction not long since. Some thought that York Town was on fire. We will probably go there before long. Gen. Gorman gave us some very good advice. He told us if we ever came into action to keep cool and not to mind if brother or cousin fell, but to go ahead. We have the best rifles in the service. I do not think there is any probability of our going into action. I do not know what the name of the place that we are in, but I shall call it Camp in field, for that is where we are, about 16 miles from Hampton. We left that place day before yesterday. Gen. McClellan is now at the head of the army out here, or at least out this way. I have seen him twice. He is a young looking man.
>
> The rebels have taken a great deal of pains by throwing up intrenchments along here, but all to no purpose. They were in camp here last winter about two miles from here. Their barracks are very good — much better than we had. In regard to their grub — I do not see why they could not live as well as our troops.
>
> It is very chilly today. The air is very heavy at night. When I march I am always sure to sweat. I have seen several trees in blossom since I left Washington. I should like to be at home and take a sleigh ride. I hope the cows and horses at home will have enough hay and straw. I must confess it is all like a dream, my being here, but if I ever go home I can say that I have seen a good deal of the world, by hard marching.
>
> Write as often as you can. I remain your aff. Son,
>
> Henry
>
> P.S. Always address my letters to Washington and I will be sure to receive them.

### Historical Significance: `major`

## Editorial Notes

Gen. Gorman's battle advice — "keep cool and not to mind if brother or cousin fell, but to go ahead" — is the most chilling line in the collection given Henry's fate at Antietam five months later. The pioneer duty (road-building with axes through rebel-blocked woods) shows the unglamorous labor of siege warfare. McClellan seen twice and described as "a young looking man" — he was 35, commanding 100,000+ troops. Henry's "it is all like a dream" is his most reflective moment, a soldier seeing Virginia trees in blossom while yearning for Clinton County sleigh rides. The rebel intrenchments and abandoned camps document the Confederate defensive strategy on the Peninsula — elaborate earthworks constructed over the winter.
