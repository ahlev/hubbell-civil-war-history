## Full Transcription

> June 13th, 1862
> Camp within five miles of Richmond
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your letter day before yesterday. Very glad to hear from you and know that you are well. How I long to have one good meal at home, even if it wasn't anything but potatoes and salt, anything but hard crackers. I can not bear to look at them. As for fresh meat, I cannot eat it. I often think of what a good time I will have when I am at home. I have not been well for the last three days. Could not take anything but what I would throw up. The Dr. gives quinine and pills, but they have not helped me much. I am better this morning. Do not worry about me.
>
> I suppose you have heard before this time of the Regt. going into battle. As I wrote the day after, the papers state we lost 150, but it was 123 wounded and killed. Our Co. lost two killed and ten wounded. I cannot be too thankful coming out without being injured. Lieut. Scott, I think, has gone home, as he was wounded. He acted very brave. Most of the boys were down on him before, but now they are all for him. Lieut. Ransom has command of the Co. now, as Capt. Reich has been discharged. I wish he was back. Ransom is liked very well.
>
> This morning the rebel battery commenced firing bomb shells into our Camp and grapeshot. A twelve pounder was picked up this morning by one of our boys. The way the balls whiz around here is a caution. We have breastworks built in front of our Camp of logs and dirt thrown up against them. We have good protection from the shells. It is probably Gen. McClellan's intention to storm that battery before long, and then for Richmond.
>
> I wonder how Sid can be so ugly about our cows. I am shure if I was at home seems to me if I was in Sids place I would put up with almost anything, but I suppose our cows are very unruly. They would never jump over Nyes fence, I am sure. I have not received a paper from home for some time, three or four weeks. Give my respects to Mrs. Brinkhoff. Tell her I hope to be home before winter. I do not feel able to write any more just now. I remain your aff. son,
>
> Henry

## Editorial Notes

Henry has survived the Battle of Fair Oaks — the largest engagement of the Peninsula Campaign to that point. His company lost 2 killed and 10 wounded out of perhaps 50-60 men, a casualty rate of roughly 20%. His gratitude at surviving is understated: "I cannot be too thankful coming out without being injured." Lieut. Scott's redemption is a classic Civil War narrative — the petty tyrant of peacetime proving himself under fire. But Henry's health is declining: three days of vomiting, quinine (suggesting malaria or chronic dysentery from the Peninsula swamps), and "I do not feel able to write any more just now." The rebel battery throwing shells into camp — "the way the balls whiz around here is a caution" — shows daily danger even between battles. At five miles from Richmond, the 34th NY was as close to the Confederate capital as any Union regiment would be until 1864.
