## Full Transcription

> June 21, 1864
> Morganza, La.
>
> Dear Fannie:
>
> We are still encamped on the Mississippi; how long we shall remain here I cannot tell. I heard this morning that it was probable that we should not remain here long. The location is a good one, and if we are to remain in camp this summer I should not object to remaining here.
>
> I have had but one trial of marching. That was out on a foraging expedition for corn. At night I sprawled out on my tent floor feeling that I had done a pretty hard day's work. I stood it pretty well considering I had used my legs so little since I have entered the service. We went to a small town at two great roads and filled our wagons from a French woman's barn. The men are nearly all off to war. Four barns, I think, filled our train of one hundred wagons. They talk about the rebels starving. It cannot be so here. One of our company called at a house and was hospitably entertained with cornbread and syrup, part of which he brought out in his hands. They said they had not seen wheat flour for about two years. There is considerable sugar in the mills along the river but it does not benefit us any. Our allowance is one spoonful per day. Negroes were quite plenty in the town. All spoke French like their masters. In front of some houses there were squads of eight or ten all sitting as radiantly as a negro. We saw numbers of great and little pigs, chickens and vegetable gardens, pretty well filled, all of which tempted our soldiers very much, but foraging was strictly forbidden.
>
> The houses are not built with much style, though they may be very comfortable; they are mostly low wooden buildings. I have seen some very handsome brick houses with beautiful grounds in front. Nearly all have pretty yards in front which seem to be spontaneous. I do not see why brick buildings are not more common. Clay is so abundant on the river and the Mississippi so handy to float the material down. None of the settlements are far back from the river.
>
> James Sweeney has lately come into our regt. as Surgeon. On Friday and I went over to see him. He was such a little faller when he left our place that I hardly remembered him. I think that if either of us are sick he will be a friend worth having. You know in the army a person does not get much care.
>
> Well, I have scribbled over this paper and not said much. Comment vous portez ma vous! What do you think of this war? Do you watch the Army of the Potomac with interest? O, Grant, go ahead and whip the rebels, is the feeling here. An inconvenient place to write has made me scribble and soil this paper. Will you please excuse it.
>
> Your aff. brother,
>
> James

## Editorial Notes

James's first letter reveals a third Hubbell voice — curious, slightly self-conscious, asking Frances "Comment vous portez ma vous!" (a garbled French greeting) and "What do you think of this war?" as if making conversation at a social call. He notices things differently: the spontaneous-looking yards, the clay that could make brick, the squads of negroes "sitting as radiantly as a negro." The phrasing is awkward but observant.

The foraging scene — 100 wagons filled from four barns, the French woman's property emptied while foraging is "strictly forbidden" for individuals — captures the contradictions of military requisitioning. They take everything from the barns but soldiers can't take a chicken.

"You know in the army a person does not get much care." The youngest brother, newest to the service, has already learned this truth. Dr. Sweeney from home is insurance against the army's medical indifference.
