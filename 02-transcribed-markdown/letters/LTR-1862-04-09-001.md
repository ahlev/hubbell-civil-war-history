## Full Transcription

> April 9th, 1862
> Camp of the 34th Regt.
>
> Dear Aunt:
>
> Your welcome letter I received and very glad was I to hear from you. I am not sure that this letter will go through as there has been a stop put to letter writing, but that was some time ago.
>
> The weather for some time past has been very pleasant, but for the last two or three days, we have had a drissling rain and it has been quite cold. The weather has not changed yet. We have our rubber blankets for tents.
>
> We are now sixteen miles from Fortress Monroe. Our Regt. and Brigade took the boat at Alexandria. We had very pleasant weather while we were coming except one night it rained. The rebels have possession now of York Town, but they are getting pretty well surrounded and will have to give up before long. I think the rebels are a cowardly set and will run if they see danger ahead. At Hampton the rebels have burnt a great many buildings, only a very few left, but Harpers Ferry is the place where they have been most to work, and where the greatest damage is done. I expect any day we may march to York Town and perhaps have a little skirmish. We are good for them any time. I heard that when the rebels knew that we were on the march for this place they left on a double quick so one of their slaves said.
>
> The rebels have very good barracks, much better than we have had, but they have lost them now. I expect you hear more of the news than I for we do not have any papers except what we receive from home. I received a letter from home not long since. The snow is very deep, six feet in the woods. I expect they will have a freshet this spring. I hope to be home next June — that is if the war is over.
>
> I have nothing more to write this time. Believe me to remain your aff. Nephew,
>
> Henry

## Editorial Notes

Henry's letter to his aunt is notable for the enslaved person's intelligence report: when the rebels learned the Union was marching, "they left on a double quick so one of their slaves said." This is one of the few direct references to enslaved people in Henry's correspondence. The mail censorship ("stop put to letter writing") reflects Peninsula Campaign operational security. The contrast between six feet of snow at home and Virginia spring rain underscores the geographic dislocation these Clinton County men experienced.
