## Full Transcription

> Sept. 26, 1861
> Camp Wheeler
> Ogdensburg, New York
>
> My Dear Brother James:
>
> I have at last found time to write you. Today is fastday. We shall probably not drill today. Services will be held here today at four P.M. Our Company stood on guard on Tuesday. I stood from fifteen to twenty hours. The reason why I stood so long was because a great many ran the guard after nightfall and we were obliged to thrible it to prevent this. A watch word is given to the guard at nightfall and no one is allowed to go in or out unless he can give it. During the evening I saw a person coming toward me. When within ten feet of me I chalenged him with "who comes there" (reply) "Friend" "Advance Friend and give the countersign". He advanced until hil abdomen touched my bayonet and then leaning over he gave the word. I immediately passed him. In like manner I had passed another and another until I began to wonder what was in the wind when I heard a fellow say in a half whisper to a man outside the camp "is it right". I then saw what was the matter. (I had passed five). I immediately called out "Officer of the Guard No. 11 (I was sentry No. 11). He came, and I told him the watch word was discovered. The Guards were immediately doubled and the Colonel came around and gave order to run any man through the legs who attempted to run the guard, also to pass no one in or out. No one ran the guard again that night. Last night a great many ran the guard. The Colonel went after them with an officer of the Guard and a squad of about twenty-four men. (I just wrote a letter for a fellow named Wilson.)
>
> Our Colones is a small, smart energetic-looking man, and I think a capital officer. Captain Ransom was elected by an unanimous vote. If anybody asks you how I like soldiering you may tell them I like it very well. The only very hard thing about it is that I am obliged to listen to so much profanity and obscene language. "However" as Henry says, I make the best of it. They do not fire the cannon now.
>
> Your affectionate Brother,
>
> Alex
>
> P.S. Tell Charles I will write him soon.
>
> ..... 30 .....
>
> Same Sheet —
>
> My dear Mother:
>
> I can not close this letter without saying a word or two to you. You may consider all this letter yours. I shall write you soon. I received your letter yesterday. Very glad to hear from you. I was beginning to think you had forgotten me. Daily prayer meetings are held in the camp. I attended one last night. Have not spent so pleasant an evening since I have been here. There were from thirty to forty there. They were all Methodists I think. There was plenty of groans and shouting, but I presume I shall get used to it. I just found out last night that they had meetings here. I am very well. Give my respects to Mrs. B.
>
> Your affectionate Son,
>
> Alexander F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Alexander's guard duty narrative is the most vivid action sequence in the collection so far. The escalation — watch word leaked, five unauthorized passes, calling the Officer of the Guard, doubling the guard, Colonel's pursuit with 24 men — reads like a short story. His discomfort with profanity and his relief at finding prayer meetings reveal a young man of strong religious conviction, consistent with the "Brother Craftsmen" society consideration in his Sept 27 letter. The parenthetical "(I just wrote a letter for a fellow named Wilson)" is a window into camp literacy — some soldiers couldn't write their own letters. Alexander's quoting of Henry ("However") shows how the brothers' voices echoed across their separate camps.
