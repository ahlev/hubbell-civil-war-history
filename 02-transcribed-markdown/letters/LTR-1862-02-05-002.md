## Full Transcription

> Feb. 5th, 1862
> Camp Bolivar Heights
> Harpers Ferry
>
> Dear Charles:
>
> I was very much pleased to receive a letter from you. You must excuse this bad writing as I have no bench or table to write on. Have to write just as I can.
>
> You will no doubt be a little surprised to know that our Regt. and brigade are in Harpers Ferry, but for all that it is so. Our Regt. had a very shot notice to pack up and be ready at a moments warning. Col. Ladue has returned to our Regt. We marched very early in the morn on the 27th or 28th, I am not sure which, for Adams Town. The first day we marched about 20 miles. Passed through Barnesville. Camped out the first night. Next morn I rose at three o'clock, had cup of coffee of hard crackers and salt junk. That is all a soldier can expect to get on a march. Glad enough to get that when we are hungry. We left at daylight for Adamstown. Arrived at twelve. Every white man or darkey we would meet we would ask how far it was to Adams Town. They would always reply one mile, when it would be perhaps two or three. I do not doubt but what they were strong seseesh. We took the cars at Adamstown for Harpers Ferry. Not only our Regt., but the whole brigade. We arrived about four in the afternoon, a ride of thirty miles in freight cars. Had seats of course. The remark was made by several as our Regt. passed over the pontoon bridge, that it was the best looking that passed. That has got to be an old story, however. The first night we went into a large brick building which used to be a cotton factory. We stayed there three days. There was several bushels of wheat in the first story. There are four stories to the building. The soldiers got to running the wheat through the floors. Someone asked Gen. Gorman what he intended to do with the 34th. He laughingly said he did not know what to do with them as they had got to grinding wheat the first day.
>
> We are now two miles from Harpers Ferry in large bell tents — 17 in one tent, rather crowded but can not be helped. I have just been to dinner. Had rice, soup and fresh beef. We have tin cups for soup, coffee and tea. Sometimes tin plates but I have not. I think I shall buy one next pay day. We have a stove for the tent furnished by Uncle Sam. We had to leave our stove and tents. The day after our Regt. came into Camp you would have laughed to see the chickens and sheep and fresh beef come in. I did not go into that business. There has a stop been put to it now. I do not know how long we shall remain here, probably not a great while. I shall be very glad to receive the New York Observer, but the New York Times I don't care much for as it is all old news. I should like to be home to make sugar this spring, but that is out of the question. Write as often as you can.
>
> I remain your aff. Brother,
>
> Henry

### ⚠ DATING DISCREPANCY — RESOLVED

This letter says "we marched very early in the morn on the 27th or 28th" without specifying month. The Frances letter (LTR-1862-02-05-001) says "25th of Feb" but is dated Feb 5.

**RESOLUTION (from LTR-1862-02-28-001):** Henry's Feb 28 letter from Harpers Ferry confirms: "Our Regt. Left Camp McClellan on the 26th." LTR-1862-02-24-001 shows Henry at Camp McClellan on Feb 24, with march orders read at dress parade.

**Conclusion:** The departure was **February 26, 1862.** This letter's "27th or 28th" likely refers to Feb 27-28 arrival at Harpers Ferry. The "Feb 5" date on this letter is a transcription error — the letter was written after Feb 28, likely early March.

## Editorial Notes

This parallel march account adds critical details missing from the Frances letter: Barnesville on the route, Col. Ladue's return, the cotton factory billets (soldiers grinding wheat through the floors — Gorman's deadpan "he did not know what to do with them"), and the post-arrival foraging (chickens, sheep, beef materializing into camp until officers stopped it). The 17-men-per-bell-tent detail and government stove show the logistical transition from winter quarters to field deployment. Henry's comparison of newspapers — preferring the New York Observer (Presbyterian) over the New York Times — aligns with the family's religious orientation. The sugar-making reference places the family in the maple syrup economy of Clinton County.
