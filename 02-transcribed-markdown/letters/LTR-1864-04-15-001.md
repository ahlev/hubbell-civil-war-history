## Full Transcription

> April 15, 1864
> Head Quarters
> Department of the Gulf
> New Orleans, La.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> Have rec'd no letter yet, but expect one on the steamer due today. I don't know what to think of your long silence. Am afraid sometimes that you are sick, but hope that is not the reason. One week since I saw Sergt. Peltier of our Co. He was home on furlough when the Regt. left the North, and was on the way to join it when I saw him. He said if there were any letters for me with the Regt. he would send them on.
>
> There has been some terrible fighting in Western Louisiana. It is called a victory for us but so bloody that we were not able to take advantage of it. There was a heavy loss on both sides. The 153d was in it, but I haven't heard anything about its loss.
>
> A terrible accident happened here by which one of the clerks lost his life and liquor was the cause of it. He was drunk in the evening, and about twelve o'clock at night after he had spread his mattress on the floor of the office ready to go to bed, he went out on the gallery on the third story in the rear of the building, and accidentally pitched head first over the banister striking the stone pavement in the yard on his head. He never saved nor groaned. One of the clerks in a room close by heard him come staggering along and the next minute heard him fall.
>
> The ladies here, with Mrs. Banks as president, have held a tableaux, etc. for the benefit of the soldiers. The Sanitary Commission receive the funds. The clerks here expect to get commutation of rations and quarters about the 1st of June. If we get it our wages will amount to about sixty dollars a month, everything included. We can then board where we please.
>
> When you write please tell all the news and write long letters. I have not heard from home since the early part of February. Am very anxious to hear how you all get on. I don't know whether I am to stay here or not; have not done much yet. You had better not say much about my being here yet. Penmanship is the only trouble. I have not written Alex yet and I don't know whether James is in the army or not so I can't write him. I had rather not write Alex till I hear from him or home. Please tell the boys to write. I hope Frances has not forgotten me, but will write soon, though I think she is like me — doesn't forget if she doesn't write.
>
> Hoping that you all are well and enjoying life I will close.
>
> From your aff. Son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

"It is called a victory for us but so bloody that we were not able to take advantage of it." Charles, the headquarters clerk, receives the official version of Sabine Crossroads — the kind of euphemism armies use for defeats. His regiment was in it, and he doesn't know their losses. The Red River Campaign is collapsing while Charles copies special orders.

The clerk's death is told with forensic precision — the mattress spread on the floor, the gallery on the third story, the head-first pitch over the banister, the stone pavement. "He never saved nor groaned." A death not from battle but from whiskey and a high balcony. The war kills in many ways.
