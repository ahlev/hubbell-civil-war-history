## Full Transcription

> July 8, 1864
> In the Field near
> Chattahoochee, Ga.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your letter of June 20th yesterday and the handkerchief enclosed, for which I am very, very much obliged. I can now wipe the perspiration from my brow with some degree of comfort — before I received the handkerchief I was reduced to the necessity of using a portion of an old rebel meal bag.
>
> Your letter gave me a feeling something akin, I suppose, felt by the Israelites of old when they were described as longing for "the flesh pots of Egypt." I hardly think I shall be at home in time to enjoy the cherries and plums with you, but hope to be before another season rolls around. The fighting will all be done this summer, I think. The eyes of the 60th Vets. have been blest with a sight of the goal (as we suppose) of the campaign — Atlanta. Day before yesterday we were upon a high ridge from which by climbing a tree we could see the city with its tall spires and chimneys — and a great smoke going up — from its numerous furnaces, I suppose. With a glass you could see the windows of the houses and the streets plainly. We could see the forts around the city and lines of rifle pits. The rebs have made a stand again, this side of the river. We are not fighting much at present, only skirmishing and an occasional shot from the batteries. Last night about dusk there was a furious cannonade to the fight of the line, but whether it was accompanied with musketry, we were too distant to tell. I have not heard the cause of it. About midnight the firing of the skirmishers was very sharp, and I expected we should be called up but it settled down again to its usual opening. We are, I understand, about a mile and a halfrom the river. The rebel works are on a ridge close to its banks. They are very strong, I should judge. They have several large earthen forts in the line. We are making the most of our little rest. The officers are hard at work today upon the pay rolls. I suppose we shall not be paid until the campaign is over. It has lasted now over two months. I heard from Luther yesterday. He is doing well.
>
> Is there much excitement up there relative to the Presidential campaign? It occasions very little remark down here. Fremont, I think, will meet little favor with the army. All are awaiting the action of the Democratic convention at Chicago. I see they have postponed the meeting until Aug. 29th. They have shown the right spirit and put the Republicans to shame. Every nerve of the Nation should now be used to strengthen her armies and uphold the administration. It will be time enough to talk politics after the summer's work is done. It will be hard to tell, I think, before that is done who is the right man for President.
>
> I suppose I saw the cannon fired that killed Bishop Polk as our Regt. was close to the batteries at the time. The shot or shell struck him in the breast. If the shell had burst, old Johnston and Hardee would probably ave shared the same fate, as they were close beside him. Please write often. Love to all.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Alexander saw the cannon shot that killed Bishop Polk — Lt. Gen. Leonidas Polk, the "Fighting Bishop," killed on Pine Mountain on June 14 when Sherman ordered artillery fire at a group of Confederate generals observing Union positions. "If the shell had burst, old Johnston and Hardee would probably have shared the same fate." The shell hit Polk in the chest but didn't burst — killing one general instead of three. Alexander was close to the batteries and saw it happen.

Seeing Atlanta from a ridge — climbing a tree to view the spires and chimneys, the smoke from furnaces, the forts and rifle pits — is the campaign's pivot point. The goal is visible. Two months of fighting remain.

The old rebel meal bag used as a handkerchief before Mother's silk one arrived — the war reduced to its essentials.
