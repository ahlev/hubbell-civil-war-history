## Full Transcription

> June 16th, 1862
> Camp within five miles of Richmond
>
> Dear Aunt:
>
> It is sometime since I have written you but I hope to make up lost time. I have not been well for the last week. Been under the Dr.'s care, but I am better now and hope to be around again. Have not been in the hospital yet. The weather here is very warm most of the time.
>
> It is a year ago yesterday since our Regt. swore in to the United States service. A long year it has been to me. Twelve more months and then our time is up. We may be discharged before that time, the sooner the better. There are not over five hundred men in the Regt. that do duty. It is needless for me to go into particulars about our Regt. going into a fight, as I wrote about it in Nellie's letter. We have had great praise from Gen. Gorman. One of the reble prisoners said he did not know how we could fire so fast, and load. They do not know the Yankees yet, but the are finding us out, to their sorrow.
>
> Saturday, the day we had the fight, one of the rebles said their Gen. told his men to keep up courage as they were going to surmount the Yankees and take them prisoners. He said also that the road between here and Richmond was lined with carriages with ladies and Gents. to see the Yankees, but instead of seeing us, they had to take there own carriages to carry their wounded. It is my belief that they found it rather hot work.
>
> We are having about as hard a time as we will have. Although we are not marching, hard crackers, salt junk, fresh meat I can not eat. The Sutler is sure to be around here pay day, but if there is battle, or any prospect of one, he is off.
>
> We have breast works in front of our camp, but it is all the work of us soldiers as a matter of course.
>
> So Uncle Henry is blessed with a boy. I am glad for his sake. I am sure for my part I would not object to a present of a few hundred, for the boys sake, and perhaps for his own good. He would not feel it much. Now I make motion we try it, but I do not want to have any responsibility. But enough of this for this time. My respects to all my friends. Excuse all mistakes with the hope of doing better next time. Believe me to remain your aff. Nephew.
>
> Henry

## Editorial Notes

Henry telling the Fair Oaks story a third time — now to his aunt — reveals how he adjusts his narrative for the audience. Mother gets casualty figures and gratitude at survival. Charles gets the sensory experience of combat. Aunt gets the rebel prisoner's grudging admiration and the carriages anecdote. The regiment being down to 500 from ~1,000 after one year is stark — the Peninsula swamps, disease, and one major battle have halved the unit. Henry's joke about Uncle Henry's baby is one of his rare moments of humor in the letters and shows a personality the war correspondence usually doesn't reveal.
