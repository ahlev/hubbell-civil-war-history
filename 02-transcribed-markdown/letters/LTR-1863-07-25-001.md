## Full Transcription

> July 25, 1863
> Capitol Hill
> Washington, D.C.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your welcome letter of July 16th. Was glad to hear from you and Frances. Rec'd a letter from James dated the 18th. He writes in good spirits. Am glad that Alex came safely out of that Gettysburg fight. Until I rec'd your letter did not know anything about him. Have not heard from him yet, but I will write him soon.
>
> I suppose you have heard from Mr. Steais that we have left Alexandria. It was a sudden and unexpected move. The first we knew of it, about 6 o'clock one night some of our men said they saw our reg't marching towards Wash. with knapsacks. A little while after a Co. came to relieve us and gave us an order to report to Wash. Our 1st Lieut. want to see the Major that commanded our Co. We were ordered to leave that night. But we did not leave until next morning. A good many thought that we would leave immediately for the front. We did not, though, but are now in comfortable barracks. The Reg't. came here on foot which is about 8 miles, but our Co. came on a steamboat. The day before we left ten days rations were drawn for the company as — did not expect to leave. During the night somebody stole nearly all the rations and sold them. They thought we were going to leave we could draw some more rations somewhere else. Yesterday an orderly sergt. with another sergt. and corporal James Lucas and the cook were arrested and are to be tried for selling our rations. Four of our Co. under arrest. If found guilty they will be severely punished. Jim Lucas told some one that he was there when the rations were stolen.
>
> We were detached from the Reg't. six months. Some say that we will stay here some time if we behave ourselves. Our Col. told some of the men that if they did not want to carry a knapsack this summer they must behave themselves. The Col. is very strict about everything. A private in our Co. has been tried by courts martial and sentenced to lose one month's pay and do one day's hard work for always being dirty. We are now in the Capitol of the U.S. and can't do as we have a mind to. No officer or soldier can leave the camp ground without a pass.
>
> How much was the pea tax that you paid? Frances did right in putting out that money. We are receiving our pay today. Enclosed is a twenty dollar check. I want you to use as much of my money as you want. Will you tell me who has rented the farm this year and how much he pays? I have forgotten, but suppose it is Murray. Am much obliged to Frances for her letter. They are few and far between, but then she has a good excuse as she is so busy. Hope she will keep her promise and write me a long letter. I need not tell you to write often because you generally do, although not lately. The weather is dry and hot.
>
> From your aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

The ration theft scandal reveals the petty corruption of garrison life. Ten days of rations stolen and sold the night before the company moved — the thieves assumed new rations would be drawn elsewhere. The arrests of the orderly sergeant, a corporal, and the cook suggest organized theft, not a lone opportunist. Jim Lucas informed on his own comrades.

Charles's relief about Alexander is understated: "Am glad that Alex came safely out of that Gettysburg fight." He learned it from Mother, not from Alexander — the mail system failing at the moment it mattered most.
