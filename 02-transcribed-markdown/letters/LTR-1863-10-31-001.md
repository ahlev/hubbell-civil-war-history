## Full Transcription

> Oct. 31, 1863
> Capitol Hill
> Washington, D.C.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I rec'd the letter and box brought by Alex, who arrived here the 29th. Am very much obliged for all the nice things you sent which were unaccounted. The dried apples though, I don't know exactly what to do with, as I will have to buy sugar for them. I will have plenty of socks for this winter as I now have five pairs.
>
> Yesterday our Lieut. called me to him and asked me if I wanted a furlough. I told him I did not at present. A good many are going home on furlough now so as to vote at the N.Y. elections. But the most are sick furloughs. A good many will leave tonight for home. I have only about $1.50 in money which would be very little to go home with. I can get transportation and have it taken out of my next pay. Also I got transportation part of the way. Last night I saw Capt. Rich. He asked me if I had my word to send home as he was going to C. I told him I was better. For about a week I had a cough and very little appetite and for that time did not do my duty. For two or three days I took cough mixture. I have now been on duty several days and feel all right again.
>
> Today we are to be mustered in for pay. I think I have told you a careless thing I did while we were at Alexandria. I would carry what money I had of in my vest pocket for safe keeping as I would sometimes lose my pocketbook out of my pants at night. Well, I carried a comb and looking glass in the same pocket and in pulling one of these out I suppose I jerked out a five dollar bill. I never found the $5.00 of course, as there are plenty of dishonest soldiers. Also D. said 50¢ charges on the box which is 28¢ apiece for us. I guess Alex's chance for getting into the Invalid Corps is small. I think Alex will not want to be transferred into it.
>
> I will write to James in a few days and send him that letter. What do you think about his prospects? I think Frances will enjoy vacation when it comes, as she has to work so hard. This teaching school would try my temper, I think.
>
> Yesterday I read a letter from Corbeau, in which a mother told her son that she had let out his sheep on double in four years, and is to receive a lb. of wool yr. head yearly besides. That is better than you did with your sheep. We have some fine singers in this reg't. Two or three nights I have heard some our reg't. serenading the Col. It sounded very nice. They sung, "Let me kiss him for his mother" and two or three more songs I do not know the names of. Steais is in the hospital again; complains of his breast.
>
> Today there is a detail of Sergts. and Corporals to go off somewhere with three days rations. I wrote you sometime since that I went to Point Lookout near Fortress Monroe. Well, I happened to look on a map and found that Point Lookout is at the mouth of the Potomac and it near the south of Chesapeake Bay as I wrote you. It is a long ways from there to F. Monroe. I think a good many N.Y. soldiers will be home to vote at the election Tuesday. Moses Cokey will leave tonight for Champlain. I think. Please write as often as you can. I hope you will settle with the Savages immediately. Hoping that you will make yourself comfortable this winter, I will close.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Charles declines a furlough home because he has $1.50 to his name — having sent his pay to Mother in $20 checks. The financial sacrifice of sending money home means he can't afford to visit. Meanwhile, soldiers are going home on election furloughs, a widespread practice that allowed the army to influence state elections in 1863. The regiment singing "Let me kiss him for his mother" to their colonel is an image that belongs in a different kind of war narrative than Charles's usual pragmatic reporting.
