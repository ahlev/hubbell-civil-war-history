## Full Transcription

> September 21st, 1862
> Champlain, N.Y.
>
> My dear Henry —
>
> Language is too weak, to express my anxiety about you since the commencement of the terrible battle. Than God your name has not yet appeared on the sad list, which has caused morning and lamentation to so many homes. I read the sad record day after day as it is published, with trembling. The record of yesterday shows that the 34th has been terribly cut up and that Co. B. has suffered severely, and yet you escaped, for which we cannot be too thankful to our kind heavenly Father. I trust you make Him your habitation. If you do the promise is "No evil shall come nigh you." "We shall only see it with your eyes." Trust in God until these calamities be over past. There is your only safety. Daily & hourly commit your all into His hands — He only can keep you & preserve you from all evil.
>
> Poor Mrs. Carter is overwhelmed with grief at the death of her son. I hope you will write us immediately — and give us full particulars of all the battles that you have participated in — the most minute things that come under your observation, will interest us.
>
> How I long to be near you. I am almost tempted at times to go down to the hospitals. If I was a man I am sure I should enlist. In reading the descriptions of the battle fields — my hair almost rises. I saw in the Herald of yesterday a sketch of the position of the two armies, in the battle of Antietam. As I looked at the location of Sumners Corps, I could but think of you dear revolting[s?]. How did you feel while facing & fighting that desperate army? Yet all impatience to hear from you — to know how you are after all you have passed through — as well as to learn from your own pen all about the battles.
>
> I was perfectly delighted to learn through Alex letter — that you & he had met. That a meeting it must have been. Did you find him, or he find you? He only wrote a few lines, and gave no particulars.
>
> I see the 60th Regt. has lost its Col. I see no report of any others being killed or wounded from that Regt. Perhaps it is to come yet. I hope you and Alex will see each other often as possible. Charles has not had his furlough yet, is to have a short one the last of this week. He has been made first corporal. I suppose they will leave for the seat of war next week. He seems to be quite contented so far — of course he knows nothing of hardships yet — and I think, never will experience what you have — and if he does not believe he will have an opportunity to win laurels on the field of battle as you have — for it is thought that the back-bone of the rebellion is broken. I think the war is nearly over. God bless McClellan — long live McClellan.
>
> (Remainder of letter missing)

## Editorial Notes

This is the first letter from home in the entire collection — and it arrives at the worst possible moment. Mother is reading casualty lists "with trembling," scanning for Henry's name, and she hasn't found it. She thinks he's safe. She doesn't know that Alexander has already written (Sept 19) that Henry is MISSING after Antietam. The information gap between Champlain and the battlefield is about a week, and in that week, the truth is traveling toward her.

The letter is saturated with dramatic irony that Mother cannot feel but we can: "you escaped," "we cannot be too thankful," "I think the war is nearly over." She is writing to a son who is wounded, missing, and — as the McDonald letter's editorial header tells us — will not survive.

Her voice is distinctive: biblical quotations woven into maternal anxiety, political opinion ("God bless McClellan") alongside raw fear, the desire to enlist herself ("If I was a man"), and an insatiable hunger for detail ("the most minute things that come under your observation, will interest us"). This is a woman who has sent four sons to war and is reading the newspapers every morning to see if any of them are dead.
