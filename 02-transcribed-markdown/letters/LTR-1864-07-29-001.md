## Full Transcription

> July 29, 1864
> Near Atlanta, Ga.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your letter of the 11th inst. and paper of the 12th last evening and was much pleased with its contents. They were the right thing in the right place. I am writing this letter upon the paper you sent. It is singular that I receive the papers so much sooner than the letters. You see that the letter was mailed the day previous to the paper and still I received them both at the same time. I wrote you last Sunday and intended writing you a long letter such as you like, but the rebel batteries opened so furiously that I was obliged to desist and seek safety in the works. Their shells did but little damage. One man had his arm blown off, and two battery horses were killed. Our batteries were unusually silent this day, and I understand that it was by special order of Gen'l. Sherman, but about 10 P.M. they opened lustily. The battery we call "the Atlanta Express" sent a shell into the city every five minutes during the night.
>
> We have changed our position since then and are now further to the left of the line. Day before yesterday the 15th, 16th and 17th army corps moved to the right of the line. The cannonading there was very heavy yesterday and I think they had a heavy fight. Our regt. was sent out last night to make a feint as if we intended to attack them here, in order, I understand, to prevent their sending troops to the right. There was "right smart" of shooting done. The rebs opened a heavy fire upon us from their batteries. I am confident they expected a heavy attack. We lost two men. It is tolerably quiet today, a little skirmishing and an occasional shot from the batteries. Please write often.
>
> You asked me how my health is. It is very good and has been generally during the campaign. Never been slightly indisposed only once or twice. I have great reason, I think, for gratitude to God for His great kindness in preserving me thus far. I leave all in His hands and hope for the best. I hope that James and Charles have not forgotten me. I have as yet received no letter from them and have received only one from Charles since I have been in the Department. Love to all. Please write soon.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
>
> Received a letter from Capt. Ransom last night. Will endeavor to write him soon. Please give him my regards. I received the handkerchief some time since, also all the stamps you sent. I got all your letters in the course of time. I am glad that Frances has gone to Charlotte. I hope she will have a pleasant visit. She needed rest. Will she go into school again next term?

## Editorial Notes

The "Atlanta Express" — a battery firing shells into Atlanta every five minutes through the night — is the siege's industrial rhythm. Sherman ordered the batteries silent during the day, then opened them at 10 P.M. — a deliberate psychological weapon as much as a military one.

The feint attack is a telling detail: Alexander's regiment was sent out to make the rebels think an attack was coming from the left, while the real movement was the 15th, 16th, and 17th Corps shifting to the right. "Right smart" of shooting — two men lost in a deception. The war's calculus: men die to create an illusion.

Alexander's loneliness is quietly building. James and Charles haven't written. "I have as yet received no letter from them and have received only one from Charles since I have been in the Department." Three brothers in the army, and two of them have gone silent.
