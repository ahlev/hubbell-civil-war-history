## Full Transcription

> Dec. 9th, 1863
> Champlain, N.Y.
>
> My Darling Child:
>
> Where are you? Oh, where are you? Not a word have I heard from you since the late battles in Tennessee and I am in the deepest distress about you for I should have heard from you ere this, if all was well with you. I write without knowing where this letter may find you, whether with your Reg't., in hospital, or in prison. As yet your name is absent from the heart breaking list. I shrink from looking at the long dark catalogues. How many hearts have been made desolate in the taking of Lookout Mountain. I pray that the more dark wars may roll over me, for I am nearly engulfed in the billows. Oh, how they lash my poor tempest tossed, stricken heart. Dear, dear, precious Henry. I cannot dwell a moment upon his fate without an agony that is almost insupportable.
>
> I cannot purchase the things you sent for till I know that you have come safely out of the battles. If you have been cast on Belle Island you would never receive them. God forbid that you should ever have a home there even for a day. Perhaps you have written and your letter got reached me. I know you would not keep me in an agony of suspense. If you were in hospital, some of your comrades surely would write me.
>
> I rec'd. a letter from Chas. yesterday. He is still in Washington awaiting orders since they were relieved by the Invalid Corps. He is very anxious to hear from you since Hooker's battle. I have sent you stamps in almost every letter; have sent you envelopes as in newspapers and letters. If they are not rec'd, it is not worth while to send any more. Let me know if they are. I have selected material for your shirts which I like very much. The price will be a little over four dollars. I cannot get boots made for less than five dollars and a half. I can get them at the store for $3.75, said to be good. Socks for 60¢. Altogether, including the express charges it will amount to about fifteen dollars. I wish you would send without delay the measure of your foot or the number you wear.
>
> Are you not glad you were not with Meade in his late retreat? The Army of the Potomac has a hard fate. Grant must come up and throw his wand over it and it will go.
>
> James' examination is new. I hope he will be successful. Take good care of yourself. What a happy day it will be when you come home to stay. Then the silence of this great castle will be broken which is so painful now. Mrs. Ransom says Col Goddard's name is on the list of killed or wounded. I hope it is not serious. Write me immediately, my precious boy, and write as often. May God keep you "in the hollow of His hand," be a shield and buckler to you, preserve you from all evil and soon restore you to us is the daily prayer of your
>
> Anxious and affectionate
>
> Mother

## Editorial Notes

"Where are you? Oh, where are you?" Mother's opening is a cry into the void. She has been through this before — after Antietam, when Henry disappeared into silence and never came back. Now the silence repeats for Alexander, and every day without a letter is a day closer to the worst news.

"Dear, dear, precious Henry. I cannot dwell a moment upon his fate without an agony that is almost insupportable." In the middle of her terror for Alexander, Henry's ghost rises. The two crises — one past, one present — merge in Mother's mind. She cannot separate her fear for the living son from her grief for the dead one.

"The silence of this great castle will be broken which is so painful now." The house in Champlain — the "stone castle" — has become a tomb for the living. Mother sits alone in it, waiting for letters that may never come. The domestic prison of waiting is the home front's cruelest burden.

She signs herself "Anxious and affectionate Mother" — the adjectives tell the whole story.
