## Full Transcription

> October 4, 1864
> Harrisonburg
> In the Shenandoah Valley
>
> Dear Alex:
>
> When did I write you last! I know it is a long time since. Do not think because I have not written you that I have forgotten you. We have had two fights of which you have undoubtedly heard, driving the rebels both times. The last pursuit the inhabitants of the towns through which we passed said the Rebs went through there with a rush, and that their army was demoralized. In the road in the pursuit during the night we passed several of their wagons burning. I do not believe that the Johnnies will make another stand here in the valley. You have travelled this road once, I think.
>
> October 12th — Since I commenced this we have fallen back to Cedar Creek near Strasburg — burning barns and forage as we went. Alex Douglas has been boggled by the guerrillas, it is thought. He fell to the rear several days since with one man of our company while on a march and has not been seen or heard of since. The boys in this army have a habit of running through the houses and taking whatever they choose, which I think exasperates the rebels very much, and I cannot but fear for a straggler. This other fellow from our company was one of the greatest house thieves in our regt. Douglas was not any of that. I have heard this thief tell some of his stories. While he was in one house one of the children said, "Ma, there is a Yankee down cellar taking all our preserves and bacon." "Ma, see that Yankee looking in the oven." If they remonstrated with him he would "blackguard" them. I do not pity this fellow much. This running through houses is checked carefully by the officers but cannot always be prevented. Sometime before the battle of Winchester there was a little boy of our brigade gobbled while foraging. He was shot up with rebel deserters. During the fight they were ordered to turn out and help fight; he slipped out with them and made his escape. He said they were going to hang him for a spy.
>
> Last night I received a letter from home. They want to know why we do not write. I have been rather negligent in writing. Charley has written several times but they have not received his letters; the mails have been irregular. Mother says you are quite ill. I hope you are better ere this. I have seen one month in the hospital since I enlisted. I'm all right now. Charley's health is now pretty good. There is some talk about our going to Louisiana. I hope we will not. I do not like the climate.
>
> What are your views on the coming elections? I'm a voter; I go for old Abe; he is the man, I think. Little Mac will probably run strong. This war has lasted nearly four years. I would like to see it ended, and believe it will be soon.
>
> Your aff. brother
>
> James

## Editorial Notes

James's Shenandoah letter is the youngest brother's most substantial war narrative. The foraging stories are priceless — "Ma, there is a Yankee down cellar taking all our preserves and bacon." "Ma, see that Yankee looking in the oven." James tells these with a storyteller's ear, but then pivots to the consequences: Alex Douglas is missing, probably captured by guerrillas. "I cannot but fear for a straggler." The "house thief" from his company may be dead for his thieving.

The captured boy who was nearly hanged as a spy — "He said they were going to hang him for a spy" — is another James detail. The war at the individual level: a boy foraging, grabbed by rebel deserters, nearly executed, escaping in the confusion of battle.

"I'm a voter; I go for old Abe; he is the man." James at 18 (or 19) votes Lincoln without Alexander's agonizing. The youngest brother's politics are the simplest.
