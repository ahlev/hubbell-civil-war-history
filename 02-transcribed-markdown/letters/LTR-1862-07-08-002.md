## Full Transcription

> July 8, 1862
> Camp near James River
>
> Dear Frances:
>
> I received your welcomed letter yesterday after coming off from fatigue duty. I am glad you are at home again. I only wish I was there, but I must have patience. Time is gliding away gradually — before I know it it will be gone. You must enjoy yourself as well as you can this warm weather. The sun here almost melts a person, more so when we have nothing but rubber blankets for a shelter, which draws the sun down to almost furnace heat.
>
> I am quite delighted with the plans you have been making for my spirits have been raised greatly since the reception of your letter. If they can only be carried out. I should like nothing better than to farm it in Vermont. Aunt Laura made some hints in her last letter about Uncle Henry making a present of a few acres, but it was all in fun I suppose. But if our house and farm can be sold for a reasonable price, I can save enough money to buy a team and go to farming and make Mother's life a good deal easier or more comfortable than it has been.
>
> I have no doubt but what James will succeed for he is a good scholar. I am very anxious to hear from him, but for my part if I get through this, it will be military enough for me. You may be sure I have seen hard times. One night I shall never forget for we were obliged to march about all night in deep mud and rain. I look back and it seems a wonder to me what I have gone through, but thank God I have my health yet.
>
> Our Regt. was in a battle the last of June. I was obliged to throw my knapsack away as we went on the double quick. All our Co. lost their knap-sacks then except Peter Gugan. He managed to find his again. What things we have lost will be made up free of cost so I understand. If you could send me a couple of handkerchiefs it will be all I shall want in regard to clothing as I cannot be loaded down. I can draw shirts and socks from the Government. I have lost all my towels. If you could send me one I shall be much obliged.
>
> In this retreat — if we call it such — I think it will in the end turn out all for the best. Although we have lost a great many men, the rebels have suffered the most. I have great confidence in McClellan. There was one day in which our Regt. was exposed to the rebel batteries. Their shells flew thick and fast around us. The Major from our Regt. had both his feet taken off by a solid shot. It was bad management in having us so near their battery. There is a want of judgement in some of the officers in this army.
>
> I do not know whether you can make out this scroll, but I have to write just as I can. I shall be glad if you could send me some writing paper and pens as I have lost everything. We have all our supplies from the James River. We are under cover of the gun boats. They are a great protection, as yesterday the rebels threw twelve solid shots into a steamboat, but they dare not come very near our boats. There is a great deal I could better tell you about the war than write. You will not doubt see all in the papers before long. All the letters that I receive now I burn. Most that I received before from home I burnt, but those from Aunt L. and Nellie went with my knap-sack. I shall be obliged to stop as the mail goes in a short time.
>
> I hope Lieut. Scott will return for our Co. is running down pretty fast. There is a want of discipline. I hope you will enjoy yourself as well as you can. I should like to have a furlough, but I do not think I can get one. I think it would be pleasant for Mother if you could stay at home at least two or three months for I may be at home by that time, yet I may be obliged to stay my time out. I must hope for the best. I shall send ten dollars in this letter although it is a risk.
>
> Your aff. Brother,
>
> Henry

## Editorial Notes

Henry is on the James River after the most devastating week of the war for the Army of the Potomac. He has lost his knapsack, his letters from home, his towels, his writing materials — everything. A Major in his regiment lost both feet to a cannon ball. Yet he writes to Frances about farming in Vermont and buying a team, clinging to a post-war vision. His burning of received letters is a stark contrast to Alexander's passionate refusal to burn his ("No — no — I cannot do that"). Henry may be destroying evidence of his earlier despair — or simply lightening his load for the next retreat. The phrase "in this retreat — if we call it such" shows Henry aware that the army's language is euphemistic about what happened. His faith in McClellan persists despite everything.
