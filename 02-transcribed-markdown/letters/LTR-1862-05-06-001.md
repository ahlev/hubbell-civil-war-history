## Full Transcription

> May 6th, 1862
> In the Rebels Camp Ground
> York Town, Va.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> The rebels have evacuated York Town. Their fortifications are immense and very strong. I think they are great cowards. Our Regt. went into the rebel intrenchments early Sabbath morning. They had sand bags built up as high as a man's head in some places. Their tents are very good, some of them, much better than ours. I am writing in one of the rebel tents.
>
> We had a night march in a hard rain and deep mud. It was awful. We stopped every ten paces it seemed to me, several divisions being ahead of us. We marched all night and came back to camp about two o'clock in the morning. It was the hardest night I have seen since I have been a soldier.
>
> I expect we shall cross the James River before long. The rebels left shells for us to step on. Some of our men were hurt by them. They are the most cowardly enemy I have ever heard of. I should think they would be ashamed.
>
> Nothing more to write this time. Write often.
>
> Your aff. Son,
>
> Henry

## Editorial Notes

Henry is writing from inside the rebel fortifications he has been approaching for a month — a satisfying narrative arc across the collection. The "shells for us to step on" are Confederate torpedoes (buried artillery shells with pressure fuses), one of the earliest uses of land mines in warfare and widely condemned by both sides. McClellan ordered Confederate prisoners to dig them up. Henry's night march — rain, mud, stopping every ten paces with divisions stacked ahead — is the most physically grueling experience he has described. His expectation of crossing the James River suggests confusion about the campaign's next moves; McClellan would actually advance up the Peninsula toward Richmond via the York and Pamunkey Rivers, not the James.
