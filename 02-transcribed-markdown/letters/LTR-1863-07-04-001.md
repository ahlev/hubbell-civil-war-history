## Full Transcription

> July 4th, 1863
> Near Gettysburg, Penna
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> God has again in his great kindness preserved my life through another most terrific battle. I have not received a scratch. The reg't. lost fifty-two — eleven killed and forty-some wounded. We commenced fighting about 2 P.M. on the 2nd inst. (We had been under artillery fire for some hours previous) and continued fighting until about midnight. We fought nearly all day yesterday. If God spares my life, I will endeavor to write you again soon and give you particulars.
>
> Please tell Capt. Ransome that Milebus Ayres of my Co. was killed and Sergeant Brookway and Private John Tickle were wounded. We have gained a great victory. We are burying our and the rebel's dead today. Love to all.
>
> Your aff. Son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
>
> P.S. Our Captain deserves especial praise for his bravery.

## Editorial Notes

This is the letter the family had been dreading and hoping for since Alexander marched on June 4th. Written on July 4th — Independence Day — from the Gettysburg battlefield, it is the collection's most historically significant document. Alexander survived without a scratch, but his regiment lost 52 men in two days of fighting.

The 60th NY fought on Culp's Hill, the right flank of the Union line. The fighting Alexander describes — from 2 PM on July 2nd through midnight — was the desperate defense of Culp's Hill against Ewell's Corps. Greene's Brigade (including the 60th NY) held the breastworks while other 12th Corps units were pulled away to reinforce the left. It was one of the most critical defensive actions of the entire battle.

"We are burying our and the rebel's dead today." On Independence Day, 1863, Alexander Hubbell was burying the dead at Gettysburg. The irony needs no editorial comment.
