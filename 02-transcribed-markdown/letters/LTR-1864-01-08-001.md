## Full Transcription

> Jan. 8, 1864
> Lolliecoffer House
> Nashville, Tenn.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I know you think I am a very naughty boy not to write you oftener, but as I have said before, it is hard for me to write when I get no answers to my letters. This state of things I trust will not last much longer, as I am now on my way to Savannah to join the regt. To accomplish this I was obliged to leave Chattanooga clandestinely. I suppose I am reported at my detachment as a deserted, but this will do me no harm as I shall be able to join the regt. before my report can reach there. I smuggled my way through to this place with a detachment of the 18th Corps on their way to Sherman. Our detachment is still on duty with General Thomas. Some are at Dalton — some on the railroad, etc. I did not like the way things worked and determined to get to the regt. if it took a leg. I reported to the Colonel commanding those barracks and was booked by him for Savannah.
>
> We shall go by the way of New York. A great many of the men will go home when they get into the state. I should like to, but shall not be able. I have not heard from home since I left Savannah. How are the boys? Is James in as good spirits as ever? How many I should like to see them. Much love to Frances and say that I shall endeavor to write her a long, long letter soon. I feel quite sad tonight. I suppose Frances would say that I have the blues. I shall wait until I feel a little more cheerful to finish the letter. Did ever you see a postscript longer than the letter itself before?
>
> April 8th — The mail has come in this morning bringing me a N.Y. Tribune from home. It was mailed March 31st. I hope to get a letter soon. I shall send this off if I can manage to borrow a stamp. O go on guard at half after nine today. Shall be at the fence or stockade.
>
> I shall come home, probably, unless I should fail to pass the medical examination at New York.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Alexander deserts his detachment to rejoin his regiment — and calls it "clandestinely" with evident satisfaction. "I suppose I am reported at my detachment as a deserter, but this will do me no harm." The irony of the same army that executed deserters in September now having one of its sergeants desert a rear-area assignment to get back to the fighting is quintessentially Alexander. He didn't desert from the war — he deserted toward it.

"I feel quite sad tonight." After months of brave letters, the mask slips. Alexander is alone at a Nashville hotel, far from his regiment, technically a deserter, heading to Savannah via New York, missing home. It's the only time in the collection he admits to "the blues."
