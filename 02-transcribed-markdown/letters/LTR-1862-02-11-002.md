## Full Transcription

> Feb. 11th, 1862
> Camp McClellan
>
> Dear Charles:
>
> I was very glad to receive a letter from you. It has been a very mild winter thus far, not any snow worth mentioning, but it has been very muddy. It freezes very hard nights. Tonight we are having quite a snow storm, but it will not last long.
>
> There was quite a row in our Co. tonight about six o'clock, all from the cause of getting drunk. One of our Co. drank a little to much of the good thing. I think it was beer, and was making a good deal of disturbance when the Officer of the day came along and ordered him to the guard house, but he would not go. He said unless two of the guard and a Corporal went with him. He was perfectly right there, for he has been through the Mexican war, and the war in India, and of course knows all about the rules of war. Not long since another of our Co. got to fighting, and was completely covered with mud.
>
> You wished to know if I had heard about the soldiers alloting part of their pay to their families. I have heard or at least I have read about it, but there is a great many in this Regt. and in our Co. that spend most of their pay to the Sutlers. George Dodds, for instance, has used up all his wages thus far and is now borrowing, but I would not mention it. There are several others in our Co. in the same way. I have received my pay for the floor and stove. The floor we had to leave in Since. We live quite comfortable with our stove. I have bought a pocket diary. It cost me 50 cts. It will be very convenient to
>
> (Continuation of Feb. 11, 1862.)
>
> note down everything. I must tell you how we live. We do not have tin plates, but eat dog fashion. That just expresses it. We do not mind now and then to eat a pound or two of dirt. Good enough for soldiers, don't you think so. We have fresh meat every two or three days and bread. Have boiled pork or salt junk every morning and hot coffee and hard bread. Soup every noon, and tea every night. We receive our rations every ten days. We have sugar of course. None of our Co. save their rations as I know of, but I think as a general thing they are used up. I am sure if I cooked my own rations I should save a good deal, but as it is, can not.
>
> I remain your aff. Brother,
>
> Henry

## Editorial Notes

**CHRONOLOGICAL ANCHOR: CAMP McCLELLAN, FEB. 11–13, 1862**

This letter serves as a critical anchor in the Henry Hubbell chronology. He was definitively at Camp McClellan on Feb. 11–13, 1862. Combined with his other letters from Camp McClellan dated Feb. 12 (two letters), this establishes a cluster of correspondence from a specific location and date range. This contradicts the Feb. 5 "Camp Gorman, Bolivar Heights" letters and strongly suggests those earlier letters are misdated.

**SOLDIER DISCIPLINE & DRUNKENNESS**

The drunk soldier incident shows military discipline in action. A soldier (possibly a Mexican War veteran) correctly invokes military law to resist arrest without proper escort. Henry notes this knowledge approvingly ("He was perfectly right there"), indicating his respect for due process even in military context. The incident was resolved (soldier went to guard house), but it illustrates the challenges officers faced managing troops.

**FINANCIAL HARDSHIP & SUTLER DEBT**

Henry provides the first clear evidence of financial problems among soldiers. George Dodds spent all wages and is "now borrowing" — from comrades, one assumes. Henry asks Charles not to publicize this ("I would not mention it"), showing protective concern for a comrade's reputation. The broader context: soldiers' pay was small ($8.65/month), sutler prices were high, and credit was available (and used). This prefigures the money-anxious letters Alexander will write later.

**DETAILED DIET & RATION SYSTEM**

Henry provides a rare, detailed record of soldier diet:
- **Breakfast/Morning:** Boiled pork or "salt junk" (salt pork), hot coffee, hard bread
- **Noon:** Soup
- **Evening:** Tea
- **Occasional:** Fresh meat (every 2–3 days), regular bread
- **Ration cycle:** Received every 10 days
- **Cooking method:** Company mess cooks; Henry and others prepare meals
- **Eating method:** "Eat dog fashion" — no tin plates provided; using hands or improvised utensils
- **Sanitation:** "Do not mind now and then to eat a pound or two of dirt" — acceptance of poor hygiene

This vivid description shows both the reality of camp life and Henry's humorous resignation to it.

**POCKET DIARY PURCHASE**

Henry bought a pocket diary for 50 cents — a significant expenditure for a soldier, suggesting he wanted to keep a personal record. If this diary survives, it would be an invaluable primary source.

**"SINCE" LOCATION UNCLEAR**

The phrase "The floor we had to leave in Since" is unclear. "Since" may be:
1. A place name (Seneca Mills? Fort Singe?)
2. A misspelling or mishearing (Since → Seneca? → Science?)
3. An abbreviation or local term

This requires clarification from the original handwritten document.

## Event: Drunken Soldier & Guard Duty Rules (Feb. 11, 1862)

An unidentified soldier in Co. D became intoxicated (on beer, Henry guesses) and disorderly. When the Officer of the day ordered him to the guard house, the soldier refused unless accompanied by two guards and a corporal — invoking military law correctly. The soldier's knowledge came from service in the Mexican War and a campaign in India. Henry approves of the soldier's legal knowledge and correct procedure, suggesting proper military discipline was expected even in disciplining drunks. The soldier was eventually taken to guard house. This was a routine military incident but illustrates soldier morale, discipline problems, and Henry's pragmatic acceptance of such issues.

## Event: Ration System & Camp Cooking (Feb. 11–13, 1862)

Henry describes in detail how soldiers in camp lived. Rations arrived every 10 days. Cooking was done in company mess by soldiers assigned that day (Henry mentions his "turn"). Meals were basic but adequate: fresh meat every 2–3 days, preserved pork daily, coffee, bread, soup, tea. Eating was "dog fashion" without tin plates. Men were expected to accept occasional dirt in food and poor sanitation. The system was inefficient — rations were not carefully conserved, though Henry notes he would save more if cooking his own. This reveals the day-to-day reality of soldier life: boring, repetitive, unhygienic, but survivable.
