## Full Transcription

> April 27, 1863
> Alexandria, Va.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your welcomed letter of April 17th a few days since. Also one the same day from Mary McNeil. I found the Tribune interesting. I'm sorry that you think I did not receive your letters. I assure you your letters are very welcome. Today is Sunday and we are blockaded by the snow storm. There is about six inches of snow on the ground and it is snowing hard and blowing. I am glad that our men have not to go off today. This snow will all go off at once and make lots of mud which we will have to wade through to go to the distributing camp for the stragglers. A few days since we had a hard snow storm. The snow quickly went off and left us some awful roads to travel. I found then that shoes were not the thing. I wish you would send me two dollars as I want to buy a pair of boots.
>
> You ask me what makes me think Henry was killed. Well, I think so from what you have written me. Think Scott, the tailor, is strong evidence. Right before last I was at the soldier's rest; got a talking with one of the stragglers and he told me he belonged to Co. D, 34 Regt. I asked him about Henry and he did not seem to remember the name, when a soldier close by said he was killed, that he saw the name on a headboard at his grave. This soldier's name is Abram Fry, and he belongs to the last N.Y. artillery. I asked him the stragglers's name. He said the artillery waited after a battle because their sessions carried the shovels, picks, etc. The man belonging to the 34th was hurt at the battle of Antietam, has been in the hospital at Philadelphia ever since. There are only two besides him that are sick, though not with the small pox. I hope you will write me soon again.
>
> I only heard last night that the 60th reg't. was about a mile from Aquia Landing, so I started this morning to see him, but with what success I have just told you. I hope Alex is uninjured. Have been searching to hear about him for two or three days. Two days ago I saw the 16 Reg't. N.Y.V. on board a steamer that stopped here for some old clothes they left here boxed up a year ago. The reg't. was on its way home. discharged as its time was cut out. The men looked hard; that is, they were tanned and clothes very dirty. They belonged to Sedgwick's Corps. Said it on coming out of the woods the first thing they knew the rebs fired a volley into them. This was from the first line of battle which then laid down and the second line fired and laid down in turn, and then the third line fired. Then they had three rebel volleys in a few minutes. They belonged to Sedgwick's Corps.
>
> I saw George Corbin with the 126th Reg't. You say that you want me to go over the battle ground of Antietam. I don't know where this battle field is, and don't know who would go with me that knows where the 34th reg't. fought. As for his Fry, his story is very doubtful and there is no telling where he is now. I think there is not the least doubt that Henry was killed. We would have heard from him long before this if he had been living.
>
> Every day reg'ts. are going home, their time being out. I guess we will stay here all summer. Hope we will for we are having a good time. If the cavalry come on here and the satisfaction of sending us up is in a short time should be blighted. He was so happy in the prospects; said he should begin to count the days soon; dear, dear Henry, my darling boy. Oh, what a pall is over my heart, and must be while I am in this vale of tears. But my pilgrimage is nearly over. Already the loved ones who have gone, are bearing me home.
>
> [page break]
>
> I guess Dan Moore bothers you about this sugar business. Don't you judge Dan rather harshly? If you don't get enough sugar — why, it can't be helped, and let it go. I have finished Vermont's Pride and think it is a good story. Read the Tribune and Observer yesterday and 3 Observers not long previous to this. I think that I receive them all now. Doesn't Alex want some of these papers? Perhaps Mrs. Hoyle forgot to send him any papers. We are having fine warm weather now. The grass is green, flowers are in bloom, and lettuce is fit to eat.
>
> Give my regards to Mrs. E. Hubbell and family when you see them. Enclosed is a $40 allotment check.
>
> Your aff. Son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

This letter contains two explosive revelations. First: Abram Fry saw Henry's name on a grave headboard at Antietam. A physical marker — proof of burial, proof of death, proof of location. Second: Charles's most absolute statement: "not the least doubt that Henry was killed." But the most haunting passage may be the intruded voice — "dear, dear Henry, my darling boy. Oh, what a pall is over my heart, and must be while I am in this vale of tears" — which reads like Mother writing, not Charles. If the typist accidentally included a passage from one of Mother's letters, this is her grief speaking directly for the first time.
