## Full Transcription

> Dec. 8, 1861
> Camp Jackson
>
> My Dear Mother:
>
> Don't you think I was glad to hear from home, eh? Why when I received those four letters I hardly knew whether I was on my head or feet. You wish me to acknowledge all the letters that I receive. I have received one mailed Nov. 18, another Nov. 6th. (I believe the figures are not very plain.) Another Dec. 3 and one from Henry mailed Dec. 4. I have not received any papers as yet. I never enjoyed anything more than the reading of those letters.
>
> We received our pay last Friday morning up to the first of November. I received seventeen dollars and thirty-three cents. I have sent home fifteen dollars. The Capt. sends it for me. He sends it to George Dunning. Dunning will pay it over to you as soon as he received it. Now, my dear Mother, I give it all to you. This is the first time that I have been able to make my Mother a present and I want you to take it. If you do not I shall feel very, very bad. I want you to buy you a nice warm cloak for winter or else a warm woolen dress and a warm pair of gaiters. Now if you disobey my orders I shall feel very much like having you courtmartialed. It is rather a bad thing to fall into the hands of such a court. You had not better disobey. I give you fair warning.
>
> Quite a sad accident happened night before last. (Luther and I were on guard duty). David Dava[?]way, one of the pickets on a bridge a little below the camp, while stepping off from a wall, in some way stumbled and dropt his rifle. He endeavored to catch the piece with his right hand, but before he could do so it struck against a stone and went off. The ball passed through his right thumb between the first and second joints and almost graised his head. His thumb was taken off. He will be discharged. When I go out with my reliefs, I am oblige to caution the men with "men be careful how you carry your rifles". They sometimes carry them very carelessly. Tell Frances I shall never forget the text she wrote in my bible, "God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble." He is such indeed to me. Love to all.
>
> Your affectionate son,
>
> Alexander
>
> Dec. 9 — We are now having very warm weather. The weather today promises to be quite sultry. I was really uncomfortable yesterday on account of the heat. Luther and I are on guard duty today. Send me papers as often as you can. We have nothing to read here. Tell Frances I will write her soon. I shall give you the direction again for fear that you will forget them. Direct to Baltimore, Md. Sixtieth Regt. N.Y. S. A. Co. H, care of Capt. Ransom.
>
> Your affectionate son,
>
> Alexander
>
> P.S. You ask me in one of your letters "how I like soldiering" and to be candid xxxx about it, I like it very well. Mother, I am dependent on no one. That is a great comfort to me. I shall try and get a pass tomorrow to go to Baltimore and have my miniature taken.

## Editorial Notes

This letter is a treasure. Alexander's playful threat to "courtmartial" his Mother if she doesn't use his $15 for a warm cloak shows the family dynamic at its warmest. It's his first pay and his first gift, and he's adamant she spend it on herself. The accidental shooting of Dava[?]way (the third such incident across the collection) reinforces how dangerous careless weapon handling was — Alexander takes responsibility, cautioning his relief parties. The Bible verse from Frances (Psalm 46:1) functions as a family talisman carried into the field. Most critically, the P.S. mailing address definitively confirms Alexander's unit: Co. H, 60th New York Volunteers. His comment "I am dependent on no one — that is a great comfort to me" may hint at pre-war family dynamics or his youthful desire for self-sufficiency.
