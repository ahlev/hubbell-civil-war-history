## Full Transcription

> March 7, 1864
> Stevenson, Ala.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I rec'd. your welcome letters of Feb. 14th, 18th, and 22rd and also one enclosed from Frances yesterday. Am sorry to hear that Charley has left Wash. Had hoped he would remain there all summer. I hope he has gone to Texas. I do not think there will be much fighting there. I expect you and Mrs. Bostwick are having dolorous times together, eh? I have written Mrs. Bostwick today — so that she will understand matters and things generally.
>
> Have you heard from James yet? I hope, and I believe, he keeps a stout heart. There is nothing like it. I hope he will get a commission. James is young yet, and the world is wide — there is no reason why he should not retrieve his fortunes.
>
> I hope this war will end soon. There were quite a number of deserters from Johnson's army and from Longstreet's. They give a most dolorous acc't. of the affairs of the so-called Southern Confederacy. They say that Johnson's army is suffering terribly for want of food — that one days rations is scarcely sufficient for one meal, and that the men are determined not to stand at the next fight. They say they never want to be nearer home than they were in the rebel army. Longstreet's men also give a very doleful acc't. They and the 62d Corps are very well acquainted. They asked us if we had not rather be in "the Potomac army than out here." Said they fared very much better while they were under Lee. Said the Confederacy was played out and that it was only the pride of the leaders that kept it up.
>
> One Div. of our Corps will doubtless be sent to the front. I hope it will not be ours. It is said that General Slocum said that if any troops were sent from our Corps to the front it should be the 1st Div., as we had done our share last fall. General Geary, I guess, does not think so, and I am afraid will not rest until he gets us up there. We will doubtless know in a few weeks what we shall do this summer. I hope they will give the men a sound drubbing this summer in Va. I will appoint you and Frances my business agents in Champlain. I do not know that I can give you any more directions. Averill is to get my county bounty and my third of Underhill's. Marshall Dunn will give you my state bounty. Please pay him five dollars for his fee. I think Averill will deal honorably with me. If he plays any rascality it will be very easily proved on him as the receipts for bounty paid at the offices of the Supervisor of the town and the County Clerk will show what amount he received. I shall finish up what I have to say in my letter to Miss Fannie.
>
> Your aff. Son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

The Confederate deserters provide the most detailed picture yet of the Confederacy's internal collapse — food rations for one meal that are supposed to last a day, men determined not to fight again, the admission that "the Confederacy was played out and that it was only the pride of the leaders that kept it up." Longstreet's men saying they "fared very much better while they were under Lee" reveals the fracture between the Virginia and Western theaters even within Confederate ranks.

Alexander appoints Mother and Frances as his "business agents in Champlain" — managing bounty collections, dealing with Averill and the County Clerk. The women at home are running the family's financial affairs while three sons serve.
