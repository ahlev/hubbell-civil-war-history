## Full Transcription

> March 23, 1862
> Washington
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received a letter from you this morning. Nothing does me more good than to have a letter from home. Our Regt. left Harpers Ferry yesterday morning for Washington, a distance of eighty-three miles on a freight train. We came through in twenty-four hours. Arrived last night about two. We left the cars and marched through the City a little over a mile, and are now in brick barracks. Shall probably remain here until we are paid off. I have nothing to pay the sutler this time.
>
> I don't know where our Regt. os destined to go. Will know in a few days. The bridge across the river at Harpers Ferry is finished. The cars run across now. It was a perfect jam coming through on the train, but that is better than marching. I have had enough of that for a while.
>
> Our Regt. goes to mess about half a mile from the barracks. It reminds me very much of Albany, only we do not live quite so well. Have bread, coffee and salt junk. That is better than hard crackers. Today is the Sabbath, but it does not seem much like it. Cars running all the while. A good many of the soldiers got drunk. The provost guard goes around the City picking up all straglers, and those that go out without a pass. The pass has to be signed by the Gen. Two have been taken prisoners today. I mean to look out for one, and not be taken.
>
> I am glad that you have let out the Sugar-bush. Look out and have an honest man. I shall be glad when this war closes, and be at home again. It seems more like fall than winter, no snow, but mud.
>
> I have nothing more of importance to write this time. Henry Cooper and Jacen, I think, will have to get their discharge. Both of them have been sick. Have not been able to do duty for some time. Write often.
>
> I remain your aff. Son,
>
> Henry

## Editorial Notes

The 34th NY's movement from Harpers Ferry to Washington (83 miles in 24 hours by freight train) marks the regiment's transition from the Shenandoah Valley to the Washington defenses — likely staging for the Peninsula Campaign or reassignment. Henry's observation that Washington on the Sabbath doesn't feel like it — cars running, soldiers drunk, provost guard rounding up stragglers — contrasts with his Harpers Ferry quiet. The sugar-bush being "let out" confirms the family is managing the farm economy without the two older brothers. Henry Cooper and Jacen being discharged for illness is part of the steady attrition of the 34th NY through disease rather than combat.
