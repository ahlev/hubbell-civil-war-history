## Full Transcription

> Feb. 12th, 1862
> Camp McClellan
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I was very glad to receive a letter from you. We are having very mild weather, but very muddy. I am shure I should not like to live in this part of the country. I would rather have cold weather for my part.
>
> I received two letters from Alex at the same time. I received yours. He seems to be in good spirits. There is nothing that does us so much good as a letter from home. My health is pretty good now. Have a good appetite. Your letter did me a great deal of good. I am sorry to write that there is a great deal of drunkenness in the Company and some fighting, but I keep out of it all. The hardest of soldiering is guard duty. Have to be up about all night, but I have got so I do not mind it much. I will be glad when we make a move of some kind. I have no much to write now as I wrote a short time ago. Nothing more at present.
>
> I remain your most aff. Son,
>
> Henry

## Editorial Notes

**SHORT LETTER, HIGH EMOTIONAL VALUE**

This is one of Henry's shortest letters, but it expresses something important: the critical role of home correspondence in soldier morale. Henry explicitly states that "There is nothing that does us so much good as a letter from home" — and that the mother's letter "did me a great deal of good." For soldiers in winter quarters, far from home, letters were the primary connection to civilian life and family. This short note, though containing little news, conveys gratitude and love.

**GUARD DUTY AS HARDSHIP**

Henry identifies guard duty as "The hardest of soldiering" — having to "be up about all night." Yet he notes he's "got so I do not mind it much," suggesting he's adapting. This reveals the physical toll of military service: exhaustion from night watch, the need to adjust to hardship. By Feb. 12, he's been in service ~9 months and is becoming inured to the worst aspects.

**DRUNKENNESS & FIGHTING IN COMPANY**

Henry expresses sorrow at "a great deal of drunkenness in the Company and some fighting" — but notes he "keeps out of it all." This suggests alcohol use was a major problem in camp (consistent with other letters), and fights often broke out. Henry's self-discipline in avoiding these troubles is noteworthy: he maintained temperance and avoided violence while surrounded by men succumbing to both vices.

**EAGERNESS TO MOVE**

Henry states: "I will be glad when we make a move of some kind." This suggests restlessness in winter quarters and perhaps eagerness to see action (or at least to move to a new location). Seven months later, the 34th NY would move south and see action at Antietam, where Henry would be killed.

**WEATHER & CLIMATE COMPLAINT**

Henry repeats his complaint about the Virginia/Maryland climate: he would "rather have cold weather" than the current "very mild weather" with mud. Cold is bearable; mud is objectionable. This suggests the region was uncomfortably warm and damp in February — conditions that might promote disease (which killed more soldiers than combat).

**ALEXANDER'S LETTERS & INTER-REGIMENTAL COMMUNICATION**

Henry received "two letters from Alex at the same time" — evidence that inter-regimental mail was regular and that the brothers maintained close contact. Alexander's letters are an important source of family and military news across regiments (34th NY vs. 60th NY).

## Event: Guard Duty as Life's Hardship (Ongoing)

Henry identifies guard duty — staying awake most of the night standing watch — as the single hardest aspect of soldiering. This was not unique to the 34th NY; night guard duty was universally recognized as exhausting and dangerous (friendly fire, cold exposure, sleep deprivation). By mid-February 1862, Henry has adjusted somewhat: "I have got so I do not mind it much." This shows adaptation to military hardship, but also the cumulative toll of nine months of service. Soldiers who survived the physical demands of guard duty in winter quarters would face far worse in coming campaigns.

---

## Event: Mother's Letter as Morale Boost (Feb. 12, 1862)

The arrival of a letter from his mother had significant morale impact on Henry. He explicitly states: "There is nothing that does us so much good as a letter from home" and "Your letter did me a great deal of good." This underscores the centrality of home correspondence to soldier morale during the Civil War. Despite the brevity of this particular response, Henry's gratitude is clear. The mother's letters were one of the primary mechanisms by which the Hubbell family maintained emotional bonds across the distance of war.
