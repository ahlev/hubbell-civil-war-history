## Full Transcription

> Feb. 24, 1864
> Charleston, S.C.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> We reached this place yesterday afternoon after a march of some sixty miles. I have not yet been around to see the lions of the place; but shall as soon as possible. The Johnnies must have left here in considerable haste, if I may judge by the appearance of things, for in several of the forts I have passed the guns were left unmuzzled and fit for immediate use. Close to here, they burned the carriages of two guns, but left several others by them unharmed. Two enormous rifled guns lay beside the track ready to be loaded onto cars. I should judge, but I expect Kilpatrick slipped them us on that calculation by tearing up the railroads. They must have left an immense amount of heavy ordnance here. Before writing again I will try and see all things and give you them a minute description.
>
> Sherman is off again and there is no telling when we shall get to him. I presume likely we shall take transports from this place. Love to all. Remember me to Mrs. B.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

The header says "Charleston, S.C." but this is almost certainly Charleston, Tennessee (Bradley County), not Charleston, South Carolina. Alexander's 60th NY was operating in the Tennessee/Georgia theater, and a 60-mile march from Stephenson, Alabama, would reach Charleston, Tennessee — not South Carolina. The typist likely expanded "S.C." incorrectly, or Alexander abbreviated the state incorrectly. Charleston, Tennessee, was a key railroad junction on the East Tennessee & Georgia Railroad.

The abandoned Confederate ordnance tells the story of a retreating army — guns left ready to fire because Kilpatrick's cavalry had torn up the railroads needed to remove them. The Confederates burned what they could and left the rest.
