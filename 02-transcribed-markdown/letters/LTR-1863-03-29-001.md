## Full Transcription

> March 29, 1863
> Aquia Landing
>
> Dear Mother and Sister:
>
> I received your letter of the 20th inst, a day or two since, and was much displeased with a certain part of it. Why! Mother, I am really astonished that you should say what you did: "Write the surgeon to assign me a place of safety in time of battle!" Haven't Wherever my reg't goes, I will go (God willing). Do you think, Mother, — really think that I could let my company go into battle without my being with them to bear my part of the danger? I am astonished you should counsel such a thing. Now, dear Mother, for my sake do not write the surgeon. Would you have me branded as a coward? Though I love my life as dearly as any one could love it, yet I have too much respect for myself and country to play any such part as that. Should heartless, who have so much at stake, be a whit behind those noble foreigners who are doing so much to save our country from ruin? "No! No!" I know you will say, Mother. A few vigorous and heavy blows dealt soon will, I think, finish this rebellion. You people at the North must give us your support. There is no way of settling this rebellion by the bayonet, and we can, and **will** do it. Oh! what a happy day it will be to me when I can come home, this war over and our glorious Union restored to its former greatness. There is a certain tailor in Rutland I should like then to see.
>
> Well, Mother, so you have let Mr. Clark have the horse and heifer to take care of. A very good idea, I think, and also that of letting out the sugar bush for one hundred and fifty pounds of sugar. 150 lbs. of sugar is a matter not to be despised in these times.
>
> And so, Miss Fannie, you think Silas is in a fair way to take to himself a rib, eh? (one of the Misses Crowley, perhaps.) An extra rib would do him no harm, I think.
>
> There is a very loyal and wealthy widow in Baltimore whom the boys of this Co. thought the Captain thought a great deal of. She is a noble woman and thinks a great deal of the reg't., perhaps partly on account of the Capt. Many a poor soldier has had reason to bless her.
>
> We are expecting our pay next week and will you please write and inform me of the best way of sending my money home? I am now out of stamps. Will you send me two or three? Love to all. Remember me to all my friends, especially to the Capt. I received a long letter from him a day or two since. And please give my regards to Mr. Clark.
>
> Yours with much affection,
>
> Alexander F. Hubbell

## Significance Flags

### Historical Significance: `major`

> **ALEXANDER REFUSES MOTHER'S PLEA FOR SAFETY.** Mother asked Alexander to write the surgeon for "a place of safety in time of battle" — Alexander's response is fierce: "Would you have me branded as a coward?" He will not hide while his company fights. "There is no way of settling this rebellion by the bayonet, and we can, and will do it." This is Alexander's most passionate defense of his duty and honor. The passage is devastatingly ironic given what Chancellorsville and Gettysburg will bring.

### Emotional Intensity: `high`

> Indignant, passionate, patriotic. Alexander is wounded by Mother's suggestion — "I am astonished you should counsel such a thing." But the indignation melts into tenderness: "Though I love my life as dearly as any one could love it."

## Cross-Reference Notes

### Connections to Other Letters in Collection
- **Mother's fear:** After losing Henry (almost certainly killed), Mother asked Alexander to seek a safety assignment. This reveals the depth of her terror — she has already lost one son and cannot bear to lose another. Alexander's refusal is honorable but terrifying in context.
- **Parallel to Henry:** Charles's Jan 2 letter said "when we were called out the roll was called and every man was obliged to go." Alexander now says the same thing differently: he will not be a coward. Neither brother will hide. Henry didn't either.
- **"Foreigners" doing their part:** Alexander's praise of "noble foreigners" saving the country contrasts sharply with the anti-immigrant sentiment of earlier letters. The Irish and German soldiers are now his comrades.

## Editorial Notes

This is the most emotionally intense letter Alexander has written. Mother, who has almost certainly lost Henry and hasn't had definitive word in six months, begged Alexander to ask the surgeon for a safe assignment. Alexander's response is a collision between maternal love and masculine honor: "Would you have me branded as a coward?" He means it — he will go where his company goes. But the letter also contains the sentence "Though I love my life as dearly as any one could love it," which is not the statement of a man eager to die. He loves his life. He will risk it anyway. In six weeks he will be at Chancellorsville.
