## Full Transcription

> Sept. 30/62
> Plattsburgh Barracks
>
> Dear Mother —
>
> I received your letter today. I was sorry to hear about Henry. But then it may be all for the best. If he is a prisoner at Richmond as I think he is, he is safer than in our army. I think the wound in his leg prevented his running away. Unless he is exchanged we may not hear from him for sometime. As there is no mail between the south & north it will be some time before we can hear from him.
>
> I shall try & come home this week but it depends upon what time we are mustered into the service. All the men have to be mustered in together.
>
> I did not know anything about Henry till nine ock. last night. Alek Douglas told me. I was inquiring about the 34 regt.
>
> Aff. Son
>
> C. F. Hubbell
>
> I hope you will send newspapers to the boys.

## Editorial Notes

Charles's letter is strikingly different in tone from Mother's. Where she is shattered, he is pragmatic: "it may be all for the best." He constructs a hopeful narrative — Henry is a prisoner, prisoners are safer than soldiers, the leg wound just prevented escape. This is a twenty-year-old trying to process the first real blow the family has taken. His brevity and the postscript ("send newspapers to the boys") suggest he doesn't yet understand the magnitude of what's happening. He will learn.
