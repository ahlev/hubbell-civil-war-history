## Full Transcription

> June 13th, 1864
> Camp on the Bank of the
> Mississippi River —
> Morganza, La.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your welcome letter of May 21st a few days since. Am glad that you and Frances are both well. I accidentally saw in a newspaper a short time since that Buzzard's Roost is where Gen. Sherman is encamped. From that I learned that Alex is not in the Army of the Potomac. I am glad of it.
>
> In your letter you spoke of receiving a letter from Alex dated B. Roost, but I supposed that B. Roost was in Va. I have seen no account of Sherman fighting. Newspapers are very acceptable here and I hope that I will receive one from you soon. I send this letter in the express package which contains $400. Twenty dollars of this money is sent by Joseph Martin to his wife. He is a Frenchman from Champlain belonging to our Co. If his wife does not call on you for it, he wants you to let her know that you have received it. Fifty dollars I send and James the balance, which is $330. Think I shall insure it, which with the express charges will cost $2.00 on a hundred. James says he wants his invested in some safe place. Mr. Savage can tell you where it would be a good place to invest. I would as lief let them borrow it if they want it. If you need any of my money I hope you will use it freely at all times. Two or three days since we were paid four months pay, paid up to the 1st of May. James rec'd no pay this pay day, but will next. It is said here that soldiers will receive $16 a month from the 1 of May. Is this true? James has been talking with one of the soldiers that came here with him who told him that the $800 he rec'd was N.Y. bounty. James also rec'd one month's pay, making $873 but he has received. There is due James yet $75, N.Y. State bounty and $242 U.S., which is to be paid at different times. I wish Frances would keep an account of the money each of us sends home and where it is put out. James says he will write in a day or two.
>
> Hoping that you and Frances try to enjoy life I will close. A few days since we went out foraging. Two regts. went with a long wagon train about fifteen miles. We rode out and walked back. When the wagon train came where the corn houses were — plenty — pulled off the side boards of the barns and filled the wagons with corn. The corn had the husks on. The women did not like to see the corn taken. Please write soon.
>
> From your aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

"The women did not like to see the corn taken." Charles's characteristically flat observation of the war's impact on Southern civilians. The boards pulled off barns, the corn loaded into wagons, the women watching — foraging that is indistinguishable from theft, sanctioned by necessity. Charles reports it without judgment, which is its own kind of judgment.

The financial complexity of three soldiers' pay, bounties, and investments flowing through Mother and Frances in Champlain is remarkable. Express packages, insurance, investment advice from Mr. Savage, county bonds, state bounties due in installments — the home front as financial operations center.
