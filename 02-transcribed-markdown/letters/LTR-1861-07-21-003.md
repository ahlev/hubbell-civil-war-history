## Full Transcription

> (On same sheet)
>
> Dear Frances:
>
> You must excuse me for writing on the same paper but could not think of sending this without writing a few lines as I received so good a letter from you. I am enjoying comparatively good health thus far. There are several that are sick. I am glad if you are pleased with my picture. Albert thought it was perfect, as did others. I shall get a better one taken when pay day comes around and let you have it.
>
> I suppose you would like to know how I pass my time when not on drill. I find it very dull unless I have something to read. Happy to say I have two papers to read. We drill five hours a day. Rise at five in the morning, retire at nine in the eve. The Colonel told each Capt. last night that the men were improving in drilling. Each camp gave him three cheers. I have written to Nellie and Aunt L. Was a real treat to read their letters. Want me to write often which I intend to do. There is not much of importance going on now. I shall have to go on guard before a great while. They will never find me sleeping while on guard. If you would send me one dollar I should like it. Our letters are franked now and then. I want to get a shirt washed. Please excuse this scribble. My pen is not good. Please write often if possible. Shall have more to write next time.
>
> I remain your Aff. Brother,
>
> Henry
>
> Later - We are ordered off. Shall probably leave in the morning. Ready tonight. May leave before morning. There has been a hard battle today. Have not heard much about it yet. You need not send any money now, till I write.

## Significance Flags

### Historical Significance: `major`

> Writing from Washington, Henry fills most of this letter to his sister Frances with the ordinary rhythm of camp — five hours of daily drill, the Colonel's praise drawing three cheers, two newspapers to pass the dull hours, a dollar begged to wash a shirt. Then a postscript breaks the calm: "We are ordered off... There has been a hard battle today." He doesn't yet know it is Bull Run, only to leave by morning — and tells her not to send the money after all.

### Emotional Intensity: `high`

> The contrast between the calm body of the letter (drill, reading, laundry) and the urgent postscript ("We are ordered off... Ready tonight. May leave before morning") captures the sudden shift from camp routine to war mobilization.

## Editorial Notes

This letter's power lies in the whiplash between its body and its postscript. Henry spends most of the letter on ordinary camp life — drill schedules, reading newspapers, wanting a dollar for laundry, sending his picture — and then appends a postscript that changes everything: "We are ordered off." The postscript also contains a telling practical reversal: he'd just asked for money, then writes "You need not send any money now, till I write" — because he doesn't know where he'll be.

The Union defeat at Bull Run sent shockwaves through Washington. Thousands of panicked soldiers and civilians streamed back into the city. Henry's regiment, apparently held as city guard, was now mobilized. Within two weeks he would be in Maryland.
