## Full Transcription

> March 19, 1863
> Alexandria, Va.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> Your letter of March 10th received; glad to hear from you. I have not heard from Alex since I saw him. He is not a good correspondent and I am not one either as I promised to write him and have not done so. Last night I had just come from changing the one o'clock guard when a man fried fire. One sentry ran across the road and pounded on the door of the engine house with his gun crying "Fire." This engine house and the two steam fire engines it contains belong to Uncle Sam. This engine house and the steam fire engines are new engines, just
>
> [page break]
>
> come. Well, as the horses are kept harnessed night and day and fuel in the engines ready for the match, the engines were soon at the fire with steam up ready to commence throwing water. The engine hose was screwed on to the hydrant and a stream of water began to play on the burning houses. I heard that a Captain's wife living in one of the houses had several hundred dollars in money burnt up. The engines prevented the spread of the fire so that only four buildings were burned, thanks to Uncle Sam's engines, which were brought here to protect govt. property. There are only two posts where we have sentries, one at the door where we go into our camp and the other in the city guarding some guns. Two corporals a day are on, each taking half the day and half the night. We are having easy times and I hope we will stay here.
>
> No liquor is allowed to be brought into the city except for hospitals and the private stores of officers. But it is sold slyly still. Some of our men got drunk; they were taken to the provost marshall's office and compelled to maser where they got their liquor. The result was that an Irish woman who sold the whiskey and one of our men were sent to the slave pen for ten days. There is a corporal here by the name of Beardsley who, our orderly says, is a great fool, only he don't know it. Tells about when he taught school so that he was called school master by the serpeant at Plattsburgh. He is a conceited but a good natured fellow. Well, I have received three wrappers from his sister. I did not know that he had a sister till I had received a paper and asked him. I think she may keep sending if she wants to, but I will not send her an entire stranger, any letters. She is careful to put her name on the papers.
>
> I received two Tribunes today; have received two before, so that I have not read all you have sent. I find the Tribune interesting. I also read the Observer a few days since. I saw in the Tribune that mothers can receive a pension for a son that is killed provided that they are dependent or partly dependent on him for support. Savagham has never been heard from.
>
> When do you think the war will end? I hope that we will give the rebels a good thrashing before long. The news is good from Vicksburg if it is true.
>
> My health is very good at present. Alex Douglas complains of a headache for a day or two. Will you send me two dollars immediately if you have not done so, as I borrowed $2.00 about three weeks ago to be paid in about a week which I was not able to do. I hope Frances will write soon. How is her health and yours good? We now ride to the convalescent camp in the cars and bring the stragglers, as we call them, back, in the cars. We go up in the freight cars, no seating and come back with them naked inside and out, — that is, on top of the cars. The stragglers call us $800.00 and a cow, because the soldiers guarding that camp are now nine months men and receive $500.00 bounty. How did Silas' speech go off?
>
> [page break — Mch 20 continuation]
>
> Mch. 20 — I received a letter from Alex this morning enclosing one from you with $3.00. I send you the envelope to show you how it was directed. I suppose the Postmaster at Champlain put on the figures 113th and 60th at the top. Alex was well. You speak of my having wet feet. I have not had wet feet more than once or twice since I enlisted. I wear shoes because they are easier for the feet, I think. Think I shall sell my boots. Twelve of our men have left today for Fortress Monroe as a guard on the boat; took 4 days rations with them.
>
> I don't think the land Mr. Rye claims has any maple trees on it. La Vally I don't think would be a good man. I saw in the Observer that the names of 400 Union soldiers who had died in rebel hospitals was about to be published. I think it would be well to see the list.

## Editorial Notes

The Henry passage is buried between fire engines and pension law, but it's the most important sentence in the letter: "I am afraid he was killed." Charles has been circling this conclusion since December. Now he pairs it with practical information — the Tribune says mothers can get pensions, and a list of 400 Union dead in rebel hospitals is coming. He's telling Mother to check the list and file for the pension. The bureaucratization of grief.
