## Full Transcription

> Nov. 13, 1861
> Camp Jackson
>
> My dear Mother:
>
> Here I am safe and sound three miles from Baltimore. Our regiment is distributed along the railroad between said place and the Relay House, a distance of nine miles.
>
> Now that you know that I am well and where I am I presume you would like to hear the particulars of my journey. We left Camp Wheeler November the first and arrived at Rouses Point at six. I was very much surprised and very much pleased to F and C there. I was feeling very lonely and seeing them cheered me up wonderfully. I only regret I could not have seen you.
>
> The steamer there could only carry half of the regt. and we (our company) being in the left wing, were oblige to wait an hour for another boat, the United States. Supper was given us on board the boat. I gave that case to Capt. Ransom then. He wishes me to give you his thanks and said that he did not see how you managed to contrive so convenient a thing.
>
> We took the cars at Whitehall for Albany. There we left them and marched down to the river escorted by a company (I think) of artillery. A steamer and two barges carried the regiment to New York. We arrived there Sunday morning. We passed West Point. The scenery on the river is the most beautiful I ever saw. We passed West Point at 3 A.M. It was daylight when we passed through the most beautiful part of the river. I was on deck nearly all night. We lay anchored off New York until Monday evening when we crossed over to Jersey City and took the cars for the South. While at New York we received the rest of our knapsacks and blankets, and each man a beautiful Enfield rifle and sword bayonet.
>
> On Monday afternoon we left the boat and marched into the city. We were drawn into a hollow square on a beautiful square in the center of which stood an equestrian statue of Washington. A star spangled banner was presented to the regiment and a beautiful bay horse to the Colonel. We were again drawn up in line in another street and a banner was presented to us there. We then left the city. It was about 2 oclock A.M. when we reached Philadelphia. An excellent breakfast was given us there. It consisted of beef, sweet potatoes, coffee, cheese, sourcrout and bread and butter. After breakfast we were taken to another room where there was every convenience for washing. I shall always think with pleasure of the Quaker City. At New York they gave us something to look at, but at Philadelphia they gave us something better pleasing to hungry soldiers — something to eat. They both treated us very kindly, but of the two I like the treatment of the latter place the best.
>
> We reached Baltimore between three and four oclock in the afternoon. Before marching through the city five rounds of cartridges were distributed to the men, but no caps. (What the reason was I don't know).
>
> Nov. 14. It was dark when we left Baltimore. We did not reach Washington until between two and three in the morning. We remained there till afternoon when we left for Kalarama Heights, three miles distant. It was dark when we reached there and we were obliged to pitch our tents after we arrived. We all suffered from hunger while there (on account of the Quartermaster's inexperience. The boys have given the place the name of Starvation Heights. Orders were received on Friday (November eighth) for us to leave.
>
> I don't know what to think of our Colonel. He sent our baggage on before and we were obliged to sleep on the ground that night. There was no necessity for it, for they were expecting us in Washington that night, and had supper prepared for us. We left Washington Saturday morning. Five companies were left at the Relay House and the rest distributed between that place and Baltimore. We are stationed three miles from Baltimore as I have told you. Company C is encamped
>
> (Contd.)
>
> with us. We have hired a black cook for our company. Each pays the cook two shillings per month. The business of the regiment is to guard the railroad. I was on guard duty yesterday. I was obliged to walk half a mile every two hours during the night to relieve the pickets. They do not extend so far during the day. The first night we came to this camp was a very hard one to me. The pickets from our Company were entirely under my charge. I was obliged to call out the reliefs and take them around. What made it so hard was we had no guard tent and I was obliged to go around among the tents and hunt out the relief every two hours. Another thing that made it hard was that it was my first experience of picket duty. I think that I gave satisfaction.
>
> Now, my dear Mother, the reason that I haven't written is because I lost my knapsack the first night we came here and I never found it until yesterday (November 13). I wish I could see you all very much. Write me often and send me a paper now and then. I try to keep my promise to you "to do nothing of which you would not approve." I feel that God is very near me. I sometimes feel very lonely, but I think of Him and you fighting for me and I feel better. I think of all of you often, very, very, often.
>
> Your affectionate son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
>
> P.S. Direct your letters to Washington, D.C., Sixtieth Regt. I do not think you could go two miles in any direction without coming upon some picket or camp. Give my respects to all friends.

## Editorial Notes

This is the richest letter in the collection — a full travel narrative spanning 9 days and 500+ miles from Ogdensburg to Camp Jackson. Alexander's eye for detail makes this a vivid primary source: the tipsy steamer at Rouses Point, the Hudson River at dawn past West Point ("the most beautiful I ever saw"), the flag ceremony in New York, the Philadelphia breakfast down to the sauerkraut, the ominous cartridge distribution entering Baltimore, and the miserable "Starvation Heights" at Kalorama.

The P.S. is the single most important line for our project data: "Direct your letters to Washington, D.C., Sixtieth Regt." — confirming Alexander served in the 60th New York Infantry (the "St. Lawrence Regiment"), organized at Ogdensburg. This was not clear from previous letters.

Alexander's closing is the most emotionally raw passage in any letter so far: "I try to keep my promise to you 'to do nothing of which you would not approve.' I feel that God is very near me. I sometimes feel very lonely, but I think of Him and you fighting for me and I feel better. I think of all of you often, very, very, often." The double "very, very" and the theological framing of Mother as co-combatant with God reveal a young man finding his moral bearings in an alien world.
