## Full Transcription

> [At the top of the letter was written: "My dearest child; Write me immediately."]
>
> Aug. 8, 1864
> Near Atlanta, Ga.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your letter of July 25th day before yesterday and your papers, the "Times" and the "Tribune" the day before, also the envelopes and paper enclosed. As I had written you the day I thought I had fulfilled your request of writing you immediately, but like a dutiful son I shall write you again today. Now, "ain't" I a good boy?
>
> You recollect when I last wrote that I mentioned that the armies of McPherson and Schofield were moving to the right. Since then a battle has come off in which our forces were victorious, driving the enemy a mile and a half — taking a fort and a battery, and cutting, I understand, the only remaining road of the rebels. But then I will not weary you with a detailed account of this affair, as you will have seen accounts of it in the papers long before this reaches you.
>
> I regret to inform you that Gen'l. Hooker has left us. He had endeared himself to the whole corps. The day he left he rode along the line bidding the men goodbye. A man from one of the Pennsylvania regts. of our brigade had just had his leg taken off by a cannon shot, was being carried off on a stretcher just at that time. "Good bye, General Hooker!" he sang out. It seemed to have moved the Gen'l. greatly. He got off his horse, took off his hat and went up and shook hands with the poor fellow, making some kind encouraging words at the same time. The 20th Corps will never forget old "Fighting Joe." Major Gen'l. Slocum, our old Corps commander, is now I understand in command of the Corps. We feel somewhat comforted for the loss of Hooker.
>
> I suppose Charles and James are now on the Peninsula with Grant, as the excitement occasioned by the last raid must be nearly over. I hope the report of Charles being ill is not true, but if it is he is where he can get the best medical treatment. I shall try and write them both today. I hope James will try and bear his disappointment like a man. I am afraid it is he instead of Charles that is ill. I suppose Frances is still enjoying her visit at C. Have been expecting a letter from her for some time. She will write soon. I have not a solitary stamp and hardly know what I shall do to end this letter. If you can do so without inconveniencing yourself, please send me some in your next letter. I do not think I have received all the stamps you have sent. I have not received the tea as yet. Culver Bostwick received some some time since. Please write often. Love to all. Give my regards to Mrs. Brikerhoff.
>
> Your aff. Son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Hooker's farewell is the most cinematic scene Alexander has written — the general riding along the line, the legless Pennsylvania soldier calling out from his stretcher "Good bye, General Hooker!", Hooker dismounting, removing his hat, shaking the man's hand. "It seemed to have moved the Gen'l. greatly." The 20th Corps loved Hooker, and Hooker loved them back. His departure was a command dispute — passed over for the Army of the Tennessee after McPherson's death on July 22 — but to the men it was a loss beyond measure. "The 20th Corps will never forget old 'Fighting Joe.'"

The battle on the right — driving the enemy a mile and a half, taking a fort and battery, cutting "the only remaining road" — is the Ezra Church/Utoy Creek fighting of late July. Sherman's flanking strategy is working. The roads feeding Atlanta are being severed one by one.

"My dearest child; Write me immediately" — written at the top of the letter, likely by Alexander's mother on receipt, or possibly Alexander's own urgent appeal. "Now, 'ain't' I a good boy?" — the playful tone of a man who writes faithfully even when he has no stamps.
