## Full Transcription

> Feb. 12th, 1862
> Camp McClellan
>
> Dear Frances:
>
> I was very glad to receive a letter from you. I had about or almost given you up, but I take it for granted that you are perfectly excusable this time. I am very glad for your sake that you have a School. I hope you will enjoy yourself. I saw Lieut. Miner not long ago. He spoke of you having a school in M.C. He thought it was all very fine, etc., etc. He also spoke of James having an appointment at West Point. The Capt. thought it was the best thing a young man could do.
>
> I suppose you would like to hear all the news, but I have not much of that to write. What I do know you shall have forthwith or immediately. The pickets have been firing across the river at each other, but it is since our Co. came off last. I am not shure whether the rebels commenced first or not. If they keep up that game they will get the worst of it. The pickets go on four days at a time now. I think that will be when the roads are in proper order, but now it is very muddy. The weather has been very mild thus far this winter. For my part I would rather have cold weather, but not for soldiering. I should not like to live in this part of the Country. The work performed on the farms down here is all worked by slaves.
>
> Feb. 13th: News came into our Camp last night of the glorious victory in South Carolina. There was great cheering in our Camp when the news came in. The news came from the Burnside Expedition. It seems that our troops captured two thousand prisoners. Many such victorys, besides the one in Tennessee, and the rebels will in time have to give up. Only let the 34th go into action once and the rebels will have to run. That is what I believe.
>
> I have not much more to write this time. I hope when I write again to have more news. We have a very fine band to our Regt. They are beginning to play pretty well now. I received a letter from Nellie and Aunt Laura a short time ago. I have not answered them yet. If it would not be to much trouble I should be very glad to receive a paper. Excuse this scrawl. Hope to do better next time.
>
> I remain your aff. Brother,
>
> Henry

## Significance Flags

### Historical Significance: `major`

> Writing to his sister Frances from Camp McClellan, Henry passes the night's electric news: word of "the glorious victory in South Carolina" by the Burnside Expedition, two thousand prisoners taken, met with "great cheering" through the camp. Eager and untested, he boasts that once the 34th New York goes into action "the rebels will have to run" — then notes, plainly, that the farms in this country are "all worked by slaves."

> Warm brother-to-sister tone. Pleased to hear from Frances. Excited about Burnside victory. Confident in 34th NY's fighting ability. Regrets for not hearing earlier from some correspondents (Nellie, Aunt Laura). Minor request for newspaper.

## Editorial Notes

**FRANCES HUBBELL'S EMPLOYMENT IN MONTREAL**

This letter confirms Frances is teaching school in Montreal, Canada. The abbreviation "M.C." clearly means Montreal, Canada (not a US location). Lieut. Miner's knowledge of her employment suggests she had been there for some time, or the family had publicized it. Her work in Canada during wartime suggests either:
1. She had taken a position abroad before the war and remained there
2. She had gone to Canada during the war for personal/family reasons
3. She was part of a Canadian educational institution (boarding school, academy, etc.)

Henry's tone suggests he's proud of her independence and her work. The fact that a Union officer (Lieut. Miner) knew about it and approved suggests it was respectable and perhaps known in regimental circles through family correspondence.

**BURNSIDE EXPEDITION VICTORY & MORALE IMPACT**

The arrival of news on Feb. 13 of the Burnside Expedition victory in South Carolina (Battle of Roanoke Island, Feb. 7–8) triggered "great cheering" in camp. This is a significant morale marker: soldiers celebrated Union success, and it evidently boosted confidence in the cause. Henry's statement — "Only let the 34th go into action once and the rebels will have to run" — is characteristic soldier bravado but also reflects the confidence Union soldiers had at this point in the war. (Seven months later, the 34th NY would see action at Antietam, where Henry would be killed.)

**PICKET DUTY & RIVER SKIRMISHING**

Henry mentions pickets firing across the river at each other. This was likely the Potomac (near Harpers Ferry/Camp McClellan), the boundary between Union and Confederate forces. Such skirmishing was common but not yet large-scale combat. The pickets were rotating on 4-day cycles, and roads were too muddy for major operations — consistent with winter quarters.

**LIEUTENANT MINER: FAMILY CONNECTIONS IN OFFICER RANKS**

Lieut. Miner appears to be an officer who knows the Hubbell family or at least knows of them through regimental connections. His knowledge of Frances's school in Montreal and his endorsement of James's West Point appointment suggest he was a senior or well-connected officer. Miner's name may appear in other letters or regimental records.

**SLAVERY OBSERVATION**

Henry notes in passing that farms in this region (likely Virginia or Maryland) "is all worked by slaves" — a matter-of-fact observation rather than moral commentary. This suggests Henry was witnessing slavery firsthand in the occupied border states, which may have influenced his thinking about the war's purpose (initially a Union preservation war, later an emancipation war).

**COMMUNICATION PATTERN**

Henry is a prolific letter writer in mid-February 1862. On Feb. 11–13 alone, he writes to Charles, Frances, and his mother — maintaining correspondence with family across the lines and abroad (Frances in Montreal). This shows the importance families placed on communication and Henry's commitment to keeping ties active despite military service.

**"VERY FINE BAND"**

Henry comments that the 34th NY has "a very fine band" that is "beginning to play pretty well now." Regiment bands were common and important to morale. This suggests the band was still improving but showed promise. Music was valued in camp life for morale and entertainment.

## Event: Burnside Expedition Victory (News Arrival, Feb. 13, 1862)

On the evening of Feb. 13, 1862, news arrived at Camp McClellan that Union forces under Gen. Ambrose Burnside had won a "glorious victory" in South Carolina (Battle of Roanoke Island, Feb. 7–8). The report stated that approximately 2,000 Confederate prisoners were captured. The news was greeted with "great cheering" in camp, indicating a significant morale boost. Henry interpreted this victory, combined with an earlier victory in Tennessee (Fort Henry or Fort Donelson), as evidence that the Union would eventually prevail. He expressed confidence that if the 34th NY got into action, "the rebels will have to run."

This is a critical morale snapshot: soldiers celebrated distant Union victories, maintained confidence in their own regiment's capabilities, and interpreted the war as progressively moving toward Union success. In reality, the eastern theater would become far more difficult, and Henry would be killed in action within seven months.
