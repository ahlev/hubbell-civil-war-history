## Full Transcription

> May 31, 1863
> Alexandria, Va.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your letter two or three days since containing eleven dollars. Since I last wrote you, I have been to Aquia Creek to see Alex. Found him well and much surprised to see me. The watch that I surprised him he was delighted with. It was a double cased silver lever watch and I paid $15. for it. Bought it of a Serg. in our Co. who said $20. for it, but as he was hard up, sold it for $15. cash down. I only had $11 and he waited for the other $4 till I rec'd, the money you sent. So I owe him and his watch one week before I received the money from you. Staid over night with him. They are fixed up quite comfortable. Should not be surprised if they staid there some time as I don't think Hooker will make an advance very soon. Alex is in the same old of his camps, Fort or Net large as ever. He was color bearer in the fight. He is detached from the reg't. and is Serg. in the ambulance Corps. Alex told me he was not in the fight, but was detailed to guard a baggage train. Said he felt bad that he could not be in the fight with his reg't. I told him he was lucky. The men said they laid flat on their bellies, the shells whistling over them and they hugged the ground close. They were lying behind a breastwork that was thrown up with him classes and bayonets. I had some trouble getting on board the boat to come back. There were three lines of guards on the wharf. I fell in behind our guard that was going on the boat and passed two lines of guards, but the third stopped me, thinking I was not a guard as I had no gun. Our Serg. told him I belonged to his, but they told me I could not pass. After waiting nearly two hours on the wharf the guards let all go through in a rush without showing their papers and the Captain redirected the passes at the gangway of the boat. I had no pass, but our Serg. told the captain how it was and he let me on. Then I was stopped on the wharf, I was afraid I would have to wait till the next day, but I did not. Alex told me that you had applied to his Lieut. Col. for his discharge. But neither of us thought it would do any good. I told him if his discharge was offered him he had better accept it, but he did not say that he would.
>
> Albert Cook was here while I was gone to Aquia C. Am sorry that I did not see him. Burdick is back and is promoted to Captain. Another Capt. is now Lt. Col. and Capt. Strain is Major and the Major is Col.
>
> We expect a cavalry raid in this city. Today is Sunday and all the niggers in the street were impressed this forenoon and set to work throwing up a breastwork across each street leading to the wharves. A vast amount of government stores are in buildings along the wharves and the rebels would like to destroy them. Our reg't. has been for some time at work digging a trench around the city. This trench answers
>
> [page break]
>
> it a good breastwork. We have cannon placed at different points on the inside of the ditch. A few days ago a nine months reg't. came here. Their time is out next month. They have been at work digging this ditch also and a lot of niggers were impressed to work also. These negroes are contrabands. I said that at each street breastworks are being thrown up, but they are not breastworks, but stockades which are logs of wood placed end on end close to each other. A train of cars is now here loaded with green hardwood logs. The men are going to work all night at the stockades. This is Sunday night work, but then whiskey is plenty for the laborers and the rebels may make a raid any time. There will be some very heavy gates on these stockades. They are to be 8 or 10 feet high. A part of it is done now and every little way a log is left shorter than the others for the soldiers to fire through. The streets are stockaded about two blocks from the river.
>
> An incomplete letter from Charley

## Editorial Notes

The stockade description is the most detailed engineering account in the collection — vertical hardwood logs 8-10 feet high, close-set with firing gaps, heavy gates, cannon on the interior of a surrounding ditch. Alexandria is being transformed into a fortress against cavalry raids. Meanwhile, Alexander was the color bearer at Chancellorsville — the man who carries the flag into battle, the primary target for enemy fire. Mother asked him to seek safety; he carried the colors.
