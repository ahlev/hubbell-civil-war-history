## Full Transcription

> April 22, 1864
> Stevenson, Ala.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> We are at length fully organized and now form the 20th Army Corps, Army of the Cumberland. Direct your letters to 3d Brigade, 2nd Div., 20th Army Corps, Army of the Cumberland.
>
> The third Brigade is composed of seven regt's., the 60th, the 78th, the 102d N.Y. Vets. and the 29th and 111th Penn. Vets. and the 137th and 149th N.Y. Volunteers.
>
> The Corps is divided into four Divisions. We have the same Div. commander as formerly — Gen. John M. Geary. Now, Mother, please to remember what I have told you. It may save you a great deal of needless anxiety and another thing, I want you to be able to give an intelligent answer to any one who may inquire about the Corps. Gen. Hooker commands the Corps. There is a rumor current here that McClellan is about to take command of the Army of the Potomac. I hope it is true. I have tried hard to forget McClellan and think his removal a good move on the part of the Administration, but I cannot do it. My heart still yearns toward the man. I have never had, and am afraid never can have, the confidence in any military officer that I had in him. I do hope he will again take command of the Potomac Army.
>
> I rec'd your letter of the 7th inst. a few days since and am wonderfully pleased about the boots. I went down to the express office this morning to see if they had come. They had not but will probably be here in a few days. Tattoo is now beating so I shall have to stop for tonight.
>
> Apr. 23rd — I rec'd a N.Y. Tribune this morning mailed Apr. 18th. I have rec'd both letters of Charles that you sent, also Aunt Laura's. There is very little of interest to write. I will write again soon. Love to all.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell
>
> P.S. I have rec'd eight postage stamps. Why doesn't Charley write me? I have not rec'd a letter from him since I have been in this department — some seven months. I have written him twice since I have been in this place.

## Editorial Notes

Alexander's confession of devotion to McClellan is the most politically revealing passage in the collection. "I have tried hard to forget McClellan... but I cannot do it." The soldier who despises draft dodgers, demands soldiers' voting rights, and scorns Copperheads still loves the general Lincoln fired. McClellan's appeal to the rank-and-file was personal — they felt he cared about their lives. Alexander's loyalty is emotional, not political: "My heart still yearns toward the man."

The order of battle he recites — seven regiments, three states, veterans and volunteers — is the unit that will march into Georgia in two weeks. Alexander instructs Mother to memorize the address so she can "give an intelligent answer to any one who may inquire about the Corps." He's preparing her for the campaign's public attention.
