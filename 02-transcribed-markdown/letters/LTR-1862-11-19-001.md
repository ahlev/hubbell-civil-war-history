## Full Transcription

> Nov. 19th, 1862
> Harpers Ferry
>
> My Dear Mother & Sister:
>
> How I long to see you. Wonder what you are doing today. Miss Fannie I expect is in that cozy room in the old academy (of pleasant memory to me) teaching (teaching the young ideas of how to Charles vous Francis (don't like my spelling do you Miss Fannie?) — and you, Mother, well — I cant imagine what you are about — what do you do with yourself these long days all alone as you are? I should enjoy your pleasant smiles. I am weary — weary of this an ugly day and stir. A little rest would do me oh so much good. Nevertheless, I am content to stay. Our Government is worth any sacrifice.
>
> The war cannot last much longer. Both sides are getting heartily sick of the affair. A vigorous winter campaign on our part will, I think, speedily put an end to the war — and then for home, sweet, sweet, home. And oh — how good it will seem to see you all once more, and shall we all — all — meet again. God grant it. Dear, dear, Henry how I long to see him. I have seen him but twice since I left home. You wished me in one of your letters to tell you all about our first meeting.
>
> About three miles beyond Georgetown, D.C., we passed a large encampment. I hailed a soldier and inquired what troops were encamped there and ascertained that it was Sedgwick's Division. He pointed out the position of the 34th to me. I could not then leave the ranks, but as soon we halted (about two miles beyond) I went to the Colonel and told him I had a brother in one of the regiments we had passed that I had not seen for over a year, and asked permission to go back and see him. The Colonel said he had not the power to grant my request, but said "If you are willing to run the risk, you can go." I said nothing about it. I was willing to run it. It was not dark, but there was a bright moon which gave me sufficient light to see by. I passed all the guards without difficulty. After a considerable search I found the regiment and company. "Is this B company?" I inquired. "Yes." "Is there a man among you by the name of Hubbell?" "Yes." "Hubbell, there is somebody out here that wants to see you." Oh, how my heart bumped. I was in a moment to see my long absent brother. A large bearded man stepped out from one of the tents. It was Henry. The way our two hands flew
>
> [page break]
>
> together was a caution. I laughed — I — well — I was so glad I did not know what to do. You can well imagine Henry's surprise at seeing me. "Why Dick, is that you — really you. I can hardly believe it. How glad I am to see you. Come in my tent and sit down." I soon had quite a crowd of the boys around me and we had a right merry time, I can assure you. It was quite a late hour when I left for Camp.
>
> I saw him again a few days after a little beyond Rockville, by day light. He was a noble looking soldier. He was, I think, undoubtedly taken prisoner at Antietam, and if he yet survives his wound (which from all that I can learn I do not think was dangerous) you will hear from him before long. What do you think, Mother, Henry and I were in the fight at the same time, and the 34th was but a few hundred yards to the left of us. Then I feel like it, Mother, I will try and tell you more of the battle. I have not told you a third.
>
> Please write often, and tell me all bout home affairs. Has the draft taken place yet. Love to all.
>
> Your affectionate son,
>
> Alexander F. Hubbell
>
> P.S. I am enjoying excellent health. Capt. Ransom is very — very kind.

## Editorial Notes

This is the most extraordinary letter in the collection — a set piece of wartime narrative that reads like a scene from a novel. The moonlight walk, the challenge at the tent, the bearded man, the cry of recognition: Alexander has constructed a story, polished it in memory, and delivered it to Mother as a gift. She asked for this story two months ago, and he has waited until his eyes could sustain the writing.

But the story is retrospective, and we know what Alexander doesn't yet: Henry will not be seen again. The "large bearded man" who stepped out of the tent and said "Why Dick, is that you" is already gone. Alexander's belief that Henry was "undoubtedly taken prisoner" and that his wound was "not dangerous" is based on hope, not evidence — and it will be contradicted by Misner's report, which is already in transit.

The military detail is also valuable: the two regiments were "but a few hundred yards" apart at Antietam. Alexander and Henry fought the same battle, at the same time, within earshot of each other. One survived. The other vanished.
