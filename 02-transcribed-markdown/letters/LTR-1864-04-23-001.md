## Full Transcription

> Apr. 23, 1864
> Dept. of the Gulf
> N. Orleans, La.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I was relieved of duty here by Special Order April 21st and ordered to rejoin my Regt at once, but am still here waiting for the boat. I expect to carry the mail to Gen. Banks Hd. Qtrs. in the field, on my way back and if I do will have a state room and subsistence on the boat. "Will have a long ride up the Red River which is better than footing it the way the army did." The army is amassed on the Red River so I will not have far to march to get to the regt. What stuff the Regt. could not carry is left in the city in storehouses. I put my overcoat and dress coat in a box and have put them with the regt's. baggage in this city. Shall carry a knapsack, rubber and woolen blankets, and change of underclothes, also half a shelter tent.
>
> Rec'd. a letter from Alex dated Apr. 10th, Stevenson, Ala. He was well and glad they were doing picket and guard duty there, but were under marching orders. Said that he had rec'd a letter from you saying that I was at these Hd. Qtrs. The army on the Red River expects to have another fight very soon. It is receiving reinforcements.
>
> There is one consolation about my being relieved, which is that I am not the only one that has been relieved. Two men were relieved before I came here. They were both from one regt. but neither of them suited. The man clerk that has just come out cannot write very well. He is not set to work yet but was told to practice writing till he could write better. Should not wonder if he was relieved. I have received $11.60 extra pay for March, but will not receive any for this month. The A.A. General here is very particular about penmanship. You see I have seasoned a long march as far and a fight, so that so much good has been done me by being here. I am in good spirits about going back as I have been expecting to be relieved. Have done nothing lately, but when I first came did a little work.
>
> I was rejoiced to receive your letter of April 8th; was beginning to think something was the matter. Was sorry to learn that you did not know where James was. He does very wrong not to let you know where he is.
>
> Apr. 24th — I leave here today or tomorrow for the regt. Have rec'd. but two letters since I came here, one from you, the other from Alex. Expect to find several when I arrive at the regt. Will write you soon after I get there if I have an opportunity. Where did you get the copies and why did you send that signature? Also say Frances is preceptress of the Academy. She is rising in the world, but I am afraid it is too hard work for her. There is no necessity for her teaching all the time.
>
> There are a great many French here, also many negroes that speak French. In the Constitutional Convention now in session here there is a great deal of debate about publishing the laws in one or two languages. But I think they will decide for the English language only. Some things here are strange to us, for instance, cisterns are made out of wood and are all above ground and stand nearly as high as some houses, so you see they are very prominent. Sidewalks are called banquettes. Street cars are drawn by mules, and there is no conductor, trusting to the honesty of the passengers to put their fare in the box. Some cars have two stories, that is, people ride on top of the car. Cemeteries are different here; most of the people are buried above ground, either in tombs, or in a wall that has holes in it that look like ovens. There are some very fine residences in the suburbs of the city. Trees are trimmed off in different shapes and look very well. A park in this city has a great many beautiful flowers in bloom and it is a pleasant place to take a stroll. The Island of Tortugas on the coast of Florida is a great prison for soldiers. A large fort is being built there and the soldiers condemned to hard labor work on the fort. A good many soldiers in this city are sentenced to the Dry Tortugas, as it is called.
>
> The chief clerk at these Hd. Qtrs. received $4 a day, another clerk that interprets French and German receives $100 a month, the rest receive the same pay — 40¢ a day extra pay. I hope that you will write often and that when James writes you will let me know. I should not be surprised if a long time elapsed before you heard from him as he may be ambitious of making a mark before writing. How do Sid's wife and mother get along? Give my regards to Silas and Sid, also to David Savage. I hope that you will not worry about James or the rest of us, for it does no good and I think we will all get along well enough. Hoping that you are all well, I will close.
>
> From your aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

Charles's New Orleans travelogue is his most ethnographic writing — cisterns "nearly as high as some houses," sidewalks called banquettes, streetcars on the honor system, cemeteries with wall niches "that look like ovens." He sees a city utterly unlike anything in Clinton County, New York, and describes it with the eye of a careful tourist.

The Constitutional Convention debate — English only vs. bilingual — is Reconstruction's mundane reality. Louisiana's French-speaking population clashing with English-speaking Union authority, all while the army fights up the Red River.

Charles's philosophical acceptance of being relieved — "so much good has been done me by being here" — is characteristic. He escaped the regiment's hard marching, earned extra pay, saw New Orleans. Now he carries mail up the Red River in a state room while the regiment walks. The irony that his penmanship failed him — after months of lecturing Alexander about his handwriting — is lost on no one except perhaps Charles himself.
