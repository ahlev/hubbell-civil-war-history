## Full Transcription

> May 31st, 1864
> Entrenchments near Dallas, Ga.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I am happy to inform you that I am all right thus far. God has been very good to me. This is written while we are under fire, the bullets whistling over our heads (there goes one now — b-e-a-u-t-i-f-u-l) every now and then — not a little oftener. "We have lain rather comfortably healthy." James Thurber and John Rawlins of Co. H have been wounded. Thurber is wounded through the arm and slightly in the shoulder. His wound is not dangerous. Rawlins is wounded through the right side. His wound is severe, but I hope not dangerous. The total loss of this regt. in this big fight is twenty-eight. Seven killed — the remainder wounded. The rebel works are but a very short distance from ours. We can hear them talk quite frequently and distinctly. The Gen'l. will not allow us to go out on the skirmishing line. Says I am always ready to do my duty — but that is no place for me, — the men of the keenest sight are the only ones that are needed there.
>
> Please write often and send me some postage stamps, and will you be kind enough to send me by mail a silk handkerchief. I lost mine the other day and it is almost impossible to get along without one here. I have not rec'd a letter since I left Bridgeport but a number of papers. The letters will be along by and by. This writing to the noise of bullets not very pleasant business. Love to all.
>
> Your aff. son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

"There goes one now — b-e-a-u-t-i-f-u-l." Alexander writes while bullets whistle overhead and spells out his appreciation of one as it passes. This is the Civil War letter in its purest form — the impossible intimacy of writing home while under fire, the soldier's humor that transforms mortal danger into a parenthetical aside. The letter is signed "C. F. Hubbell" — a typist error that has Alexander signing with Charles's name — but the voice is unmistakably Alexander's.

The General's refusal to let Alexander on the skirmish line — "the men of the keenest sight" — is a quiet revelation about Alexander's physical condition. The sergeant who survived Gettysburg, Wauhatchie, and Lookout Mountain is no longer fit for the sharpest end of the fighting. But he's still there, writing under fire, spelling out the beauty of bullets.
