## Full Transcription

> Camp in field
> April 6, 1862
>
> Dear James:
>
> I was very glad to receive a few lines from you. Today is very chilly but not very cold. I have seen a great deal more of the country than I thought I would, and I am very likely to see more, but if I ever go around the country again I would rather take a different way than to foot it, but I have stood it pretty well thus far.
>
> The day before yesterday we expected to have a fight. We went on a double quick for about a mile. A good many fell out, but most of our Co. kept together. If we ever go into a fight away goes my knap-sacks, but they are the best friend we have except our guns at night, for it is quite cold here at night.
>
> A rebel prisoner was taken by some of our troops the other day. He was very well dressed — had a gray overcoat, light cap, black pants. He looked very sullen. There are a great many troops around here. If the rebels keep on as they have they will soon get into a place where they will have to fight or surrender. I do not think they have much courage. They are doing themselves more harm than good by holding out as they have.
>
> I forgot to mention that I have been keeping a pocket diary, write down everything that transpires from day to day. I have 28 dates. Take good care of the cows and horse. I shall be home next summer I hope.
>
> If you get into West Point you will probably have an office in the army. I hope you will succeed. I have nothing more to write this time. There was some heavy firing of musketry last night at York Town, or off in that direction. Cooper has not left yet so I shall mail both letters. Write often. I remain your aff. brother,
>
> Henry
>
> P.S. Afternoon. It is raining now. Our tents are made of rubber blankets. I would not object to having a few sleigh rides.

## Editorial Notes

The pocket diary is now confirmed with specific detail: 28 entries, recording "everything that transpires." Starting from ~March 5-8, this covers the march from Harpers Ferry to Washington, the steamboat to Hampton, and the advance toward Yorktown. If this diary survived in the family trunk, it would transform the collection. The double quick for an expected fight — with men falling out but Co. D staying together — shows the regiment approaching real combat for the first time. The musketry at Yorktown marks the beginning of the Siege of Yorktown (April 5 – May 4, 1862). Henry's advice to James about West Point ("you will probably have an office in the army") is practical: a West Point graduate would be commissioned, not enlisted.
