## Full Transcription

> Jan. 24, 1862
> Baltimore
>
> My dear Mother:
>
> Wouldn't I like to be at home in your nice cozy sitting room this cold blustering evening. How pleasant it would be, wouldn't it? But here I am and here I must be. Nevertheless I am well content. Didn't you know that I am taking care of myself, eh? Luther insists on my going to bed. He says "it is bad for the health to sit up so late." Rather think so too. Good night.
>
> Jan. 25th: I experienced this morning one of the keenest disappointments that it has ever been my lot to experience. Messrs. Hoyle and Finley left this morning without my seeing them. (Next few lines cut out.) .....
>
> This morning our little drummer said they wished me to come up (this was near eight o'clock). I immediately went up and met the Capt. a short distance from the "quarters." He said they left in the "seven train". I felt all things. "All things" — that don't express half what I felt. I hope my dear Mother, you will tell Messrs H and F the reason why I did not see them. I am afraid they will not think I am very prompt. Luther saw them both up in the city yesterday. Well, well, "disappointment is the common lot of man." I might as well make the best of it.
>
> I said in my last letter "I would tell you something about the 'Union Relief Association'" in my next. This Association is sustained partly by private contribution and partly by Government. Its object is to feed soldiers passing through Baltimore. I boarded there while I was at "Camden Station." They have room to seat over eight hundred men at one time and sleeping room enough for a regiment. God bless the Association. It has saved the soldiers from a great deal of suffering. I wish we had had such a place to go to when we passed through Baltimore.
>
> Your plast two letters filled me with pleasure and anxiety. So you think James will get an appointment. Oh! I hope — I trust. I know he will pass examination. How kind Mr. Wheeler is. I should like to give him three cheers. Hurrah! Hurrah! Hur-- I shall have to wait until I get out doors.
>
> I have not taken a glass of malt or spiritour liquor since I came in the army. I try to keep my promise to you. The Capt. sends home for me twenty dollars. Take it and fix James off with it. If he does not need it, use it yourself. You are welcome to it.
>
> I think our Regt. will leave Baltimore soon. We are doing hard service, very hard service. Please to send me stamps (postage) from home. It is no use to get them here. They all go in no time. Don't forget to send them. Love to all.
>
> Your affectionate son,
>
> Mrs. F. A. Hubbell                Alexander.
>
> P.S. I have bought myself a dictionary. I am very much obliged to you for your correction. I wish you would write and tell me my mistakes. Keep me posted concerning all the doings of the "Adelphic Debating Club". Tell the boys to persevere. Those meetings in the old Academy I love to think of. I am glad that "Slavery Question" has for once been rightly decided. How I should like to stand up in that old Chapel and hold forth on that subject again. How much did your pig weigh? How does the little bosey get along? How many of the hens died this winter. Now, dear Mother, don't give up housekeeping. Hire a boy. I will pay him. I expect to be home next fall if I am alive. Write often. Oh! how good it does seem to get a letter from home. I charge you again write often, write often, often.

## Editorial Notes

The Union Relief Association description is the payoff Alexander has promised since December — a social welfare institution that seated 800 soldiers at a time, fed and housed men passing through Baltimore. His gratitude ("God bless the Association") is personal: he boarded there himself. The James excitement — "Hurrah! Hurrah! Hur-- I shall have to wait until I get out doors" — is the most joyful moment in Alexander's correspondence. The "Slavery Question" at the Adelphic Debating Club reveals the family's political orientation: Alexander is glad it was "rightly decided" and wants to "stand up in that old Chapel and hold forth on that subject again." These are abolitionists. The P.S. cascade — dictionary, debating club, pig, bossy, hens, hire a boy — shows Alexander's mind racing between intellectual life, politics, and farm management, all in one paragraph.
