## Full Transcription

> Feb. 11, 1862
> Baltimore
>
> My Dear Brother: (James)
>
> I have just finished dinner. Have been busy all the forenoon washing dishes, sweeping, etc. and getting dinner. I think I shall be quite a cook when I return home, entirely independent of the feminine gender. It is my turn today to see to these things.
>
> So, my man, you have had an appointment to go to West Point, have you? Well, that's good news certainly. You must study hard, very hard, James. How proud I shall feel of you if you get into West Point. Why, man, I shan't see my boots for a week. James, you must put 'your best foot forward.' Now is the time for you to exert yourself if there ever was a time. If you get into W.P. you will save yourself a great many years of toil in getting your education. It is well worth a grand effort. Do you remember, James, what little Doolittle once said to me concerning you. Oh! how mad I was. I could use my bayonet upon him now with every good ground. Exert yourself, James, if it be only for my sake. I should like to 'square up accounts' with little Doolittle one of these days, but then I must 'forgive and forget'. You must have a sweet time at the Academy this winter, I think. I hope your debating society prospers. Be punctual in your attendance. Speaking there once does a man more good than six months of 'Friday afternoon speaking' to my mind. Why don't the trustees 'oust' Dobbin? The scholars can have no respect for the man. What a misfortune it must be to have a 'scolding wife', eh? Look out Master James for yourself.
>
> We have received new pants (blue). We shall probably have our 'dress suit' soon. The pants we have received are the same as those we received at Camp Wheeler. The jackets we received there are still good but the pants are worn out. My health is good. Please acknowledge all letters received. Love to all.
>
> Your affectionate brother,
>
> Alexander
>
> P.S. James, you must write longer letters. 'As you sow', you know, 'so you must reap.'

## Editorial Notes

**MAJOR EVENT: WEST POINT APPOINTMENT — FAMILY MILESTONE**

This letter marks a critical turning point in the Hubbell family history. James, who was academically struggling or uncertain (per the Feb. 7 anxiety), has now received his formal appointment to the United States Military Academy at West Point. Alexander's response is explosive with pride and exhortation. The appointment is a federal honor in the midst of a national war — demonstrating the family's status and James's academic capability despite earlier doubts.

**ALEXANDER'S EMOTIONAL RESPONSE**

Alexander's exuberance is evident in his hyperbolic language: "I shan't see my boots for a week" (excessive pride), "I could use my bayonet upon him [Doolittle] now with every good ground" (desire for revenge), and repeated imperatives to "study hard," "put your best foot forward," "exert yourself." This is the voice of an older brother deeply invested in a younger sibling's success during wartime — when education and advancement might seem frivolous compared to military service, but Alexander frames it as a great opportunity worth the family's effort.

**LITTLE DOOLITTLE INCIDENT**

The reference to "little Doolittle" is the only mention in the collection. Something Doolittle said to Alexander "concerning you" [James] made Alexander "very mad." Alexander now fantasizes about revenge with his bayonet but catches himself with "I must 'forgive and forget.'" This suggests a bullying or insulting incident in their academy — possibly predicting James would fail or unfit for West Point. Alexander's anger suggests he took the insult personally and sees James's appointment as vindication.

**DOBBIN & ACADEMY GOVERNANCE**

"Dobbin" is criticized as a trustee with a "scolding wife" — a trustee with a bad home situation cannot command respect from students. Alexander's pointed question ("Why don't the trustees 'oust' Dobbin?") suggests dissatisfaction with academy leadership or curriculum. This may be relevant to understanding James's academic situation and the support network required for his success.

**DOMESTIC HUMOR & RANK**

Alexander notes he's on kitchen duty (forenoon) and jokes he'll return home "entirely independent of the feminine gender" in cooking skills. This reveals a different side of military life: soldiers performed domestic labor, and Alexander had a sense of humor about it. He's already a Corporal (per earlier letters) and will receive NCO stripes on Feb. 17 (per that letter).

**CAMP WHEELER UNIFORM COMPARISON**

Alexander compares current blue pants to those from "Camp Wheeler" (a NY training camp). The jackets from Wheeler are "still good" but the pants are "worn out" — suggesting the campaign season has been harder on pants than jackets. New blue pants (same pattern) have just arrived; a "dress suit" is expected soon. This indicates the 60th NY is transitioning from training to campaign readiness.

**LETTER-WRITING REQUEST**

Alexander ends with a P.S. demanding longer letters from James, using the proverb "As you sow, you know, so you must reap" — suggesting James's short letters are reciprocal for Alexander's efforts. This is characteristic of war-era correspondence: brothers competed for attention and used letters as currency of affection.

## Event: West Point Appointment Confirmation (Feb. 11, 1862)

This letter confirms James Hubbell's appointment to the United States Military Academy at West Point, possibly for the class entering summer/fall 1862. The appointment represents a significant federal honor and family achievement during the Civil War. Alexander's response indicates: (1) surprise or recent announcement, (2) high family expectations for James's success, (3) some prior concerns about James's academic capabilities or social acceptance, (4) pride in a sibling achieving distinction while older brother serves in the ranks.

**SIGNIFICANCE FOR HUBBELL FAMILY NARRATIVE:** This is a family success in the midst of war. While Henry and Alexander serve as enlisted men and NCOs, James will serve as an officer (eventually). This represents social mobility and educational advancement during wartime — a counter-narrative to the grinding hardship of camp life that dominates the letters.
