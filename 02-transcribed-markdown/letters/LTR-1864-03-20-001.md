## Full Transcription

> March 20, 1864
> Stevenson, Ala.
>
> Dear Fannie:
>
> Here at last is the long expected letter which I have promised so often. Everything has gone on about as usual since I last wrote. We have got our shanty up and a fireplace built and are now quite comfortable. We have had several little starts occasioned by the movements of Morgan.
>
> The other night just as I was about to turn in the orderly came to me and ordered me to provide myself with three days rations and report myself to the Adjutant's office. I did so, and was sent with twenty men to picket about a mile east of camp. Such is a soldier's life. I had promised myself a good night's rest, but who can human calculations. I was ordered today to be ready to go to Chattanooga at half after two to guard prisoners, and this when I had settled myself comfortably to write you, but fortunately for your letter, the order was revoked and your letter will be finished if nothing else turns up. Now, Fannie, I will try and give you a picture.
>
> It is a most beautiful day. The whole country is dotted here and there with the different camps. Away in the distance you see little columns of smoke rising from the fires of the pickets. On a hill at a little distance is a large earth work mounting a brass battery which gleams and glistens brightly in the sun. Close by where I am sitting are headquarters — over which the stars and stripes are proudly floating. At a little distance from headquarters the Brigade band is playing — their instruments gleaming brightly in the sun. Quite a distance from there the drum corps of the different regts. are practicing. It altogether forms a most beautiful picture. I wish that I could paint and in painting could convey to you the different sounds. I would furnish you with many a delicious treat — a view of Seville with all the noise of the drums and bugles, — of Guard Mounting with the soul stirring airs of the Brigade band, — dinner call with its throng, each flourishing a tin cup or plate and each striving to be first at the cook house, — dress parade and its ceremony, — Tattoo and its accompaniment of drums and bugles, and last but not least, — Taps, the last plaintive, soothing call of the bugle which seems to invite all to repose.
>
> I guess you think I am running wild, — eh? Wouldn't blame you at all if you did. I have seen lots of southern women today — and such women! Mother Alice is a queen to these! Pooh! I wish Mr. Bass would come down here and see some of the Southerners he admires so much. He is short many of them would call a "Yankee dough face" — a class of men much despised by them. Oh these Southerners, long legged, long haired, lank, lean, saucy cheeked "sons of guns." Mrs. Bass says, you know, that three Northern men could be beat before one Southern one could. Poor doctrine, I think.
>
> If Marshall Dunn has got that bounty and you have my papers back again, please send me my furlough as I can get nine dollars on it for rations I have not received. You know that we are allowed thirty cents a day for rations. Government has got to shell over the difference.
>
> I have received only one letter so far from home and that was mailed Dec. 23rd — had a great deal to say about "petition" and "discharge" much to my amusement. Please write soon. I hope Culver Bostwick will come along soon. I suppose he is at Elmira now. He will find us very comfortably situated, but does Nathaniel Nichols have to say? How does your school prosper? I hope there are no scenes at the castle now. Guess you are about tired out, so will close.
>
> Your aff. brother,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell

## Editorial Notes

This is Alexander at his literary best — the panoramic camp scene, building from visual to auditory, from picket fires on the horizon to the last plaintive note of Taps. "I wish that I could paint and in painting could convey to you the different sounds." He's writing to his teenage sister, and the letter is a gift — a carefully composed word-painting of soldier life, from the gleaming brass battery to the scramble at the cook house with tin cups.

The scorn for "Yankee dough faces" — Northerners who admire the South — is Alexander at his most political. Mr. Bass back in Champlain, with his Southern sympathies, doesn't know what Alexander knows: "long legged, long haired, lank, lean, saucy cheeked sons of guns." The soldier's contempt for the civilian's romance.
