## Full Transcription

> Sept. 22, 1861.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> As the Captain is expecting to go home in a short time, I though I would write a few lines, although I have not much to relate. I expect Reich wi. go tomorrow or next day. I shall send twentyfive dollars by him.
>
> Our Camp was routed up not long since, but it was a false alarm. Two of the guard fired at a white horse. Some got frightened at nothing some times, but I doubt I should not wonder if we were called up again to night. Bombs have been fired over into Virginia today.
>
> I don't know of anything that I want except paper and ink pens. It is quite cold this morning. Seems more like fall. Our company goes on picket today for two days.
>
> So Alexander is in for the war. Perhaps it is all for the best. I don't think it can last a great while. I can say for one that I would rather be where I am now than to be any place, for I have gone all through the drilling and that I consider the worst of it. I received a week ago four papers besides the Observer — one paper from Aunt L. I have no more time to write.
>
> Your affectionate son,
>
> Henry.

## Editorial Notes

Henry's reaction to Alexander's enlistment is measured: "Perhaps it is all for the best." His reassurance that drilling is "the worst of it" reflects both brotherly comfort and genuine belief the war won't last long. The false alarm — guards shooting at a white horse — is a classic camp-life anecdote illustrating the nervousness of inexperienced sentries. Henry writes three letters in three days (Sept 21, 22, and 27), suggesting active correspondence and possibly anxiety about sending money home before deployment to picket.
