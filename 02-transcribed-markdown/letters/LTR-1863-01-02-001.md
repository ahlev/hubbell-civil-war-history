## Full Transcription

> Alexandria, Va.
> Jan. 2, 1863
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> You see this letter is dated different from my other letters and the reason is our Co. has been detached from the regiment and is stationed in the city near the Potomac. Our duties, I think, will be to take soldiers back near their reg'ts. Two squads from our Co. have taken two different lots of soldiers to Aquia Creek. I expect to go tomorrow as I have not been yet. The men like to go as they go and come on a steamboat. We take one or two days rations with us. One of our Lieuts. goes with the men. We moved day before yesterday, brought our tents and stoves with us. We all like our new quarters better than the old and I think we will stay here some time if the men behave themselves.
>
> Last night there was a heavy wind with rain and it blew down four of our tents leaving the tent I am in and another one standing. The men whose tents were down went into the large d tent which is close to us and staid till morning when they put up their tents. I went to Fairfax Station Jan. 14th to see Alek. Their camp is about one mile from Y. Station in the woods. I staid every night with A. I intended to take the cars at 7 A.M. Alex went with me to the depot, but the cars were gone, so I had to wait till afternoon. I was gone four hours over my time, but nothing was said about it. Alex was well and surprised to see me. His face is scarred with the eruption he had, but it is not bad. Capt. Ransom read me his letter to you. They had just had orders to be ready to march at 12 hours notice with 8 days cooked rations in their haversacks and 8 days rations in the wagons. The men were getting fixed for the winter and hated to leave. Some of the men told me that they had had hard times. They said that the 60th reg't. was called the thieving reg't. That the reg't. left no money behind them, but took all they came across. Alex told me that they did not have enough to eat on a march, but were compelled to buy of the sutler. Told me he had had no pay for six months and he owed the sutler $11.00. They get their water out of a kind of a ditch. The water was riley, but they did not seem to mind it. Said they had often drank out of mudholes in the road on a march. The 60th reg't. forms a part of Gen. Slocum's 12 army corps which is encamped in the woods where I was. The 60th Reg't. is in the 2nd Brigade and 2nd Division. James Luther sends his respects to you and Frances. He looks fat and healthy. The 11th and 12th army corps from the Reserve Grand Division of the army of the Potomac commanded by Gen. Sigel.
>
> I received those two handkerchiefs and say much obliged. Have received the Observer quite often lately. Alex told me he had no postage stamps; said it was the reason he had not written. I have some other. Wish you would send me some. Have borrowed quite a number. I tried to buy some with that dollar Frances sent me, but they wanted U.S. money.
>
> [page break]
>
> You say that Henry was urged not to go into that fight. Perhaps it is not so. When we were called out the roll was called and every man was obliged to go. Mr. Rye says he will pay over and above the appraisal what the land is worth. Does he mean that if they appraise the land below its value, he will make up the balance? If so, what does he call it worth? Mr. Savage has sold several lots at $500 an acre, just over Mrs. Turners. I suppose we had better sell a strip of the garden, but they ought to pay what it is worth. About the sugar bush, if his claim is good he ought to have that strip, but if there is any doubt about it, he can not have it. I don't know how he can come at it.
>
> Have you got fodder enough for this winter? How does Joe get along? Does he do his chores well? I hope Frances will not overwork herself and that you both will try to enjoy yourselves as well as you can. Please write often and I will do so.
>
> Affectionate son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell
>
> I suppose we ought to pay the church tax. But this tax is not for beautifying the church. I paid that tax at the time. This tax has run some time. Has the collector called on you? Have you paid Mr. Copeland anything?
>
> ..... 30 .....

## Editorial Notes

The first letter of 1863 opens with Charles in a new role — detached to Alexandria for straggler duty, ferrying soldiers back to their regiments by steamboat. His visit to Alexander at Fairfax Station provides the most vivid outsider description of the 60th NY's conditions: ditch water, mudhole drinking, the regiment's reputation as thieves, forced reliance on sutlers at exploitative prices.

The Henry passage is quietly devastating: "You say that Henry was urged not to go into that fight. Perhaps it is not so." Charles is correcting his mother's narrative — the comforting idea that Henry could have stayed behind, that someone urged him not to go, that his death was a choice rather than an obligation. "When we were called out the roll was called and every man was obliged to go." The roll was called. Every man went. Henry had no choice.
