## Full Transcription

> Dec. 8, 1862
> Camp near
> Alexandria, Va.
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> As I am off guard today I will write to you. Guard duty at the city these cold nights is no fun. We are not allowed any guard fires now. The reason is, I suppose, that the soldiers steal all the wood they can get hold of. Have been burning the grave yard fence. There are three fires, one at each end of the pickets & one in the middle, but we are going to have guard tents put up with stoves in them so that the guard can warm themselves, I hear. There are 2 or 3 men put on in a place so that they can relieve each other. When we go on guard nights we put on our over coats & our blankets. I dont know as I can say put on our overcoats for we have them on nearly all the time. When three men are on a post two can put their blankets together & lay down, but I dont think any one will try it this cold weather.
>
> There is snow on the ground a slight covering & plenty of ice. A creek near us thot running into the Potomac is frozen over solid that we might have good skating if we had any skates. But it will not last long, I think. Food is scarce here. We got out of wood so we went to a fine piece of ashwoods, young trees about as large as my leg, & carried it to camp. It was a pretty large piece of woods, but they came from the convalescent camp in a steady stream all day to hack it & to their camp which is some ways off. Our men & theirs have cut this woods all down. There are 10 or 12 thousand men in the convalescent camp sick, convalescent, & well. The camp is about to be broken up, the well men to return to their regiments, some to return to the hospitals & some to be discharged. This camp we call loose hill.
>
> Capt. Rich is now Aid De Camp to Gen Slough who is military governor of Alexandria. I suppose Rich's employment is till we leave here, but I think from the looks of things we will stay here this winter. The officers are fixing up their tents to well to leave soon.
>
> You wished to know if I was detailed as funeral escort. I was detailed one day, but did not act as there was no death. 26 men were detailed daily as funeral escorts, but there are more detailed now. They went to the provost marshals office where they were told if there were any deaths. The escorts that was to butt of the provost M. office all day if they had nothing else to do, for a funeral might take place any time.
>
> I am glad you have found that memorandum book. When us boys get our pay you will have some figures to put down. I signed an allotment roll at Fonda allotting $10 a month to you. Have it the men signed it. I wrote Jake a few days ago. Received your letter of Dec. 1st, also the Observer. The Observer is like an old friend. I have just seen Ben Bouron & Freeman Houron. Ben is going to Yorktown
>
> [page break]
>
> if he can get a pass, to see his brother Lyman. Ben says it cost him $16 to come here. Will you send me a little woolen yarn & a couple of darning needles if you send me anything. Mrs. Doolittle says he does not have enough to eat. Says he eats when he can cook for himself. Says the men are lazy. Says that each man cooks for himself.
>
> We went down to the city & went through it, but not a thing could we buy as neither of us had a cent of money. There is nearly 6 months pay due Alek & nearly 4 months due me. I dont know when we will be paid. Uncle has poor I guess. The men in our reg. are being trusted by the sutler. I shall not trade with him as he sells to dear. He has good butter, but it is 40 cts. a pound. Some of the men in our tent buy it. Alek says he owes the sutler $11.00 — had to buy as they could not get enough to eat.
>
> We have just received our rubber blankets. I am very glad for they are just what we need. I have been very lucky about getting wet — have not been wet yet. I mean the time very well when not on duty. Riley from Corbeaus has papers sent to him which I read. With a good fire in the stove & a canoe to read I make out first rate. When we are on guard at night we are excused from any duty the next forenoon when we stretch out on the floor, feet to the stove, & try to take a nap, but we are pretty sure to get woke up by the mates. They say that one of the Fonda companies, the Captain I mean, cheated the Government by taking some men of another Co. & putting them with his to fill out his Co. till they had been inspected & drawing the bounty for three men who did not belong in his Co.
>
> I have not been home sick since I have been here. Am glad that I am not a family man as they are the ones who are homesick. I bet Staats says if he ever gets home they will not catch him again — he will not be such a fool as to enlist. The soldiers here all seem to hate the niger. They say it makes them mad to think they are fighting to free the niger. We have a small brass band & a lot of drummers so that we have music mough. Hear it every day at dress parade & guard mounting. I guess you forgot that Alexandria is a large city. I can see and can buy almost anything here. I should not feel anxious about James if he does not succeed at West Point. He will in something else. I wish you would write all about home matters.
>
> [page break — Dec 13 continuation]
>
> Dec. 13, 1862
> Camp near Alexandria
>
> Dear Mother:
>
> I received your welcome letter last evening. They always come in the evening. Are brought to each man by some one of the Co.
>
> I was very sorry to learn that McDonalds story about Henry was not true. I was afraid when you first wrote of his story that there was some mistake. I still think he may be in or prisoner in some rebel hospital. Probably he was badly wounded. Savagham heard from Mathews that his brother was most likely in a hospital. Bethune belongs to Capt. Rich's old Co. He was not in the battle as he was in the hospital at the time. I saw him in Washington when our reg. was coming here. Also saw him not long since here. He had cut from a newspaper a list of the killed & wounded in his Co. at the battle instances[?]. He showed me the list of killed & wounded. Came here to see Capt. Rich. A stayed here with him a day or two. I did not see him long either time. Both meetings were accidental & he introduced himself. Savagham heard from Mathews, not since if we had heard from Henry. McDonald's report might have started at Champlain. Somebody might have wrote to their friends in the army about him. Mr. Cook might have written Albert & he some one else in Co. B.
>
> [page break]
>
> About horse beef; we do not have any. We have salt beef which I like when the salt is all out. That is, when it is well cooked. Some call it salt horse.
>
> We are not going to North Carolina. I don't think anyone believed we were. The regiment will stay here all winter. We have just had guard tents put up around the city for the guard. There is a fire in each tent & the guard relieve each other. We have also our rubber blankets so that we will be quite comfortable. The guard stays at the city 24 hours. We have boiled fresh beef about four times a week. Each man has a loaf of bread a day which he puts in his haversack. The bread sells in the city for 5 cts. We have boiled rice quite often which I like. It is sweetened a little. A good many of the men dont like it. We have two good cooks now.
>
> I think we will not get our pay till next month. Wish you could send me one dollar. I have sold two dollars for repairing my watch. If that box comes will you send me two pairs of wool socks & two cheap handkerchiefs. They will be plenty. I have 3 prs. of socks but they will wear out before spring. A good many of the men have drawn socks. Some of them were thick & good — others not so. If not convenient dont send them as I can get socks etc. here.
>
> I have not heard from sick yet. I do not like to write letters so Uncle John need not write often. I mean letters to strangers. Has Pollard married Mary Ann Brinkerhoff — you know who I mean. "I. Staats heard so from home. Have you engaged any one to draw those down saw logs. They ought to be drawn the first snow. Neshan will draw them or know of some one.
>
> About James: I hope he will succeed, but if he does not it need be no disgrace. All are not calculated for military men & besides a good many smart men have not succeeded there.
>
> I have seen some paroled prisoners (there is a camp of them not far off), but it would be useless to ask them about Henry as if they saw him they would not notice him as he was not in their Co. We have a good Chaplain. He has only preached to us two short sermons so far. & his sermons ought to be short as the men are drawn up in hollow square & stand. I mean 3 sides of a squad. The Chaplain stands in the 4 side & the officers also. He prays without kneeling. Some of the soldiers sing out of the soldiers hymn book. The Chaplain is quite sick now, caught cold at a soldiers funeral. The Catholics in our Co. attend church every Sunday in the city under the charge of the 1st Lieut. who is a Catholic. I dont know as there is a service anywhere else in the city.
>
> I hope you will write often. You & Frances must make yourselves as comfortable as possible. We do not suffer from the cold as we have good fires. Our tent is a good tight one.
>
> Your aff. Son,
>
> C. F. Hubbell
>
> ..... 30 .....

### Connections to Other Letters in Collection
- **McDonald chain debunked:** Charles traces how the false hope spread: McDonald told someone, someone wrote to Champlain, Champlain wrote to the army, and the story circulated back. "McDonald's report might have started at Champlain." This is the 19th-century telephone game at its most devastating.
- **Anti-emancipation sentiment:** Charles reports this without endorsing it — "The soldiers here all seem to hate the niger." Lincoln's Preliminary Emancipation Proclamation (Sept 22) has shifted the war's stated purpose, and many soldiers resent it. This is December 1862 — the final Emancipation Proclamation takes effect Jan 1, 1863.
- **Fredericksburg timing:** Charles's Dec 13 continuation is written the same day as the main assault on Marye's Heights. He is in Alexandria, not at the front — but the 34th NY (Henry's old regiment, where McDonald serves) IS crossing the Rappahannock. The 60th NY (Alexander's) may also be engaged.

## Editorial Notes

Charles's most sprawling letter — a comprehensive portrait of garrison life in December 1862. The anti-Black passage is uncomfortable but historically significant: it documents the rank-and-file resistance to emancipation that nearly fractured the Union army in late 1862. Charles doesn't offer his own opinion — he reports what "the soldiers" say — but the inclusion is telling.

The McDonald chain-of-evidence passage is important for the collection's investigation of Henry's fate. Charles has essentially performed a forensic analysis of how the false hope originated and spread. His conclusion — nobody can actually confirm Henry is alive — is the most clear-eyed assessment anyone in the family has offered.
