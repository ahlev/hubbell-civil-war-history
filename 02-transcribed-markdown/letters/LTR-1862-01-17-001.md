## Full Transcription

> Jan 17, 1862.
> Camden Station — Baltimore
>
> My Dear Brother:
>
> I received a letter from home yesterday and no one who has not been from home can imagine how glad I was to hear from there. I thought you had all forgotten me. So Frances has really gone. You must be very lonely there this winter. How I envy your long quiet pleasant evenings. What are you studying at school this winter? How do Mr. and Mrs. Dobbin get along. Any more "hair pulls" yet, eh? So Dobbin did not want his wife to eat "nasty orange peel picked off from the floor", eh? How I wish I had been there. I should have had a regular ha ha. I suppose old Dobbin would have taken my head off. How I should like to see him and Mrs. D. get into a "squabble". I should enjoy it up to ninety-nine. What am I talking about. Write me all about home matters. I feel much more interested in matters concerning the "pig", old white cow and calf, and the pony than in anything else.
>
> Have you drawn any more beach logs this winter. Rather unprofitable business with that little "pony" I think. How has Plummer fulfilled his contract? I am afraid you will get shaved. Keep a sharp lookout on him. He will lookout for No. 1, never fear.
>
> (Contd.)
>
> Jan. 17, 1862 — (Contd.
>
> Now I suppose you would like to hear about that "sad accident" that happened here some days since. I had just thrown myself down on a mattress to "snoose" a few moments (I was helping to take care of a sick mess mate) when Sergeant Finch came to the door of the Lieutenants room and roared out Lieutenant, Lieutenant, Porter has been killed upon the track. Shall I take some men and bring him in? I immediately jumped up, put on my overcoat, went down and joined the squad. We had gone about three quarters of a mile when we came upon the body. It was a heart rending sight. He lay partly under the cars and was I might say, literally cut in two as the body only hung together by the cords and muscles of the back. A large piece of his back bone was torn out and lay some distance away. A large piece of flesh lay all around. His liver and entrails were also torn out and pieces of flesh lay all around.
>
> Jan. 21 — I will finish this narrative in another letter. I am enjoying excellent health though I have had a severe cough and cold and am not entirely over them yet. I try to obey your injunctions "to take care of my health". When I read in your letter what Brink Miner said I felt just mad enough to kick him. (Excuse me for saying it.) I wonder if he thinks every one is like him, not two seconds content in one place. I did not enlist only for the novelty of the thing as he did, but to try and take care of myself and to fight for my native land. I don't like to see a young man able to care for himself sit at home with his hands in his lap, or when he is employed to grumble, growl and fret about it, and wish himself out of it. I believe in making the best of everything. (at least I try to.) A man is a great deal happier for doing so.
>
> I have changed my mind and think I will finish my "narrative" in this letter. It was as I have said before — a heartrending sight. The dim lantern light made it doubly so. We made a litter of boards and brought him down to camp. It was pretty tough work, as he was a very large man. We were obliged to be very careful when we lifted him on the litter he was so crushed. His body was completely crushed from the breast down, also his left arm and right leg. There was an ugly gash on his left temple. As soon as we arrived at the camp the Capt. was sent for. A room was immediately prepared and the Capt. laid him out as well as it could be done. An inquest was held next day and a verdict returned of accidental death by being run over by the cars. He was buried the day after the inquest.
>
> Perhaps you would like to hear something about the funeral. First went a Corporal and eight men with arms reversed, next the Chaplain, next the hearse, and last the Company. The band for some reason was no there. When we arrived at the cemetery the coffin was placed in a vault and three volleys fired over it.
>
> Good night. It is now late. Luther is snoring away like a good fellow. Write soon. Love to all. I will write about the Union Relief Association in another letter.
>
> Your affectionate Brother,
>
> Alexander
>
> To James Hubbell esq.

### Historical Significance: `major`

> Porter has been killed upon the track. Roused from a mattress at a sick mess-mate's side, Alexander joined the squad that found him three-quarters of a mile out, "literally cut in two." ## Editorial Notes

This is Alexander's most viscerally graphic letter — the recovery of Porter's body by lantern light, "literally cut in two," backbone torn out, entrails scattered. Alexander doesn't flinch from the description, telling James he'll share it "if you want me to" — then deciding to include it. The military funeral is described with procedural precision (corporal + 8 men arms reversed, chaplain, hearse, company, 3 volleys) — Alexander the observer, documenting. The Brink Miner passage reveals a new edge: Alexander is furious at being judged by a man who enlisted "only for the novelty." His defense — "I did not enlist only for the novelty of the thing as he did, but to try and take care of myself and to fight for my native land" — is the clearest statement of his motivation in the collection. The juxtaposition of graphic death, farm business (pig, cow, pony, beach logs), and Dobbin's domestic comedy makes this letter a microcosm of the wartime mind: death and livestock, patriotism and orange peels.
