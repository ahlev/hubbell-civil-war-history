## Full Transcription

> [First 8 pages missing]
>
> It has been a beautiful warm day. The weather of last month was raw and chilly most of the time. If it was a sample of what you had up there, there must have been very little sugar made during the month of March. From what you say I suppose old Nye is running our bush. It would hardly pay to go to law with him. His heirs will probably be more tractable to settle with than he.
>
> We are enjoying ourselves the best way we can and there is but little lack of way. The great game on the docket now is ball. Two regts. of the Brigade challenged our Regiment to a game of (Union Base) a kind of game of ball. Our regiment beat them badly.
>
> Evening — The boys have got a fiddle and are having a grand dance on the company street. If I understood dancing I should take a turn with them.
>
> Culver Bostwick has not yet appeared, nor any of the recruits for our regiment. They still probably be sent on before long. We will probably be sent on before long. We will probably be on the march before they reach us, though we have not a great way to go before we will find the enemy. It is the opinion of some here that we will not do much more at present than to harass the enemy in order to prevent reinforcing Lee. Everything indicates active service and soon.
>
> You can direct your letters to the same Corps, as usual. When we are thoroughly organized I will give you the address. I feel quite sad tonight. I suppose Frances would say that I have the blues. I shall wait until I feel a little more cheerful to finish the letter. Did ever you see a postscript longer than the letter itself before?
>
> April 8th — The mail has come in this morning bringing me a N.Y. Tribune from home. It was mailed March 31st. I hope to get a letter soon. I shall send this off if I can manage to borrow a stamp. Go on guard at half after nine today. Shall be at the fence or stockade.
>
> We had a great time yesterday. The officers of the Brig. clubbed together and made up a number of purses to the winners in a horse race, sack race, wheelbarrow race, foot race, cook fight, and whoever managed to climb the greased pole. They also had a greased pig expressly for the contrabands to run after. You ought to have seen the little darkies rolling over together on the ground — the greased pig slipping like an eel, from under them. The shell barrow race was performed with a sack over the heads of the competitors with their eyes blindfolded. The sack race was about as amusing as anything I saw there.
>
> I was wonderfully pleased yesterday by the receipt of a Magazine. It was like a shower to a thirsty desert. I hardly know who to thank for it. The address on the Magazine was in your handwriting — on the inside of the cover was "Prospects of S.P. Hubbell" and a little further over Miss Fannie wishes to know if "I will be satisfied with this until she can comply with my favorite advertisement of batter and lard." Now which shall I choose — I am in a quandary and shall leave you to settle it amongst you — giving my most heartfelt thanks to whoever sent it. I hope I may again — and often — have the pleasure of reading "Harpers" in this benighted land.
>
> [Page break]
>
> I want to give you a sentence of a contraband preacher's eloquence, which I doubt not was very edifying to his colored brethren, but which amused me some by its oddity. He was holding forth on the suffering of our Savior. Said he made — "Fess my brederin, day drove a ten penny nail troo his hands and feet — just as true as you are born!" They probably understood the truth presented in this way better than if it had been presented in a more polished manner.
>
> Please write often. Much love to Frances and all friends. I received a letter from Mrs. Reams (Cousin Nell) and she wants to know why you and Frances don't write. I wrote as soon as I received your letter announcing her marriage.
>
> A letter of ten pages. Beat it if you can.
>
> Your affectionate Son,
>
> Alex F. Hubbell.

## Editorial Notes

The baseball reference is historically significant — "Union Base" was one of several ball games played by Civil War soldiers that contributed to baseball's spread across America. The 60th NY beating two regiments of the Brigade is reported with obvious pride. The game is embedded in a catalogue of camp amusements — races, cock fights, greased poles — that capture the army at leisure before the killing begins.

The contraband preacher's sermon — "Fess my brederin, day drove a ten penny nail troo his hands and feet" — is Alexander's most extended engagement with Black speech. His comment that "they probably understood the truth better" than if presented in "more polished manner" suggests genuine respect beneath the era's condescension.

**Note on text:** The "blues" passage and April 8 postscript are nearly word-for-word identical to text in LTR-1864-01-08-001 (dated January 8). This is almost certainly a typist duplication — the same passage transcribed into two different letters. The original 10-page letter is fragmentary, making verification impossible.
