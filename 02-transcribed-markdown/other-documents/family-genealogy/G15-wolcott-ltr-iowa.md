## Full Transcription

> WOLCOTT WOOD HUBBELL
> 1000 NO. VIRGO AVE
> PASADENA, CALIFORNIA
>
> December 22, 1962.
>
> Mr. Frederick A. Hubbell, Jr.
> 97 Peach Tree Way, N. E.,
> Atlanta, Ga.;
>
> Dear Fred:
>
> My cousin, Cordelia Patterson Fairburn, of Fonda, Iowa, sent me an air-mail letter concerning something in the deep, far past of the parents of your father and myself. I will give you a brief history to acquaint you with the facts.
>
> Our father and mother (of your father and myself and your Aunt Affa) were all devout Presbyterians. As you probably know, father went into far western Iowa (the last forty miles or so by horseback) in 1869, and he and the only one of the other three brothers who survived the Civil War bought land both by purchase and by homestead and settled there in 1870. His mother and a sister, Frances, made their homes with the brothers. Charles died in 1875, as a result of wounds and illness in the war. In 1886 father was one of a small group who organized a Presbyterian church in Fonda, eight miles from the farm. He as well as his mother, Aunt Frances, and Mrs. Adele Curtiss (an older sister of mother's) were among the seventeen listed as charter members. Mother and her father and mother and other brothers and sisters were almost charter members, and were active in the church almost from the start, until death or removal from Fonda.
>
> In 1890 grandmother Hubbell gave the church a silver communion set. In 1898 Aunt Frances added two cups and plates. All of these, suitably engraved with the names of the donors, the dates, etc., are still in possession of and in use by the little church. For some reason a minister in Des Moines wanted one of the cups, and has requested it. Cousin Cordelia did not give me any of the details — whether it was merely for a private collection, a historical collection, or what. But apparently the elders of the church feel under some compulsion, and obligation. Also, they do not seem to want to give the pieces up. Cordelia was asked if there were any living relatives, and she named me. The elders asked for a letter, which would be kept in the pitcher for future generations' information, opposing the gift of the cup. I have sent such a letter to Cordelia, and I thought that you, as the other surviving direct male heir, might also want to express your views.

### Page 2

> Also, the years that have passed are so many that the set is really of historical value.
>
> If you should write address your letter to the Elders of the Presbyterian Church, Fonda, Iowa, and send it to Mrs. Frank A. Fairburn, Fonda Iowa. She will probably endorse it. The elders asked her to endorse the letter from me.
>
> The Aunt Frances named was our Auntie Fan of childhood days, who so adored your father. He was the first born, and was the apple of her eye — much more so than the others of our children. In fact, according to family hearsay, she was so devoted to Fred that she somewhat interfered with necessary juvenile discipline — [strikethrough]
>
> I sometimes wonder what has ever become of father's Civil War letters. If they are still in the possession of your mother or one of you children they are not doing the world any good. Why not donate them to some library? You are fortunate in having in Atlanta one of the country's foremost Civil War Authorities, Dr. Bell Irwin Wiley of Emory University. Furthermore, he specializes in such material as this, and has edited two books THE LIFE OF BILLY YANK and THE LIFE OF JOHNNY REB, both made up from hundreds of letters, diaries, etc., of old soldiers. [And] still additional advantage in contacting Dr. Wiley is that he is the president of a publishing house, McCowat-Mercer, Jackson, Tenn., who have published a very creditable line of Civil War books. Why not at least get in touch with him and ask him to evaluate the material? You might have a real find. The National and all of the State Civil War Centennial Commissions are begging for such material as this to be made public.
>
> I am sorry that I do not know whether or not your mother is still living, or the state of her health if she is. Nor you or your sister either, for that matter. But anyway, I will hope that you are all alive and well and enjoying life, *that you all had a Merry Christmas and will have a New Year the best ever.*
>
> Very truly yours,
> [signed] Wolcott W. Hubbell
>
> [Handwritten note at bottom:] *Cordelia is the daughter of mother's younger sister, Cora*

### The Civil War Letters — Meta-History
Wolcott's 1962 question is deeply poignant in retrospect:
> "I sometimes wonder what has ever become of father's Civil War letters. If they are still in the possession of your mother or one of you children they are not doing the world any good."

**The answer, 63 years later:** Fred Jr.'s mother Gladys had transcribed them in 1947–1949. Fred Jr. wrote the introduction in 1996. Bruce Levitt scanned them in 2025. Alexander Hubbell Levitt is now building a digital archive from them in 2025–2026. The letters found their audience — not through Dr. Wiley at Emory, but through the family itself, across four generations.

## Notes

This letter, written 9 years before G14, is warmer in tone — the family rift may have deepened over the intervening years. Wolcott signs his full middle name "Wood" (for his mother's maiden name).

The recommendation of Dr. Bell Irwin Wiley is historically significant — Wiley's *The Life of Billy Yank* (1952) and *The Life of Johnny Reb* (1943) are foundational works in Civil War social history, built from exactly the kind of soldier correspondence the Hubbell collection contains. Wolcott recognized the scholarly value of the letters decades before anyone acted on it.

The handwritten postscript identifying Cordelia as "the daughter of mother's younger sister, Cora" confirms Lois Wood Hubbell had at least two sisters: Adele (older, married Curtiss) and Cora (younger, whose daughter is Cordelia Patterson Fairburn). This aligns with W02 (Judith V. Wood email) which lists Lois Ann as one of Alpheus Wood's children.
