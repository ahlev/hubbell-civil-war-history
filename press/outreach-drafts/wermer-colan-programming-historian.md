Hi Alex,

Programming Historian is where I keep sending people who want to actually interpret the outputs of digital methods rather than just run them — so I wanted to share a workflow and ask whether it might fit your lesson model.

I'm an amateur historian who recovered and published my family's Civil War correspondence: 273 letters from four soldier-brothers and their mother, 1861–1870, typed from manuscript in the 1940s, digitized in the 2010s, and structured by me this past year. The interesting part isn't the genealogy — it's the data architecture.

The corpus uses curated alias tables resolving to canonical person IDs instead of fuzzy name matching; provenance is preserved at the data layer while enrichment happens only at display; and an LLM is used as a constrained judge — a filter, never a source. It's a small, honest pattern other people working on family papers or letter collections could reuse.
