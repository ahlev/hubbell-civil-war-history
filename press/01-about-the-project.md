# About the Project — The Hubbell Civil War Letters

## Boilerplate (~100 words — ends every pitch and release, verbatim)

The Hubbell Civil War Letters is a free interactive archive of 272 letters written between 1861 and 1870 by four soldier-brothers from Champlain, New York — Henry (34th NY), Alexander (60th NY), James and Charles (153rd NY) — and their mother, Frances. Preserved by five generations of the family and typed in full on a typewriter in 1947–49, the collection was transcribed, structured, and published in 2026 by Alexander Hubbell Levitt, the great-great-grandson and namesake of the one brother who survived the war, using AI held to a published rule: the model is a filter, never a source. Explore it at hubbell-civil-war-history.vercel.app.

## 300 words

Four brothers from one farm town on Lake Champlain went to war in 1861. Henry was killed in the West Woods at Antietam; his body was never recovered. Alexander fought at Culp's Hill at Gettysburg — writing home on July 4, 1863, "We are burying our and the rebel's dead today" — was wounded at Lookout Mountain, and marched to Atlanta. James and Charles fought at Cedar Creek in the Shenandoah; James, wounded there, survived the war only to die in Albany on his way home. Their mother, Frances, held the household together and wrote back — including a letter to Henry, composed while she read the casualty lists "with trembling," that he never lived to receive.

The family kept everything. Frances saved the letters; her granddaughter-in-law Gladys typed all 272 of them between 1947 and 1949; later generations wrote introductions and made digital scans. In 2026, Alexander Hubbell Levitt — a technology researcher, and the great-great-grandson and namesake of the surviving brother — finished the work: every letter transcribed and checked by hand, every person and place resolved into curated registries (486 people, 353 places), the whole collection published free as an interactive archive with nine visualizations, from an animated campaign map to a ledger of the family's health to a view of what each writer didn't yet know when they wrote.

The AI that made this possible was governed by one rule, documented in a published methodology: it is a filter, never a source. Every fact it surfaced was verified against the documents and the public record — and that verification produced discoveries of its own, including a correction to the family's own memory of when James died. The result is both a family's story and a working model for what any family's box of letters could become.
