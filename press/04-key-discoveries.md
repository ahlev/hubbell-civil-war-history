# Key Discoveries & Story Starters

*Four self-contained stories, each with verbatim, letter-ID-cited quotes. Every quote below has been verified against its own transcription in `03-data/all-letters.json`. Deep links to be added when the production URL is confirmed.*

---

## 1. The mother who wrote to a dead son — Antietam, September 1862

Henry Hubbell of the 34th New York was killed in the West Woods at Antietam on September 17, 1862. His body was never recovered. What the archive holds is the disaster arriving at one family, in real time:

- **Sept 19, 1862** — his brother Alexander (60th NY, also on the field) writes their mother from "Camp on the battle field... Near Antietam": *"I only wish I could report the same of Henry. I saw one of the 34th this morning and he said that he is missing... I feel very anxious about him, but hope for the best."* (LTR-1862-09-19-001)
- **Sept 21, 1862** — Frances, in Champlain, not knowing, writes **to Henry**: *"Language is too weak, to express my anxiety about you since the commencement of the terrible battle... I read the sad record day after day as it is published, with trembling. The record of yesterday shows that the 34th has been terribly cut up."* He had been dead four days. (LTR-1862-09-21-002)
- **April 27, 1863** — seven months later, Charles, stationed in Maryland, wants to walk the field: he doesn't know *"who would go with me that knows where the 34th reg't. fought,"* and concludes: *"I think there is not the least doubt that Henry was killed. We would have heard from him long before this if he had been living."* (LTR-1863-04-27-001)

The project's cross-referencing later confirmed Henry's service and death in the public record — where the family's private grief and the state's official register finally agree.

## 2. A letter written on the Gettysburg battlefield, July 4, 1863

Alexander's 60th New York — Greene's Brigade — held Culp's Hill. On July 4, from "Near Gettysburg, Penna," he wrote his mother: *"God has again in his great kindness preserved my life through another most terrific battle... The reg't. lost fifty-two — eleven killed and forty-some wounded."* He names his company's casualties — Milebus Ayres killed, Sergeant Brockway and Private John Tickle wounded — and reports: *"We are burying our and the rebel's dead today."* (LTR-1863-07-04-001) An essentially unpublished eyewitness account from one of the battle's most argued-over positions, written before the armies had even left the field.

## 3. The family remembered the wrong date for 160 years — and the archive corrected it

James Hubbell (153rd NY) was wounded at Cedar Creek on October 19, 1864 — *"I was wounded in the left thigh at Cedar Run... I walked several miles after being hit in order not to fall into the Johnnies hands,"* he told his brother from a Baltimore hospital (LTR-1864-10-29-001). He recovered enough to serve out the war, mustered out at Savannah on October 2, 1865 — and died in Albany ten days later, on his way home. Family history remembered his death as October 19. Cross-referencing the muster rolls, the brothers' cemetery monument in Champlain, and period newspapers proved it was **October 12** — the family's memory had fused his death with the anniversary of his wounding. The project found the error in its own founding story, and documented the fix.

## 4. Five generations, one archive

Frances saved the letters. Gladys Sands Hubbell typed all 272 of them, 1947–49, on a typewriter. Fred Alexander Hubbell Jr. wrote the collection's introduction in 1996. Bruce Levitt digitized the typescripts in the 2010s. Alexander Hubbell Levitt structured and published them in 2026 — with AI doing the labor a research team once would have, under a documented rule that the model is a filter, never a source. Every generation used the tools it had; the archive is the fifth act.
