## Chapter 14: The Furnace

---

The Atlanta Campaign began on May 7, 1864, and did not stop.

That was the thing Alexander could never quite convey in his letters — the not stopping. Previous campaigns had been punctuated by pauses: battles followed by weeks of camp life, marches followed by months of guard duty. The rhythm of the eastern theater had been burst-and-rest, fight-and-recover, the war coming in waves with troughs between them where a man could write letters and wash his clothes and remember who he was.

The Atlanta Campaign had no troughs.

Sherman's strategy was simple in conception and brutal in execution: advance. Every day, advance. Flank the enemy out of his entrenchments, push him south, force him to build new entrenchments, then flank him out of those. Fight in the morning, entrench at night. March at dawn, fight at noon. Sleep when you can. Eat what you have. Write home if there's time. There is never enough time.

The regiment crossed into Georgia in early May and entered a landscape that was different from anything Alexander had fought in before — densely forested hills, narrow valleys, roads that were little more than trails through the woods. The terrain favored defense, and the Confederates under Joseph Johnston made the most of it, building fortifications at every ridge and river crossing, forcing Sherman to maneuver rather than assault, turning every mile of advance into a chess problem with bayonets.

*We have been fighting nearly every day since I last wrote you and working the greater portion of every night (and sometimes the whole) entrenching ourselves in positions gained the previous day.*

Alexander wrote that on June 20 from a camp near Marietta, six weeks into the campaign, and the sentence was a masterpiece of compression — two months of continuous combat distilled into a single sentence that conveyed more about the reality of the Atlanta Campaign than any newspaper correspondent's dispatch.

Fighting every day. Working every night. Gaining ground by daylight and digging into it by dark. It was industrial warfare, the factory method applied to killing, and the men who endured it aged in months what men in other wars aged in years.

---

The letters from this period came in bursts — two or three in a week when the mail could get through, then silence for weeks when the lines were disrupted. They were shorter than Alexander's earlier letters, more compressed, written in the margins of combat with a hand that was steadier than it had any right to be.

May 31, 1864. Entrenchments near Dallas, Georgia. The most famous letter of the campaign.

*This is written while we are under fire, the bullets whistling over our heads (there goes one now — b-e-a-u-t-i-f-u-l) every now and then — not a little oftener.*

Beautiful. Alexander spelled it out — *b-e-a-u-t-i-f-u-l* — with the leisurely precision of a man who is either very brave or very tired of being afraid or some combination of both. The parenthetical — *(there goes one now)* — was a real-time interruption, a bullet passing overhead at the exact moment Alexander's pen was forming the word, and his response was not to duck or to drop the pen but to note it, annotate it, incorporate it into the letter as casually as if he were noting the passage of a bird.

It was the single most quoted sentence in the entire collection, and it deserved to be. It captured something essential about Alexander and about the war: the capacity of human beings to domesticate horror, to make it ordinary, to write letters in the middle of it with a pen in one hand and their life in the other.

*The rebel works are but a very short distance from ours. We can hear them talk quite frequently and distinctly.*

Close enough to hear. Close enough to kill. The distance between the lines at Dallas was measured in yards, not miles — sometimes in feet. The men on both sides crouched behind earthworks and logs, firing at any movement, and the space between them was a no-man's land where nothing lived and everything died.

*The Gen'l. will not allow us to go out on the skirmishing line. Says I am always ready to do my duty — but that is no place for me, — the men of the keenest sight are the only ones that are needed there.*

Alexander's eyesight was failing. The night blindness that would later hospitalize him was already manifesting, a consequence of the Vitamin C deficiency that was building in his body from months of salt pork and hardtack and nothing fresh. The general recognized it and pulled him from the skirmish line — a decision that may have saved Alexander's life, since the skirmish line was the most dangerous position in the army, the place where the sharpest-eyed men stood closest to the enemy and died fastest.

Alexander did not protest. He noted the general's words with the pride of a man who had been recognized for his willingness to serve, and he did not mention the eyesight problem directly. He let the general's order carry the explanation.

---

June 20. Camp near Marietta. The letter that laid bare the Atlanta Campaign's cost.

*The list of killed and wounded in our regt. is beginning to pile up fearfully.*

Pile up. The metaphor was physical — bodies, names, the paper lists that the adjutant maintained growing longer with each day's fighting. Alexander had watched the regiment shrink from two hundred at Antietam to something smaller, and now it was shrinking again, the daily attrition of the Atlanta Campaign reducing it man by man.

*None of the boys from Champlain have been injured.*

He noted this because it mattered — because the boys from Champlain were the ones his mother would ask about, the sons of the women she saw at church and at the post office. The casualty lists in the Champlain newspaper would carry the names of local men, and Frances would be reading them, and Alexander wanted her to know that the local boys were still standing.

*Our Regt. was relieved from the front line this morning by the 2nd, and we now rest a little in the rear of the line of battle but not entirely out of range as the whistle of an occasional bullet plainly tells us.*

Rest. In the rear. But not out of range. Even rest, in the Atlanta Campaign, was not safe. The bullets reached everywhere, and the sound of them — the whistle, the crack, the thud — was as constant as birdsong.

*Our batteries are keeping up a pretty steady fire. The skirmish lines are also pretty hotly engaged. The rebel sharpshooters are at present keeping up a pretty lively fire on one of our batteries on the hill just above us, but with not much effect.*

"Pretty lively." "Not much effect." The language was laconic, almost bored, and the boredom was itself a kind of courage — the courage of a man who has been under fire for six weeks and has learned to describe it the way a farmer describes plowing: it was hard work, it went on, you did it.

Then the letter turned, as Alexander's letters always turned, from the war to the family:

*Have you heard from James yet? His silence is singular and I think very wrong.*

James. Alexander's brother, the scholar, the thin one. James had gone silent — not writing to their mother, not writing to Alexander, apparently not writing to anyone. Alexander was worried, and the worry expressed itself as disapproval ("very wrong") rather than anxiety, because disapproval was easier to put on paper.

*The reason of it I think is that he feels his failure keenly and he wishes to make something of a mark in the world before he writes you, so that he may be able to compensate you for your disappointment in his failure.*

The "failure" was James's brief stint at West Point — he had attended in the summer of 1862 but had not lasted. Alexander diagnosed James's silence with a psychological acuity that was remarkable for a twenty-year-old in a trench: James was ashamed, and shame made men silent.

It was the kind of insight that Alexander usually reserved for his own feelings. But the Atlanta Campaign, with its constant proximity to death, had sharpened his awareness of others. When you might die tomorrow, you paid attention to the people you loved. You noticed their silences. You worried about their shame.

---

The letter's closing was the most often quoted passage from the Atlanta Campaign, and it deserved its fame:

*Love to all. I hope I will come out of this campaign all right, but if God orders it otherwise may we all meet in heaven. My home and trust is in Jesus our Saviour.*

*If God orders it otherwise.* The conditional was Alexander's way of saying what he could not say directly: I might die. I might die today, or tomorrow, or next week. The campaign is killing men every day, and the list is piling up fearfully, and I am twenty years old and my eyesight is failing and the bullets are coming in and the only thing between me and the ground is a God who saved me at Antietam and Gettysburg but did not save Henry.

*May we all meet in heaven.*

All. The word again — the same word he had used in the moonlight letter, the word that meant the full circle, the unbroken family, Henry included. If Alexander died in the trenches near Marietta, he would meet Henry in heaven. The theology was simple and the comfort was real and the letter ended with it, the last thought Alexander committed to paper before picking up his rifle and going back to the line.

One week later, the regiment would assault Kennesaw Mountain. Sherman, frustrated by Johnston's defense, would order a frontal attack on June 27, 1864. It would be one of the costliest mistakes of the campaign — three thousand Union casualties in a few hours, the lines barely advanced, the lesson relearned that had been learned at Fredericksburg and Cold Harbor and a dozen other killing fields: you cannot charge entrenched infantry across open ground and live.

Alexander survived it. The letter did not say how.

---

July and August were the grind.

The army pushed south — past Kennesaw Mountain, across the Chattahoochee River, to the outskirts of Atlanta. The fighting continued daily. The entrenchments continued nightly. The casualties continued to mount.

Alexander's health was deteriorating. The night blindness was getting worse — "as soon as it is night comes I am as blind as a bat" — and the scurvy was advancing, the slow poisoning of a body that had not eaten fresh fruit or vegetables in months. His gums bled. His joints ached. His energy was draining.

But he fought. He dug. He wrote.

General Hooker left the army in late July, and Alexander described the departure with the emotional precision of a man saying goodbye to someone he admired:

*The day he left he rode along the line bidding the men goodbye. A man from one of the Pennsylvania regts. of our brigade had just had his leg taken off by a cannon shot, was being carried off on a stretcher just at that time. "Good bye, General Hooker!" he sang out. It seemed to have moved the Gen'l. greatly. He got off his horse, took off his hat and went up and shook hands with the poor fellow, making some kind encouraging words at the same time.*

The scene was pure Alexander — the eye for detail, the ear for dialogue, the ability to find the human moment inside the machine of war. A man with his leg blown off, being carried on a stretcher, calling out to a departing general. The general dismounting, removing his hat, shaking the man's hand. The whole tableau framed by the ongoing battle, the cannon still firing, the stretcher-bearers still carrying.

*The 20th Corps will never forget old "Fighting Joe."*

Alexander did not forget. He remembered everything. He wrote it all down.

---

August 8, 1864. The letter to his mother that opened with her handwriting at the top — "My dearest child; Write me immediately" — and continued with Alexander's obedient response:

*Now, "ain't" I a good boy?*

He was twenty years old and dying of scurvy and the war was grinding him down and his eyesight was failing and the list of killed and wounded was piling up fearfully, and he wrote to his mother: *"Ain't" I a good boy?*

The playfulness was not denial. It was survival. Humor was the last defense, the thing you held onto when everything else had been stripped away — the health, the strength, the innocence, the brother. You held onto the ability to make your mother smile across a distance of a thousand miles, because if you could do that, you were still human, and if you were still human, you were still alive.

The letter closed with the scurvy forcing the issue. Alexander was sent to the hospital. The regiment marched on toward Atlanta without him.

He went blind at night. He lay in a hospital bed and listened to the sounds of a war he could no longer see.

He was twenty years old.

He felt fifty.
