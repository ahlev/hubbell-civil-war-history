## Chapter 17: The Order

---

*June 27, 1865*
*Near Alexandria, Va.*

*Dear Fannie:*

He wrote to his sister. Not to his mother — to Fannie. The choice was deliberate, as all of Alexander's choices about audience were deliberate. This was the letter of a brother to a sister, not a son to a mother, and the distinction mattered because the letter Alexander was about to write required a voice that was confident, eloquent, and free of the particular deference he always showed Frances. He needed to speak as a man, not as a child. And with Fannie, he had always been most fully himself.

*I advised in my last letter home that my next should be to you, so here goes.*

Here goes. The informality was its own kind of music — the tone of a man who is about to say something enormous and has chosen to say it lightly, the way you set down a heavy thing gently because the weight of it demands care.

*My dearest Sister, Fannie, I am rejoiced to inform you that last night the long wished for order came to muster the Sixtieth New York Veteran Volunteers out of the United States service immediately.*

The order. The order that every soldier in the Army had been waiting for since Appomattox, the order that meant the war was over not in theory but in practice, not for the generals and the politicians but for the men — the specific men, the men of the 60th New York, the men who had marched from Ogdensburgh in November 1861 and were still, incredibly, standing.

*Therefore just as soon as we can make out our papers and get transportation we shall come to Ogdensburgh, and there be discharged and paid off.*

Ogdensburgh. Camp Wheeler. The same place where it had begun. The symmetry was almost too perfect — the boy who had written "What a Bedlam!" in September 1861 would return to the same barracks in July 1865, and the return would complete a circle that encompassed the entire war.

*That's a happy day that will be, eh, Miss Fannie, agreed and a proud one for me.*

Happy and proud. The two emotions existed side by side, neither diminishing the other. The happiness was personal — he was going home, he was alive, he was free. The pride was something larger — he had served, he had endured, he had kept his oath. He had done what Henry had told him not to do, and he had done it well, and the country was whole because of men like him.

*God spare my life to see the time.*

Even now, even with the order in hand, even with the war over and the enemy surrendered and the nation at peace — even now Alexander could not quite believe he would survive to the end. The conditional was ingrained, the habit of a man who had learned at Antietam that survival was not guaranteed and at Gettysburg that it was not deserved and at Kennesaw Mountain that it was not even probable. *God spare my life.* The prayer of a soldier who knows that the last mile can kill you as surely as the first.

---

And then Alexander did something he had never done before. He stepped back from the personal and wrote about the nation.

*When I left home nearly four years since our country was racked from its base to its center by one of the most violent and gigantic rebellions recorded on the pages of history. We knew not friends from foes. Treason walked our legislative halls and had command of our armies.*

The prose was different from anything else in the collection — formal, measured, almost oratorical, the prose of a man who understood that this moment required more than the chatty intimacy of a letter home. Alexander was writing for history. He knew it. The words were chosen with a precision that his earlier letters, for all their vividness, had not attempted. *Racked from its base to its center.* *Treason walked our legislative halls.* These were not the words of a twenty-year-old farm boy. They were the words of a man who had been educated by four years of war and who had emerged from that education with a voice that could speak for his generation.

*Now by the providence of God, after four years of unparalleled struggling we stand a free and united nation, than which in point of honor we have no superior and in point of freedom, no equal.*

The sentence was the finest thing Alexander ever wrote. It was balanced, cadenced, deliberate — *in point of honor... no superior; in point of freedom... no equal* — and it carried the authority of a man who had purchased the right to say it with his body and his blood and his brother's life. He was not reciting patriotism. He was reporting a fact. He had been there. He had seen the racking and the struggling and the treason, and he had stood against them, and the nation had survived, and he could testify to its survival with the credibility of a witness who had nearly died in the proving.

*A free and united nation.*

Free. The word had changed meaning for Alexander since 1861. In 1862, he had hated the abolitionists. In 1864, he had voted for Lincoln. Now, in 1865, he wrote the word *free* and meant it in its fullest sense — free not just from rebellion, but from the institution that had caused the rebellion, free not just for white men but for all men, free in a way that the nation had never been before and that Alexander, at seventeen, had not understood was necessary.

The war had educated him. Henry had told him to go to college. The war was the college. And the valedictory — the graduation speech — was this letter, written in a camp near Alexandria, addressed to his sister, speaking to the nation.

---

The letter continued with practical matters — the corps being broken up, the officers receiving their farewells, the organizational details of demobilization that occupied the army's attention now that the shooting had stopped. Alexander mentioned General Greene and his son Chris — the son who had lost a leg to a cannon shot and was now serving on his father's staff, the two of them together in the army's final days as they had been together in its worst ones.

*Two of our Regt. bandaged his leg (it hung by a few muscles and these they were obliged to sever with a pocket knife) and carried him from the field.*

Alexander told the story matter-of-factly — the leg hanging by a few muscles, the pocket knife, the carrying — and the matter-of-factness was itself a measure of what four years of war had done to his threshold for horror. A leg severed with a pocket knife was a detail, not a crisis. It was a thing that happened, one of a thousand things that happened, and Alexander recorded it because it was part of the story and because the story mattered.

*He presented each of them afterward with a handsome Star with his and their names engraved on them.*

The star. The engraved names. The gesture of gratitude from a one-legged man to the two soldiers who had saved him. It was the kind of detail that Alexander always noticed — the human gesture inside the inhuman machinery — and it was the kind of detail that made his letters something more than correspondence. They were literature. They were history. They were the record of a man who saw clearly and wrote truly and never forgot that the war was made of people.

---

*The mail will leave soon — I must close.*

The letter was running out of space and time, the way all of Alexander's letters ran out of space and time, the mail clerk calling, the column forming, the eternal pressure of military schedules that did not pause for eloquence.

*Please give much love to Mother and Mrs. Hoff.*

Love to Mother. He sent it through Fannie, as if the message needed a courier, as if the love was a physical object that had to be carried from the letter to the kitchen table by his sister's hand. It was a small thing, but it was also the last love Alexander would send by mail. The next time he expressed his love for his mother, it would be in person.

*Please write soon. A letter will come from C. here in two days.*

C. — a friend, perhaps, or Charles. The initial was unexplained, as many things in Alexander's letters were unexplained, the shorthand of a life lived among people who did not need the explanations.

*Your aff. brother,*

*Alex F. Hubbell*

He signed it the way he had signed every letter since the second one — his name, clear and confident, the handwriting steady, the identity settled. He was Alexander F. Hubbell. Not F. A. Hubbell, as he had signed that first confused letter from Camp Wheeler. Not Private Hubbell or Corporal Hubbell or Sergeant Hubbell. Just Alex. The man who had gone to war and come back. The brother who was coming home.

*P.S. Please return to Mrs. Bostwick the enclosed letter. Culver was discharged a few days since, and is doubtless at home with his friends by this time.*

Even in his last official act of the war — the letter that announced his discharge — Alexander was doing favors. Returning a letter for Mrs. Bostwick. Noting that Culver was already home. The network of obligations and courtesies that connected the soldiers to their communities continued to function even in the war's final hours, the web of letters still holding the world together.

Alexander sealed the letter. He addressed it to Champlain. He gave it to the mail.

Then he began packing. For the last time.

---

The regiment moved north by train. The cars carried them through Virginia, through Maryland, through the landscape Alexander had marched across four years ago on his way south. The orchards were in full summer leaf. The fields were green. The stone walls ran along the roads, unchanged, as if the war had not happened, as if two hundred thousand men had not died between the stone walls of Champlain and the stone walls of Georgia.

Alexander watched it all from the window of the train, and this time the watching was different. He was not marching toward something. He was returning from something. The landscape was not new but familiar, not strange but remembered, the same hills and rivers and farm fields he had seen in November 1861, only now he was seeing them from the other direction, and the difference between going and coming was the difference between question and answer, between departure and arrival, between the boy and the man.

The train carried him north. Past Baltimore, where the bullet holes in the depot had been patched. Past Philadelphia, where the Quaker City had fed him breakfast. Past New York, where they had given him a flag and a horse and a beautiful Enfield rifle.

North. Toward Ogdensburgh. Toward Camp Wheeler. Toward home.
