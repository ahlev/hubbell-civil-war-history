## Chapter 10: Where, Where Is the Dear Boy?

---

Frances's investigation into Henry's fate was conducted with the precision of a forensic inquiry and the desperation of a woman who could not accept that her firstborn son had simply vanished from the earth.

She wrote to Colonel Suffen of the 34th New York. She wrote to officers of Company D. She interviewed every soldier who returned to Clinton County from the Army of the Potomac. She cross-referenced reports, catalogued contradictions, and built a dossier of evidence that grew thicker and less hopeful with each passing week.

The reports came in like dispatches from a battle she was fighting alone at the kitchen table:

Young Fiser said Henry was seen to fall. The rebels were within two rods. The line broke and the men fled and no one saw what happened after.

DaValls, detailed to examine the field, reported that Henry Hubbell was missing. Not killed — not confirmed killed — just missing. The distinction was everything. Missing meant possible. Missing meant alive somewhere, wounded, captured, hidden, lost in the chaos. Missing meant hope.

But missing also meant the worst. Missing meant a body that could not be identified. Missing meant the bushes and the mass graves and the anonymous dead who were buried without names by men who did not know them.

Frances understood this. She understood it with the particular clarity of a woman who had read every newspaper account of Antietam, who had studied the sketches of the battlefield, who had traced the positions of the armies with her finger and calculated the distances and imagined her son's last minutes with a vividness that was both her gift and her curse.

*O, how my heart bleeds — and bleeds.*

She wrote that to Alexander on October 2, and the repetition — *bleeds and bleeds* — was not rhetorical. It was physiological. The grief was a wound, and like a wound it did not close but bled continuously, draining her of something she could not name and could not replenish.

*Where, where is the dear, dear boy? Echo answers — where?*

The echo. Frances heard it in the empty rooms of the farmhouse, in the silence between letters, in the morning hours when she sat at the table with the newspaper and ran her finger down the lists. The echo was Henry's absence made audible — not a sound but the negative of a sound, the place where his voice should have been and was not.

---

October ground on. The leaves turned and fell. The farm demanded its routines, indifferent to grief. Frances milked the cows, mended the fences, managed the accounts, wrote letters. She wrote to Alexander constantly, the letters growing longer and more urgent as the silence from the 34th New York deepened.

*Write, write, write, write, write, write, write, WRITE.*

She wrote that on October 6, in a letter to Alexander, and the eight repetitions — each one a separate heartbeat, each one louder than the last — were the closest Frances ever came to screaming on paper. She needed letters the way a drowning person needs air. Letters were proof of life. Every day without a letter was a day of not-knowing, and not-knowing was worse than bad news because bad news, at least, was finite.

*I have shed an ocean of tears over dear Henry's fate — I know not what it is — but there is something.*

There is something. The mother's instinct, the thing that cannot be quantified or argued with, the bone-deep knowledge that something has gone terribly wrong. Frances did not say Henry was dead. She could not bring herself to say it. But she knew — had known, perhaps, since the first conflicting reports began arriving — that the something she felt was the specific weight of a son who would never come home.

She wrote the instructions that Alexander would carry like a second Bible:

*Read the 91st Psalm daily — and live up to its promises. The Saviour is near, near you — and can hear the heart's faintest sigh. In the din of battle no artillery can drown that sigh if it is breathed in faith.*

The sentence was magnificent and terrible — magnificent in its faith, terrible in its context. Frances was writing to a son she still had about the God who had, by any earthly reckoning, failed to protect the son she had lost. The 91st Psalm promised: *A thousand shall fall at thy side, and ten thousand at thy right hand; but it shall not come nigh thee.* Henry had been at the right hand. It had come nigh him. The Psalm, taken literally, had not held.

But Frances did not take the Psalm literally. She took it faithfully, which was different. The promise was not that her sons would not die. The promise was that God was present — present in the din of battle, present in the hospital, present in the farmhouse kitchen at four in the morning when the darkness pressed in and the grief was unbearable. God heard the heart's faintest sigh. Whether He answered it in the way the heart wanted was His business, not hers.

This theology — fierce, Scottish, uncompromising — was the rock on which Frances built her survival. It did not explain Henry's death. It did not make it bearable. It made it endurable, which was all she asked.

---

Late October. B. Misner came home from the prisoner exchange.

He had been in Richmond. He had seen all the Union prisoners taken at Antietam. He had looked for Henry among them.

Henry was not there.

Frances wrote the news to Alexander on October 29:

*I see B. Misner this morning and he has dashed any hopes with regard to Henry. He says that he and Albert are not incarcerated at all, and that they saw all the prisoners taken at Antietam, and Henry was not among them.*

Dashed. The word was precise and devastating. Not "diminished" or "reduced" but "dashed" — destroyed in a single stroke, the way a wave destroys a sandcastle.

Henry was not a prisoner. Henry was not in a hospital. Henry was not lost in the confusion. Henry was dead, and his body was somewhere in the earth of Maryland, and Frances would never find it, and there would never be a grave, and the last sentence of his last letter — *I do not intend to run into the fire before I can see my w—* — would remain forever unfinished.

*O, I fear that he was one of the "unknown" buried.*

Frances wrote it flatly, without drama, the grief compressed into fourteen words that said everything. She feared. She did not know. She would never know. The uncertainty was a wound that could not heal because it could not close — there was no body, no grave, no definitive word, only the accumulating evidence of absence and the echo that answered every question with silence.

But in the next breath, she refused to surrender:

*But still I cannot give him up. I must hope against his end that he may be a prisoner.*

Hope against his end. The phrase was a fortress built on sand, and Frances knew it, and she built it anyway, because the alternative — the acceptance, the finality, the past tense — was more than she could bear. She would continue hoping for months, perhaps years. The hope would thin and pale but it would not die entirely, because killing hope required certainty, and certainty was the one thing the war had not provided.

---

In the same letter, Frances did something that revealed the depth of her character more completely than any passage about grief:

*I congratulate you on your promotion. It is very gratifying to me to see that you merit the rank of Sergeant, for I take it for granted that you could not have it, did you not deserve it.*

Alexander had been promoted. In the midst of the worst month of Frances's life — her eldest son missing, her second son in a hospital, her third and fourth sons about to ship out — she found the capacity to be proud. To congratulate. To tell her surviving soldier son that his accomplishment mattered, that his merit was recognized, that the mother who was drowning in grief was still watching, still caring, still measuring his progress with the same exacting attention she had always brought to her children's lives.

*Dear, dear Henry was never dealt justly with.*

The sentence was a knife. Henry had been a good soldier — reliable, brave, competent — and had never been promoted. Alexander, younger and less experienced, was already a sergeant. The injustice was not Alexander's fault, but it sharpened Frances's grief with a specific, bitter edge: her dead son had been overlooked, and her living son had been recognized, and the disparity made Henry's death not only tragic but unfair.

It was the closest Frances ever came to questioning the war itself — not the cause, which she believed in absolutely, but the mechanism, the bureaucracy of armies that promoted some men and ignored others and killed the best of them without regard for merit or justice or the mothers who waited at home.

---

November brought a change.

Alexander, recovered from the hospital, back with his regiment, sat down to write the letter his mother had been waiting for. Not the letter about Antietam — he would never write that letter. But the letter about Henry. The letter about the moonlight.

*Nov. 19th, 1862*
*Harpers Ferry*

*My Dear Mother & Sister:*

*How I long to see you.*

He wrote it in the present tense, the way Frances wrote about Henry — *how I long,* not *how I longed* — and the tense was deliberate, an assertion of continuity, a refusal to let the distance become the past.

*Dear, dear, Henry how I long to see him.*

Present tense again. *How I long to see him.* Not *longed.* Not past. Alexander was still hoping, three months after the battle, that Henry was alive. The intelligence from Misner had not reached him, or had reached him and he had not believed it, or had reached him and he had chosen to write as if it had not.

*And shall we all — all — meet again. God grant it.*

The double *all.* The first was inclusive — shall we all meet again? The second was emphatic — *all,* meaning every one, meaning Henry too, meaning the unbroken circle, the family complete. Alexander was praying on paper, and the prayer was for the impossible: that the war would end and all five of Frances's children would return to the kitchen table and nothing would have been lost.

He knew it was impossible. He wrote it anyway. Because hope, for Alexander as for Frances, was not a prediction. It was a practice.

---

And then he told the story.

The moonlight. The camp. The search. The question: *"Is there a man among you by the name of Hubbell?"* The large bearded man stepping from the tent. The hands flying together. The laughter, the surprise, the night of conversation.

*"Why Dick, is that you — really you."*

He told it all, in a passage of writing so vivid and so heartfelt that 160 years later it still reads like something you are witnessing rather than something you are being told about. He told it because his mother had asked, and because telling it was a way of keeping Henry alive — not in the world, where Henry was almost certainly dead, but in the family's shared memory, where he would live as long as someone remembered the moonlight and the hands and the voice that said *"Why Dick, is that you."*

The letter was a monument. Not made of marble, like the monuments that would later be erected at Antietam, but of ink and paper and a son's love for a brother he would never see again. It was the most important letter Alexander ever wrote, and he wrote it from a hospital where he had begged for five dollars and eaten bread bought with another man's money, and the contrast — the poverty of his circumstances and the richness of his prose — was the most Alexander thing in the entire collection.

He sealed the letter. He addressed it to Champlain. He sent it north.

In Champlain, Frances would read it and weep — for the beauty of the reunion and for the finality of its loss — and then she would file it with the others, in the bundle that was growing thicker month by month, the paper archive of a family at war that someone, someday, would find and read and understand.

---

*The year 1862 ended in cold and grief. Alexander was back with his regiment, marching again, the diarrhea in remission but not cured, the bruise on his knee long since healed, the bruise on his soul permanent. Frances was at the kitchen table, the casualty lists still appearing every morning, the letters still arriving every week, the empty chair still empty.*

*Henry was gone. The echo answered where.*

*But three Hubbell sons remained. Alexander in Virginia. Charles and James in training with the 153rd New York, about to ship out. The war was not over. The letters were not over. The dying was not over.*

*Christmas came. Alexander's second at war, Henry's first in the ground. Frances set the table and left the chair empty and opened her Bible and read the Psalm and closed her eyes and prayed the prayer that could not be finished:*

*Keep them. Keep them all. Bring them home.*

*And if you cannot bring them all —*

*She could finish it now. The war had given her the ending.*

*And if you cannot bring them all, keep the ones who remain.*

*She opened her eyes. She ate her Christmas dinner. She cleared the plates. She sat down to write.*

*She had three more sons to keep alive.*
