## Chapter 9: The Hospital

---

The letter was the hardest he had ever written.

Not the longest — that honor belonged to the Harpers Ferry letters, with their catalogues of marches and distances. Not the most emotional — the moonlight reunion letter, still months away from being committed to paper, would claim that distinction. But the hardest, because this letter required Alexander to do the thing he had been raised never to do, the thing that every fiber of his Hubbell pride rebelled against.

He asked his mother for money.

*Sept. 27, 1862*
*Hospital at Harpers Ferry*

*My Dear Mother —*

*I suppose you will be both surprised and grieved to hear that I am in a hospital.*

He had collapsed. The "exhausting diarrhea" — the Army's most common and least romantic ailment, a disease that killed more soldiers than Confederate bullets, a disease that stripped a man's dignity along with his strength — had been working on him for weeks, draining him while he marched and fought and stood guard and searched for his brother. The combination of dysentery and combat had done what neither could do alone: it had put him flat on his back in a hospital bed, too weak to stand, too poor to buy food, too proud to say so.

But he said so. Because he had to. Because the hospital was not a place where a man without money could survive.

*I feel much better today and think that with proper care and nourishment I shall get along finely.*

The optimism was reflexive — a soldier's first duty in a letter home was reassurance — but it was also genuine. Alexander was tough. He had survived Antietam, survived the march, survived the disease long enough to reach the hospital. He would survive this too, if he could eat.

The problem was food. The hospital fed its patients, after a fashion — bread and broth and whatever the cooks could stretch from the Army's rations — but hospital food in 1862 was not designed for recovery. It was designed for subsistence, the bare minimum of calories needed to keep a body alive, and for a man with chronic diarrhea, whose gut could absorb almost nothing, subsistence was not enough.

What Alexander needed was bread. Real bread, bought from a vendor or a sutler, substantial enough to give his ruined digestive system something to work with. And bread cost money, and Alexander had no money.

Enter Orderly Dickinson.

*Orderly Dickinson was down here this morning and purchased some paper and some envelopes and a loaf of bread for me (with his own money). He is very, very, very kind to me. That bread will do me a world of good.*

Three *very*s. Alexander, who was precise in his language, who did not waste words, who understood the economy of adjectives the way a farmer understands the economy of seed — Alexander used three *very*s to describe Dickinson's kindness. The repetition was a measure of the gratitude, which was itself a measure of the need.

*I wish you could by some means or token express my gratitude to him. I can never forget his kindness and only hope I may find some means of repaying it. He showed himself every inch a man in the last fight.*

Dickinson was a good man. He had proven it at Antietam ("every inch a man in the last fight") and he was proving it again now, in the hospital, by spending his own money to buy bread and paper for a sick corporal who could not buy them himself. The gesture was small — a loaf of bread, some envelopes — and the gesture was enormous, because in a world where bread was survival and paper was connection, Dickinson had given Alexander both.

---

Then came the paragraph that Frances would have read with a pain beyond anything the casualty lists could inflict:

*I am now, Dear Mother, going to ask a great favor, and it is only the stearnest necessity that makes me do it.*

The preamble was as formal as a legal document. Alexander was marshaling his courage, building toward the request with the slow deliberation of a man approaching a cliff.

*Can you send me some money.*

The sentence did not end with a question mark. It was not a question. It was an admission — of need, of helplessness, of the failure to provide for himself that a Hubbell son was never supposed to make. Alexander had enlisted to serve his country and to support his mother. He had sent her fifteen dollars from his first paycheck and ordered her to buy a warm cloak. He had been the provider, the protector, the son who converted his soldier's wages into love and mailed it home.

Now he was asking for it back.

*You know, Mother, what it is to be away from home, sick among strangers and to be without money. I will not try to describe my feelings when I ask for this. You know them all.*

"I will not try to describe my feelings." Alexander, the most descriptive letter-writer in the family, the man who could make you see a moonlit camp and feel the bayonet at your belly and hear the shells over Harpers Ferry — Alexander could not describe what he felt. The words were not equal to the shame. He knew Frances would understand, because Frances had been poor and alone and far from home — she had emigrated from Scotland, had married and been widowed, had managed a farm on no income — and the shared experience of vulnerability was the bridge he used to cross the impossible distance between asking and receiving.

*We shall have four months pay due us the first of Nov. ($52) and I shall be able to return it to you ten fold.*

He was already promising to pay it back. The request was barely out of his mouth and he was already converting it into a transaction, a loan rather than a gift, because a loan preserved the fiction that he was still the provider and she was still the provided-for.

*Do you suppose you could spare me five dollars, if not, less will do.*

Five dollars. The amount was almost comically small — less than a third of the fifteen he had sent her from Camp Jackson, a fraction of the fifty-two that was coming in November. But five dollars was a fortune in a hospital in 1862, where bread cost a dime and a man with a dime could eat and a man without one could not.

*I will leave you to judge how painful it is for me to ask this of you. If you have not got it to spare do not send it. I do not wish you to deny yourself.*

The final sentence was the cruelest kindness: *I do not wish you to deny yourself.* He knew, as well as he knew anything, that Frances would deny herself anything to send her sick son money. She would sell the cow, mortgage the farm, beg from the neighbors before she would let Alexander go hungry in a hospital. And he knew that telling her not to deny herself was futile, as futile as telling a mother not to worry, but he said it anyway because the saying was part of the ritual of asking, the verbal genuflection that made the request bearable for both of them.

---

In the middle of the letter, between the bread and the money, Alexander dropped a sentence that was either the most hopeful or the most heartbreaking in the entire collection, depending on what you knew.

*I have heard from Henry. He is slightly wounded in the hand, but is still with the company.*

This was wrong.

Henry was not slightly wounded in the hand. Henry was not still with his company. Henry was dead. He had been dead for ten days. His body lay somewhere on the Antietam battlefield — in the bushes, in a mass grave, in the anonymous earth of a Maryland farm — and the report that Alexander had received was a ghost, a rumor, a whisper passed from man to man through the fog of war until it had acquired the shape of truth without the substance.

Alexander believed it. He had no reason not to. The information came from a soldier in the 34th, a man who presumably knew what he was talking about, and it was exactly the kind of news Alexander wanted to hear — Henry alive, Henry wounded but safe, Henry with his company, Henry coming home.

*When I am able I will try and give you some of the particulars of the fight.*

The promise was never kept. Alexander never wrote a detailed account of Antietam. The "particulars of the fight" remained locked inside him, shared perhaps at the tea table four years later, or perhaps never shared at all, carried to his grave in 1894 along with the other things the letters could not say.

---

Frances received the letter in Champlain and did two things simultaneously: she sent money, and she continued her investigation.

By the time Alexander's letter arrived, Frances already knew more than he did. She had been writing to officers, interviewing returning soldiers, cross-referencing reports. She knew that Henry was on the casualty list as "wounded." She knew that the reports were contradictory. She knew, in the deepest part of her gut, that the news was going to be bad.

But Alexander's letter — *"I have heard from Henry. He is slightly wounded in the hand"* — must have given her a moment of hope. A single bright flare in the darkness. Her sick son, lying in a hospital, writing to tell her that her other son was alive.

She would learn the truth soon enough. B. Misner would return from Richmond and destroy the last hope in a single conversation. Frances's own detective work would assemble the evidence into a verdict that no mother should have to render.

But for a few days — the days between receiving Alexander's letter and receiving Misner's report — Frances may have allowed herself to believe that both her sons had survived the bloodiest day in American history.

It was the last such mercy the war would offer her.

---

Alexander recovered slowly. The hospital at Harpers Ferry was not a place conducive to recovery — overcrowded, undersupplied, haunted by the particular smell of men whose bodies were failing them in every way that bodies can fail. But Alexander was young, and his constitution was strong, and the bread that Dickinson had bought him with his own money made a difference, and gradually the diarrhea subsided and his strength returned.

He was promoted to sergeant during his convalescence. The news arrived in a letter from the regiment, and Alexander recorded it with a mixture of pride and sorrow that was becoming his default emotional setting.

*My Dear Mother & Sister,* he wrote on November 19, back at Harpers Ferry but out of the hospital. And then: *How I long to see you.*

The letter that followed was the longest and most emotional of the war — the moonlight letter, the letter where Alexander finally told the story of finding Henry's camp, of the reunion, of the hands that "flew together," of the laughter and the surprise and the brother who stepped out of the tent like a man stepping out of a dream.

But that story — the story of the last meeting — was not about Antietam. It was about before. It was about the moonlight and the search and the whispered question: *"Is there a man among you by the name of Hubbell?"*

The story of Antietam itself remained untold.

Alexander could tell the story of finding his brother. He could not tell the story of losing him.

---

The five dollars arrived. Frances sent it — of course she sent it. She would have sent fifty if she'd had it, five hundred, the entire farm converted to currency and mailed south in an envelope addressed to her darling pet.

Alexander received it and bought food and recovered and rejoined his regiment and continued the war.

He would send her fifty-two dollars in November, as promised. He would send money every time he was paid, for the rest of the war, converting his soldier's wages into the sustenance of the farm in Champlain. The five-dollar debt was repaid many times over.

But the debt that mattered — the emotional debt, the debt of having needed and asked and received — was never fully discharged. Alexander carried it the way he carried his letters: always, everywhere, a weight that was not a burden but a bond.

*You know, Mother, what it is to be away from home, sick among strangers and to be without money. I will not try to describe my feelings when I ask for this. You know them all.*

She did know them all. She was Frances McNeil Hubbell, and she knew every feeling her son could not describe, because she had felt them all herself — the loneliness, the helplessness, the shame of needing, the fear that the need would outlast the help.

She sent the money. She prayed the Psalm. She read the casualty lists.

And she continued, day by day, letter by letter, to search for Henry.
