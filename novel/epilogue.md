## Epilogue: What the Letters Don't Say

---

James Hubbell died on October 19, 1865.

The date was exact — one year to the day after the Battle of Cedar Creek, where he had been wounded. He had walked five miles after being shot, walking to avoid capture, the flesh wound leaking blood into his uniform as he stumbled through the Virginia countryside. They said the wound was not serious. They were wrong.

He died at Albany Hospital. The war had been over for six months. His brothers had come home, his mother had welcomed them, the tea table had been set and the stories had been told. And then James, the thin one, the scholar, the quiet observer who had written only nine letters in the entire collection — James died at twenty-two, in a hospital bed, of a wound that should not have been fatal but was.

Frances buried her second son.

She did not record her feelings about it. No letter survived from this period — if she wrote one, it was lost, or destroyed, or simply too painful to preserve. The woman who had documented everything, who had investigated Henry's death with the precision of a detective and mourned him with the eloquence of a poet, left no written record of James's death.

Perhaps there was nothing left to write. Perhaps the first grief had used all the words, and the second grief found the well dry.

---

Alexander and Charles moved to Iowa.

The West was calling. The veterans of the Civil War, restless and damaged and unable to settle back into the lives they had left, poured westward by the tens of thousands — to Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, the territories — seeking the new start that the old places could not provide. Alexander and Charles went together, the last two Hubbell brothers, carrying their Army discharge papers and their mother's Bible and the skills they had learned on the farm in Champlain and in the trenches of Virginia and Georgia.

Alexander settled in Fonda, Pocahontas County. He married Lois Wood in 1867 — two years after the war, at the age of twenty-three. They had four children. He farmed. He was, by all accounts, a good citizen of his community — a man who attended church and paid his taxes and served when asked, the habits of duty and responsibility that Frances had instilled in him and the war had hardened into permanence.

He did not talk about the war. Or if he did, the conversations were not recorded. The 120 letters were his testimony, and they said everything he had to say, and the rest — the things that the letters could not hold, the things he had told at the tea table — belonged to the silence that follows the telling.

---

Charles Hubbell died in 1875.

He was thirty-four years old. The cause was recorded as disease contracted during service — the slow, delayed-action killing that the war inflicted on hundreds of thousands of men who survived the battles but could not survive the damage the battles had done to their bodies. Charles had written sixty-nine letters during the war, practical and matter-of-fact, the letters of a man who "did not enlist with the expectation of sleeping on a feather bed." He had endured. He had survived. And then he had died, ten years later, the war catching up to him the way it caught up to so many, the debt collected long after the loan was made.

Frances buried her third son.

Three of her four soldier sons were now in the ground. Henry at Antietam, body never found. James at Albany Hospital. Charles in Iowa, killed by a war that had ended a decade ago.

Only Alexander remained.

---

Frances McNeil Hubbell lived until 1890. She was approximately eighty-two years old.

She outlived three of her four sons who had gone to war. She outlived her husband by thirty-seven years. She had managed the farm, raised the children, written the letters, read the casualty lists, prayed the Psalm, searched for Henry, buried James, buried Charles, and held the family together through the worst four years in American history.

She had kept her promise to Frederick. *Keep them together.* She had kept them together — not whole, not unharmed, not all alive, but together. The letters were the ligaments. The faith was the bone. The love was the blood.

When she died, someone saved the letters. Someone — Fannie, perhaps, the eldest child, the teacher, the one who had written the verse in Alexander's Bible — gathered the 274 pieces of paper and bundled them together and stored them. They passed from hand to hand, generation to generation, the paper yellowing, the ink fading, but the words remaining, clear and legible and alive.

---

Alexander F. Hubbell died on December 7, 1894.

He was fifty years old. He had been a soldier for three years and ten months, a civilian for twenty-nine years, a husband for twenty-seven years, a father of four. He had survived Antietam and Gettysburg and the Atlanta Campaign and the hospitals at Harpers Ferry and Chattanooga. He had survived the loss of three brothers, the scurvy, the night blindness, the blues. He had survived the war.

But the war had not survived him. It lived in him, in the scars that were visible and the ones that were not, in the nightmares that no letter recorded and the memories that no tea table could exhaust. He was fifty years old, and he had felt fifty when he was twenty, and the intervening thirty years had been a long, slow confirmation of the feeling.

He had written, at twenty: *I sometimes feel as though I was about 50 years old.*

He was fifty when he died. The feeling had been prophecy.

---

The letters survived.

They survived because someone understood that they were not just personal correspondence but the record of something larger — a family's war, a nation's war, the cost of union measured in the particular currency of human lives.

274 letters. Five voices. Four years. Three dead sons. Two empty chairs at the tea table. One mother who kept them together.

And one boy — seventeen years old, standing in a barracks in Ogdensburgh with the noise of seven hundred men around him and a pen in his hand and a sheet of paper on his knee — who wrote:

*My Dear Mother —*

*Here I am safe and sound in the barracks. How strange it does seem. Ah! what a bedlam!*

And then, four years later, the same boy — older now, a man, a sergeant, a veteran of the worst that the world could do — wrote seven lines on borrowed paper and closed with a promise:

*I will tell you all about it at the tea table.*

He told them.

We have his letters.

We know, as well as anyone can know through ink and paper and the distance of a century and a half, what he told them.

But we were not at the tea table. We do not know what his mother's face looked like when he walked through the door. We do not know what Luther said. We do not know whether the tea grew cold or was reheated, whether the stories lasted an hour or a night or a week, whether Alexander cried or laughed or did both at once.

We know the letters. The rest — the touch, the breath, the silence between sentences, the living warmth of a family reunited at a table where one chair would always be empty — the rest belongs to them.

---

*Somewhere in Iowa, in a farmhouse that no longer stands, in a county that Alexander chose because it was flat and open and nothing like the mountains of Georgia, the letters were kept. Bundled in ribbon or string, stored in a trunk or a drawer, carried from house to house by the descendants of a family that had given four sons to the war and gotten one back.*

*The letters were not opened for a long time. When they were opened, the words were still clear — the ink had faded but not disappeared, the paper had yellowed but not crumbled, the voices of the dead were still audible to anyone willing to listen.*

*And the voices said: We were here. We fought. We wrote. We loved. We lost. We endured. We came home, those of us who could. We told our stories at the tea table, and the stories were larger than any of us, and they survived us, and they survive us still.*

*This is their story.*

*This is what they wrote.*

*This is what they didn't say.*

---
