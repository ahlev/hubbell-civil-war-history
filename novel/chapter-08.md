# PART THREE: THE SHATTERING

---

## Chapter 8: Antietam

---

September 17, 1862.

The bloodiest single day in American history began, for Alexander, with a sound like the world tearing apart.

The guns opened before dawn — Union batteries on the hills north of the Antietam Creek, Confederate batteries on the hills south of it, the shells crossing in the air like the laces of a shoe being pulled tight. The noise was not the sharp, contained thunder of Harpers Ferry. It was something vaster, a continuous roar that obliterated thought and replaced it with sensation: the shaking of the ground, the pressure waves that hit the chest like fists, the acrid fog of gun smoke that rolled across the fields and turned the morning sun into a dim red coin.

Alexander's regiment — the 60th New York — went in two hundred strong.

Two hundred men. A regiment that had mustered nearly a thousand at Camp Wheeler was now less than a quarter of its original strength, the rest having been lost to disease, discharge, desertion, detachment, and the slow attrition that ground down every unit in the Army of the Potomac. The two hundred who remained were the core — the men who had survived the railroad duty, the Valley marches, the shells at Harpers Ferry. They were the ones who had stayed, and now they were walking into the worst of it.

Alexander walked with them.

---

He would never write a detailed account of the battle. This was unusual for him — a man who documented everything, who catalogued distances and temperatures and the quality of the rations — and the absence of detail spoke louder than any description could have. What happened at Antietam was beyond his capacity to narrate, at least in the immediate aftermath. The experience overloaded the instrument.

What he wrote, two days later, from a camp on the battlefield, was this:

*My Dear Mother —*

*I presume you will be greatly rejoiced to hear that I came out of the battle of the third safe and sound.*

"The battle of the third" — he was not even sure of the date. The fighting had blurred time, the way intense pain blurs everything, compressing hours into moments and stretching moments into eternities. He had been in the battle, he had come out of it, and the space between those two facts was a darkness that his pen could not enter.

*I cannot be thankful enough for God's great kindness to me.*

The faith held. In the aftermath of the worst violence he had ever experienced — the worst violence most of those two hundred men had ever experienced — Alexander's first instinct was gratitude. Not horror, not rage, not the numb dissociation that would come later. Gratitude. God had kept him alive. The Psalm had held.

*The only injury I received was an inconsiderable bruise a little below the right knee by a ball nearly spent.*

An "inconsiderable bruise." A ball — a Minié ball, a .58 caliber lead slug traveling at nine hundred feet per second — had struck him below the knee. At full velocity, it would have shattered the bone, amputated the leg, or killed him. But it was "nearly spent," which meant it had reached the end of its arc, had lost most of its energy to air and distance, and struck him with enough force to leave a bruise but not enough to break the skin. The difference between "nearly spent" and "not quite spent" was the difference between a bruise and a coffin.

Alexander called it inconsiderable.

He was eighteen years old.

---

Then the sentence that would define the rest of the war:

*I only wish I could report the same of Henry.*

Six words. The most important six words in 273 letters. Everything that came before them was prologue. Everything that came after was consequence.

Henry was missing.

*I saw one of the 34th this morning and he said that he is missing, also that he thought he was quite sure that he was not killed. Thinks he is either a prisoner or missed the Regiment in the confusion (something that very often happens.)*

Alexander was bargaining. The letter was a exercise in hope construction, each sentence building a case for Henry's survival with the desperate logic of a man who cannot accept the alternative. Missing, not killed. Probably a prisoner. Maybe lost in the confusion. Many men reported missing had come in. "It is very probable he left the field to assist in carrying off some of his wounded comrades."

*I have strong hopes concerning him and think he will turn up alright. Don't hardly think he was hurt.*

Don't hardly think. The double negative betrayed what the words denied. Alexander was not sure. He was not sure at all. But he could not write to his mother — to the woman who read the casualty lists every morning before breakfast — and tell her that her eldest son was probably dead. He could not do that to her from three hundred miles away, in a letter that would take days to arrive, a letter she would open alone at the kitchen table.

So he wrote hope instead. He constructed a narrative of survival — Henry the prisoner, Henry the good Samaritan carrying wounded comrades, Henry the confused soldier separated from his regiment in the chaos — and he sent it north like a prayer, knowing it might be true and fearing it was not.

Two days later, a postscript:

*Camp below Maryland Heights, Sept. 21st. I have not heard from Henry since writing the above. I hope and think he is yet all right. Do not worry.*

*Do not worry.* The most futile sentence in the English language, written by a son to his mother, about the possible death of another son. Frances would read it, and she would worry, and the worry would be justified, and the hope that Alexander had so carefully constructed would collapse over the next six weeks like a building with its foundations removed, one report at a time.

---

At Champlain, the news arrived in stages.

First the newspapers, with their casualty lists that Frances had been reading every morning since July 1861. The 34th New York had been "terribly cut up." Company D had "suffered severely." But Henry's name was not on the killed list. He was not on the wounded list either, at first — and then he was. "Wounded." The specifics were unclear.

Frances wrote immediately — not to Alexander, but to Henry's regiment. She wrote to officers, to enlisted men, to anyone whose address she could find. She conducted an investigation with the systematic intensity of a detective and the raw desperation of a mother.

The reports came back contradictory.

One man said Henry was wounded in the hand. Another said he was wounded in the hip. Another said he was missing. Another said he was probably a prisoner. DaValls, detailed to examine the field where Company D had fought, reported that "Henry Hubbell was missing." Everyone agreed he had been wounded. No one could say what happened after.

*Where, where is the dear, dear boy? Echo answers — where?*

Frances wrote that on October 2, two weeks after the battle, and the sentence was the rawest thing she ever committed to paper — the punctuation of grief, the echo that comes back empty, the mother's question that has no answer because the son who should answer it is lying somewhere on a Maryland field, or in a Confederate hospital, or in a shallow grave dug by men who did not know his name.

---

The truth, when it finally emerged, was the worst of all possible truths: Henry was dead, his body was never found, and no one could say with certainty how he had died.

The evidence accumulated like sediment. A soldier had seen Henry "grasp his leg, take a step or two, and throw himself down." The rebels had been within two rods — thirty-three feet. The Union line broke. The men fled. No one had time to carry the wounded. Henry was left on the field, wounded, with the Confederate advance rolling over him.

B. Misner, returning from prisoner exchange in late October, delivered the final blow. He had been in Richmond. He had seen all the prisoners taken at Antietam. Henry was not among them.

Henry was not killed — at least, not on the list.
Henry was not a prisoner.
Henry was not in any hospital, Union or Confederate.
Henry had simply disappeared.

The most likely explanation — the one that Frances arrived at through her detective work, the one she expressed in her letter of October 29 — was that Henry had been wounded, had crawled or dragged himself into the bushes looking for shelter, had died there alone, and had been buried as one of the "unknown" — the hundreds of men on both sides whose bodies were found days or weeks later, too decomposed to identify, and interred in mass graves without names.

*O, I fear that he was one of the "unknown" buried, or one of the number found in the bushes, who had died of their wounds and could not be identified.*

Frances wrote it with a steadiness that must have cost her everything she had. The handwriting did not waver. The grammar did not break. She was a woman who had lost her son, who would never have a grave to visit, who would never know the exact hour or manner of his death, and she documented her loss with the same precision she brought to her ledger books.

But the next sentence cracked:

*My darling, darling Henry. How I long to write him, & receive letters from him.*

Not past tense. Present. She longed to write him, not she had longed. The grammar of grief refused to accept the past tense because the past tense meant finality and finality was unbearable.

---

Alexander learned the truth in stages too.

His September 19 letter — the Antietam letter, the one built on hope — gave way to weeks of silence. The silence was itself a kind of message. Alexander, who wrote constantly, who could not let a week pass without putting pen to paper, went quiet. The next surviving letter was dated November 12, nearly two months later, and it was written from a hospital.

He had collapsed. The "exhausting diarrhea" that had plagued him for weeks, combined with the physical toll of Antietam and the emotional toll of Henry's disappearance, had broken him. He was in the hospital at Harpers Ferry, too weak to stand, too poor to buy food, writing the letter that would become the emotional center of the entire collection.

But that letter — the five-dollar letter — belonged to the next chapter. What belonged to this chapter was the battle itself, and its aftermath, and the particular desolation of standing on a field where your brother may have died and not finding him.

Because Alexander did look. The letters made that clear. He went to the camp of the 34th New York. He talked to the men of Company D. He gathered the conflicting reports and tried to reconcile them. He searched, in whatever way a soldier can search a battlefield that has already been cleaned by burial details, for evidence of his brother's fate.

He found nothing.

Henry Hubbell, age twenty-three, of Champlain, Clinton County, New York, had entered the battle of Antietam on September 17, 1862, and had not come out. He had gone in as a man — as a brother, a son, a soldier who worried about money and misspelled *useful* and wanted to be home in time for sugar season — and had come out as a statistic, a name on a list, one of the 22,717 casualties of the bloodiest day in American history.

His last letter, written seven weeks before the battle, had ended with a characteristically practical observation about the sutler's prices and a truncated sentence that the transcriber could not fully decipher:

*...I do not intend to run into the fire before I can see my w—*

The sentence broke off. The page ended. The letter was his last.

What word had he been writing? *Wife?* Henry was unmarried. *Way?* Possible — he would not run into the fire before he could see his way. *Worth?* Before he could see his worth?

The transcribers could not say. The ink was faded, the word incomplete, the paper cut at the margin. It was one more thing the war had taken: the end of Henry's last sentence, the word he was reaching for when the page ran out.

---

Alexander marched off the field at Antietam with a bruise on his knee and a hole in his life where his brother had been.

He was eighteen years old.

He would carry the bruise for a week. He would carry the hole forever.

---

*Our Regiment went into the fight about 200 strong.*

He wrote that in the September 19 letter, the first letter after the battle, and it was the only sentence in the entire collection where Alexander quantified his regiment's strength. He was not, generally, a man of numbers — he left that to Henry, the practical one, the one who tracked dollars and cents the way other men tracked miles. But this number mattered. Two hundred. Two hundred men had walked into the cornfields and the sunken road and the smoke and the noise and the dying, and some of them had not walked out, and Alexander had been one of the lucky ones, and the luck felt less like grace than like a wound.

Why him and not Henry? Why the bruise and not the bullet? Why the "nearly spent" ball that left a mark on his knee instead of the ball that — what? — struck Henry's leg, sent him falling, left him in the path of the advancing rebels?

Alexander did not ask these questions in his letters. He was not a man who questioned God's decisions, at least not in writing, at least not yet. But the questions were there, in the silences between the sentences, in the two-month gap between his September 19 letter and his November 12 letter, in the deterioration of his health that followed the battle as surely as winter follows autumn.

Antietam broke something in Alexander. Not his faith — that held, as it always held, the granite foundation that Frances had laid in him. Not his courage — he would fight again, at Gettysburg and Lookout Mountain and through the entire Atlanta Campaign, with a steadiness that earned him promotion after promotion. Not his love for his family — that, if anything, deepened, intensified, became the defining feature of his character in a way it had not been before.

What Antietam broke was his innocence.

The boy who had written "What a Bedlam!" with exclamation points and wonder was gone. The young man who had loved the sound of shells at Harpers Ferry — who had said "it does me good to hear our shells shriek and howl" — was gone. In their place was someone older, harder, more careful with his words. The letters after Antietam were different. The sentences were shorter. The humor was darker. The observations were sharper and less generous. The war had shown Alexander its worst face, and the face looked like his brother's, and he would spend the next three years trying to look away from it and failing.

He was eighteen.

He had been a soldier for one year.

He had twenty-one hundred days to go.
