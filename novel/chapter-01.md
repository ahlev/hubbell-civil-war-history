# PART ONE: WHAT A BEDLAM

---

## Chapter 1: The Decision

---

The letter arrived on a Tuesday in September, the paper already soft from handling, as if Henry had folded and unfolded it before deciding to send it. Alexander found it on the kitchen table when he came in from the south field, his boots still carrying the smell of turned earth, and he knew before he opened it what his brother would say.

He was right.

*My opinion and advise is he had better stay at home, and I think I am wright. If he can get a good chance to go to College, he had better go. If not he can make himself yousefull at home. I don't know as I have spelt that long word right. At all events Alex can do better than going to war.*

Alexander read it twice, standing at the table with his hat still on, the late afternoon light slanting through the west windows and falling across the page in a band of gold. Henry couldn't spell *useful.* Henry, who had been a soldier since July, who had marched through Baltimore where they'd shot at Massachusetts boys three months earlier, who had heard the guns of Bull Run from twenty-eight miles away and written home about it as calmly as if he were reporting the weather — Henry, who was doing the very thing he now told his younger brother not to do.

Alexander set the letter down. Through the window he could see the orchard, the stone wall his father had built the year before he died, the road that led north to Champlain village and, beyond it, to the railroad. Captain Ransom was raising a company right now, right here in Clinton County. Boys Alexander had gone to school with were signing up. The regiment was forming at Ogdensburgh, sixty miles north, and it would not wait forever.

He was seventeen years old.

---

The farm had belonged to Frederick Augustus Hubbell, who had worked it for twenty years and died on it in 1853, leaving behind a widow, a daughter, and four sons. The eldest was Fannie, a teacher. Then Henry, born in '39 — practical, capable, the one who stepped into his father's boots and kept the operation running. Then Charles, born in '40 — quiet, reliable, built for endurance. Then James, born in '43 — the scholar, thin and bookish, more comfortable with a volume of Cicero than a plow handle. And finally Alexander, born in '44, the baby of the family, the one they all called different pet names, the one his mother still addressed in letters as "my darling pet."

Eight years Frederick had been in the ground. Eight years Frances McNeil Hubbell had managed alone — a Scottish-born woman of fierce intelligence and fiercer faith, who read her Bible every morning before dawn and wrote letters in a hand so precise it might have been typeset. She had kept the farm solvent, the children fed and educated, the social obligations of a respectable Clinton County family maintained, all without a husband's income or a husband's name on the bank notes. She had done it by will and by God, in roughly that order, though she would have reversed the sequence if asked.

When Henry enlisted in June of 1861, she had not tried to stop him. He was twenty-two, a man grown, and the country needed men. She had packed his things, written a Bible verse on the flyleaf of his Testament, and watched him march away with Company D of the 34th New York Volunteers. She had received his first letter from Washington — the drunken set, the bullet holes in the Baltimore depot, the knapsack that was so heavy — and she had wept only after Fannie had gone to bed.

But Alexander was different. Alexander was seventeen. Alexander was the youngest. Alexander was, in the particular grammar of a mother's heart, the last one she could hold.

---

"You've read Henry's letter," Frances said.

She was standing in the kitchen doorway, a dish towel over her shoulder, her hair pinned back in the way she wore it for housework. She was fifty-three years old and looked it — the lines around her eyes had deepened since Frederick's death, and her hands, which had once played piano at church socials, were now roughened to leather by eight years of managing a farm she'd never been trained to manage.

"I have," Alexander said.

"And?"

"And Henry can't spell *useful.*"

Frances did not smile. "Henry can spell well enough. He spells his advice plainly."

"He advises me to go to college."

"There is nothing wrong with that advice."

Alexander removed his hat and hung it on the peg by the door — the peg that had been Frederick's, that Henry had used after him, that now belonged to whoever was last through the door. "Mother, Captain Ransom is forming his company this week. He's nearly full. If I don't go now—"

"If you don't go now, you will be warm this winter and alive this spring."

"That is not guaranteed to anyone."

Frances looked at him. She had a way of looking that communicated volumes — a slow, level gaze that seemed to inventory every thought behind your eyes and find most of them wanting. Alexander had seen that gaze deployed against hired men, against neighbors who shorted her on sugar-making arrangements, against the Reverend Mr. Copeland when his sermons ran long. It was a formidable instrument. But Alexander had been watching it for seventeen years, and he had developed, if not immunity, at least endurance.

"I want to serve," he said. "I want to do something."

"You are doing something. You are helping me keep this farm."

"Charles can do that. James can do that."

"Charles and James are not you."

Alexander took a breath. He loved his mother more than he knew how to say — more than he would know how to say even after four years of writing letters that tried — and the idea of causing her pain was physically uncomfortable to him, a tightness in his chest that made his voice come out wrong. But the tightness of staying was worse.

"Mother, I have thought about this. I have prayed about it."

"Have you."

"Yes."

"And what did God say?"

Alexander paused. He was not sure whether she was being sincere or pointed. With Frances McNeil Hubbell, it was often both.

"I believe He wants me to go."

Frances set down the dish towel. She moved to the table and sat in the chair that was hers — the one at the end, nearest the stove, where she could monitor the kitchen and the conversation simultaneously. She folded her hands on the oilcloth.

"Sit down, Alexander."

He sat.

"I will not prevent you," she said. "You are nearly a man. In seven months you will be eighteen and beyond my authority in any case. If you are determined to go, I would rather you went with my blessing than without it. But I want you to hear me."

"I'm listening."

"Your father died when you were nine years old. You barely remember him. But I remember him very well, and I remember what he said to me when he was dying. He said, 'Frances, keep them together.' That was all. Not 'keep the farm' or 'keep the accounts' or 'keep up appearances.' He said *keep them together.* And I have tried. God knows I have tried."

Her voice did not break. Frances's voice never broke. But something behind it shifted, like a beam taking a heavier load.

"Henry is in Virginia. I do not sleep well since he went. I read the casualty lists in the paper every morning before I eat, and I run my finger down the columns looking for his name, and when I do not find it I thank God and eat my breakfast. That is how I begin my days now. If you go, I will run my finger down two columns instead of one. If Charles and James go — and they will, I can see it in their faces — I will read four columns every morning, and there will be nothing left of my breakfast appetite at all."

Alexander said nothing. There was nothing to say to that.

"But this is what I want you to hear," Frances continued. "If you go, you will go as a Hubbell. You will conduct yourself in a manner that would make your father proud. You will not drink. You will not gamble. You will not use profane language. You will attend services when they are offered. You will read your Bible. You will write to me — often. You will not do anything of which I would not approve. Do you understand me?"

"Yes, Mother."

"Say it back to me."

"I will conduct myself as a Hubbell. I will not drink or gamble or swear. I will go to services and read my Bible and write to you. I will not do anything you wouldn't approve of."

Frances nodded. She reached across the table and took his hand. Her grip was stronger than he expected — it was always stronger than he expected.

"Then I will write a verse in your Bible," she said, "and you will carry it with you. And you will read the ninety-first Psalm every day. Do you know it?"

"'He that dwelleth in the secret place of the Most High—'"

"'—shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty.' Yes. Every day, Alexander. Promise me."

"I promise."

She released his hand. She stood. She picked up the dish towel.

"Write Henry and tell him yourself," she said. "I will not do it for you."

---

Captain Jonathan M. Ransom was a Champlain man, which meant Alexander had known him all his life — had sat behind him in church, had watched him stride up the village street in his good coat, had heard his mother speak of him as "a man of integrity, which is not common." Ransom was raising Company H of the 60th New York Infantry, and by the third week of September 1861, the company was nearly full. The men were mustering at Ogdensburgh, at a place called Camp Wheeler, which had been a railroad yard before the war turned it into a barracks.

Alexander enlisted on the nineteenth of September. The medical examination was, in his words, "very rigid." The surgeon told him he was a fine boy. He was five feet seven inches tall, brown-haired, clear-eyed, with the kind of wiry frame that hard work builds on a farm boy who has been lifting things since he could walk. He weighed perhaps 140 pounds. He was, as far as the United States Army was concerned, fit for service.

The ride north to Ogdensburgh took most of a day. Alexander went by train, jammed into a car with other recruits — farm boys mostly, men from Champlain and Chazy and Chateaugay and Mooers and all the small towns that dotted Clinton County like stones in a field. They were loud. Some were drunk. A half-tipsy fellow, somewhere toward the front of the car, sang out: "Strike up there forward — or they will think we are all dead!" The fifer and drummer obliged, and the car erupted in noise that was part music, part shouting, and part the raw sound of young men heading toward something enormous that none of them understood.

Alexander watched the landscape slide past the window. The farms of the Champlain Valley, the orchards going amber in the September light, the stone walls running along the roads like gray stitching on green cloth. He had spent his entire life within twenty miles of this view. By this time tomorrow, he would be a soldier.

---

Camp Wheeler was chaos.

The barracks occupied a cluster of large buildings that had been a machine shop and railroad buildings before the Army requisitioned them. The machine shop served as a dining hall. Two other buildings had been converted to sleeping quarters, fitted with tiers of berths made from hemlock boards and filled with straw. The whole complex smelled of pine shavings, coal smoke, sweat, and the particular sharp odor of hundreds of men living in close quarters who had not yet learned the Army's systems for keeping themselves and their surroundings clean.

When the train pulled in — the barracks were close to the railroad — the recruits were formed in double file and marched to the parade ground. Alexander was surprised to see the ground surrounded by armed sentinels. He had not expected to be guarded. He had expected — what? He wasn't sure. Something between a school and a church social, perhaps. What he found was something between a circus and a jail.

They were dismissed, then formed again and marched to dinner: soup, bread, boiled pork, cold slum, and potatoes. Alexander thought it very good. The gingerbread his mother had packed went even better. He ate it quickly, before anyone could ask him to share, and felt guilty about it, and then felt practical about the guilt. This was the Army. A man learned to eat what he had when he had it.

That evening, he found a corner of the sleeping quarters where the noise was merely deafening rather than unbearable, braced a sheet of paper on his knee, and wrote his first letter home. People knocked his elbows. Someone above his head was singing *Am I a Follower of the Lamb.* The fifer was practicing somewhere nearby, the notes thin and sharp in the cold air.

*My Dear Mother —*

*Here I am safe and sound in the barracks. How strange it does seem. Ah! what a bedlam!*

He described the journey, the dinner, the buildings, the armed sentinels — everything his mother had asked him to tell. *You said you wished me to tell you everything.* He wrote quickly, in a hand that was neat for a seventeen-year-old but would improve dramatically over the next four years, as the volume of his correspondence turned letter-writing from an obligation into an art.

*Your affectionate son,*
*F. A. Hubbell (Alex)*

He had signed with his father's initials — Frederick Augustus — before adding his own name in parentheses, as if he were not yet sure which Hubbell he was. It was the only time he would sign a letter that way. By the next one, he was Alexander F. Hubbell, and he would remain so for the rest of the war.

---

Three days later, he wrote to his sister. The letter was lighter, more playful — Alexander always wrote more freely to Fannie than to his mother, as if the generational distance between mother and son imposed a formality that the lateral bond between siblings did not.

*I am contented and happy, though I long to see you all.*

He described the routine: reveille at six, roll call at seven, breakfast, drill, dinner at one, drill again at three, retreat at six, supper at seven, tattoo at nine. The days were structured and predictable in a way that farm life never was — no weather to worry about, no animals to tend, no fence to mend by dark. The Army told you when to eat, when to sleep, when to stand, when to march. For a boy who had spent his life making decisions on the fly — which field to plow, which fence to fix, which chore to do before the rain came — the regimentation was both constraining and, secretly, a relief.

*Our Company was examined and sworn into the States service on Saturday. The examination was very rigid. The surgeon told me that I was a fine boy.*

He asked Fannie to send Henry's overcoat — "please tuck it up so that it will not be too long for me" — and Charles's wide-awake suit for guard duty. He mentioned the twelve-pound brass cannon that was discharged at sunrise and sunset, and the Captain who was "very kind to me," and the band that had just struck up as he was closing the letter.

*Oh how I wish I could see you all.*

He had been away from home for three days.

---

The next letter, written the following day, went to his brother James. And here Alexander revealed something he had not told his mother or his sister — the quality of his attention, the sharpness of his observation, the storytelling instinct that would make him, over the next four years, the most prolific and vivid letter-writer in the family.

He told James about standing guard:

*I stood from fifteen to twenty hours. The reason why I stood so long was because a great many ran the guard after nightfall and we were obliged to thrible it to prevent this.*

He described the system: a watchword given at nightfall, no one allowed in or out without it. He described the challenge: *"Who comes there?"* Reply: *"Friend."* *"Advance Friend and give the countersign."* The man advanced until his abdomen touched Alexander's bayonet, then leaned over and whispered the word.

Five men passed him this way. Then he heard someone whisper to a man outside the camp: *"Is it right?"* The watchword had been discovered. Alexander called for the officer of the guard. The guards were doubled. The Colonel came around and ordered them to "run any man through the legs who attempted to run the guard."

*No one ran the guard again that night.*

He was seventeen years old, standing in the dark with a bayonet, responsible for the security of seven hundred men. Three weeks ago, he had been turning soil in the south field.

But it was the postscript to James's letter that carried the deeper note. He had squeezed a few lines onto the bottom of the page, addressed to his mother:

*My dear Mother: I can not close this letter without saying a word or two to you. You may consider all this letter yours.*

He could not write to anyone without writing to her. Every letter, no matter who it was addressed to, bent eventually toward Frances — toward the kitchen in Champlain, toward the table where she read the casualty lists every morning, toward the woman whose voice he heard in his head long after the sound of her actual voice had faded.

---

On the twenty-seventh of September, the same day Alexander was writing about prayer meetings and the Brother Craftsmen, Henry sat down in his camp near Washington and wrote a letter to Charles.

*So Alexander is off for the war. Well, good luck to him. I rather think he will get sick of it when he comes to go on guard duty, but perhaps he wont.*

Henry was twenty-two, a year and a half older than Charles, three years older than James, five years older than Alexander. He had been a soldier since June. He had stood guard in the rain, had heard the cannon at Bull Run, had watched a man die from a carelessly dropped rifle. He knew what was coming for his little brother, and he could not prevent it, and this knowledge sat in his letters like a stone at the bottom of a clear pool — visible beneath the surface if you knew where to look.

*I was cut four hours in the rain on guard last night, but I have got so I don't mind it much.*

He did not say: *You will learn not to mind it too.* He did not say: *The things you learn not to mind are the things that change you.* He was Henry — practical, understated, a man who wrote about the price of things and the condition of the roads and the quality of the rations, who saved his gold pieces and sent every dollar home, who carried the eldest son's burden without complaint and without poetry. He was everything Alexander was not: steady where Alexander was mercurial, dry where Alexander was effusive, resigned where Alexander was defiant.

They were both soldiers now. Henry near Washington, Alexander at Ogdensburgh. Between them lay six hundred miles, a dozen rivers, and the better part of a year before they would see each other again.

It would be the last time Henry would be wrong about his brother. Alexander did not get sick of guard duty. He got good at it.

---

Back in Champlain, Frances sat at the kitchen table with both letters open before her — Henry's from Washington, Alexander's from Ogdensburgh — and did the thing she would do every day for the next four years, the thing that would become as automatic as breathing and as painful as a wound that would not heal.

She compared them.

She checked the dates, the postmarks, the return addresses. She noted who was healthy and who was not, who had been paid and who had not, who needed clothing and who needed money. She cross-referenced: Henry mentioned Alexander's enlistment, so Henry had received her letter about it. Alexander mentioned the prayer meeting and the Brother Craftsmen, which meant he was attending to his spiritual life, which was good, though the shouting man worried her. Henry's handwriting was steady; he was well. Alexander's was hurried; he was excited.

She opened her own letter paper. She was a formidable correspondent — her letters were long, densely packed, grammatically precise, and freighted with instruction, interrogation, and love in roughly equal measure. She wrote to Alexander:

*Read the 91st Psalm daily — and live up to its promises. The Saviour is near, near you — and can hear the heart's faintest sigh. In the din of battle no artillery can drown that sigh if it is breathed in faith.*

She did not know when the din of battle would reach her sons. She did not know that one of them would hear it and survive and the other would hear it and fall. She knew only that both her boys were soldiers now, and that the ninety-first Psalm was the strongest armor she could give them, stronger than the beautiful Enfield rifles the Army would soon put in their hands.

She sealed the letter. She addressed it. She set it on the hall table for the morning post.

Then she opened her Bible to Psalm 91 and read it herself, for the first of what would be more than a thousand mornings.

*He that dwelleth in the secret place of the Most High shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty.*

*I will say of the Lord, He is my refuge and my fortress: my God; in him will I trust.*

*Thou shalt not be afraid for the terror by night; nor for the arrow that flieth by day.*

Outside, the September darkness was settling over Champlain. The farm was quiet. The fields had been turned. The orchard was heavy with fruit. Frederick's stone wall ran along the south boundary, solid and unchanging, holding the earth in place the way he had held the family.

Frances closed the Bible.

Two of her sons were soldiers.

She did not yet know that all four would be.

---

At Camp Wheeler, Alexander lay on his hemlock-board berth, wrapped in his Army blanket, listening to seven hundred men breathe and cough and mutter in their sleep. The straw beneath him smelled of dust and something faintly sweet, like hay that had been rained on. Someone two bunks down was crying, quietly, the way men cry when they don't want to be heard. Someone further away was talking in his sleep — giving orders, it sounded like, or perhaps praying. It was hard to tell the difference.

Alexander could not sleep. His mind was full of the day's images — the parade ground, the sentinel posts, the prayer meeting, the letter from Henry — and beneath them all, like a current running under a river's surface, the awareness that he had done something irrevocable. He had enlisted. He had sworn an oath. He belonged to the United States Army for three years or the duration of the war, whichever came first. He was no longer Alexander Hubbell of Champlain, New York, farmer's son, youngest of five. He was Private Hubbell, Company H, 60th New York Infantry.

He thought about what Henry had said. *He had better stay at home.* Henry was probably right. Henry was usually right about practical things. College would have been safer, smarter, more likely to lead to the kind of life their mother wanted for him — a profession, a respectable position, a house with books in it instead of a hemlock berth with straw.

But Alexander had not wanted safety. He had wanted — what? He wasn't sure he could name it. Not glory, exactly, though the flags and the cheering crowds and the brass cannon fired at sunrise had stirred something in him that felt adjacent to glory. Not adventure, exactly, though the train ride north and the chaos of the barracks had been the most exciting thirty-six hours of his life. What he had wanted, he thought — lying in the dark, listening to the crying man and the sleep-talker and the vast communal breathing of seven hundred strangers who were now, by the alchemy of oath and uniform, his brothers — was to be *present.* To be part of the great thing that was happening. To not be the one who stayed home and read about it in the papers.

Henry had gone. Charles would go. James would go. If Alexander stayed home, he would be the only Hubbell son who had not answered the call. He would sit at his mother's table and read her casualty lists with her and feel, in his bones, the specific shame of a young man in wartime who is capable of fighting and does not.

He could not bear that.

So he had come to Camp Wheeler, to the hemlock berth and the straw and the noise and the armed sentinels and the twelve-pound brass cannon and the rough men and the rougher language and the prayer meetings where men shouted at God until they were hoarse. He had come to be part of it.

*What a Bedlam.*

He pulled the blanket tighter. The crying man had stopped. The sleep-talker had subsided into mumbles. The barracks were settling into the deeper quiet of late night, the quiet that was never really quiet — always a cough somewhere, always a creak of boards, always the distant footsteps of the sentinels walking their posts.

Alexander closed his eyes.

Tomorrow there would be drill. And the day after that, more drill. And then, eventually, the regiment would be full and the orders would come and they would march south, toward the war, toward Henry, toward whatever was waiting for them in the vast and violent country that stretched from this railroad yard in Ogdensburgh to the rebel guns in Virginia.

He was seventeen years old.

He was not afraid.

He would learn to be.

---

*The next morning, Alexander rose at reveille, marched to breakfast with his company, ate his coffee and beefsteak and potatoes, and began the first of what would be more than fourteen hundred days as a soldier of the United States. He did not know it would be fourteen hundred days. He did not know about Antietam or Gettysburg or Kennesaw Mountain or the hospitals at Harpers Ferry and Chattanooga or the night blindness or the scurvy or the brother who would die or the two who would die after. He knew only that the drummer was beating and the fifer was playing and Captain Ransom was calling the roll, and when the Captain called "Hubbell," Alexander answered "Here," and meant it in every sense of the word.*

*He was here. He had come. He had chosen this.*

*In four years, he would write his last letter home — the shortest in the collection, seven lines on borrowed paper — and in it he would make a promise: "I will tell you all about it at the tea table."*

*But first he had to survive the telling.*
