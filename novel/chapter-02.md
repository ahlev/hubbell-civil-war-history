## Chapter 2: The Sentinel

---

The days at Camp Wheeler had a rhythm like a pulse — steady, predictable, inescapable. Reveille at six. Roll call at seven. Breakfast: coffee, beefsteak, potatoes, bread. Each man a quart cup of coffee with the privilege of having it sweetened. Drill from eight to noon. Dinner at one. Drill again from three to five. Retreat at six. Supper at seven. Tattoo at nine. Roll call again, and all retire.

Alexander learned the rhythm the way he had learned the seasons on the farm — not by thinking about it but by letting his body absorb it, until the bugle call at dawn pulled him upright before his mind had formed its first thought of the day. He had been in camp less than a week and already civilian time felt strange and loose, like a shirt that had lost its buttons.

The regiment was growing. Over seven hundred men now, and more arriving every day. The Colonel — "a small, smart, energetic-looking man," Alexander wrote — drove them through drill with a precision that left no room for daydreaming. Left face, right face, forward march, column into line, line into column, by the right flank, by the left flank, at the double-quick. Alexander's body learned a new alphabet: the vocabulary of mass movement, the grammar of coordinated men.

He was good at it. The surgeon had called him a fine boy, and the drill instructors seemed to agree. He was quick, attentive, eager to learn — and more than that, he was watchful. Other recruits went through the motions with their eyes on the ground; Alexander watched everything. He noted which officers were competent and which were not. He studied the way Ransom handled the company — firm but kind, the way a good farmer handles a horse. He observed the brass cannon being fired at sunrise and sunset and calculated the cost of the powder. He listened to the older men, the few who had served in the Mexican War or in state militia units, and stored their casual expertise like a squirrel storing nuts.

*If anybody asks you how I like soldiering you may tell them I like it very well,* he wrote to James. *The only very hard thing about it is that I am obliged to listen to so much profanity and obscene language. "However" as Henry says, I make the best of it.*

The quotation marks around *However* were deliberate — a small, affectionate mockery of his older brother's most characteristic word. Henry said *however* the way other men said *but* or *still* or *on the other hand.* It was his verbal tic, his placeholder, the word he reached for when he was about to say something he didn't entirely want to say. Alexander had heard it a thousand times at the kitchen table in Champlain, and hearing it echo in his own pen was like pressing a bruise to confirm it was still there.

---

Guard duty came on his fourth day.

He stood from fifteen to twenty hours — far longer than the standard shift, because after nightfall men began running the guard, slipping past the sentinels to get to the village, or to the sutler, or simply to prove they could. The watch was tripled, then doubled again when the Colonel discovered the watchword had been compromised. Alexander stood at his post — sentry number eleven — with his bayonet fixed and his blood up and the cold September air running through his uniform like water through a sieve.

*During the evening I saw a person coming toward me. When within ten feet of me I chalenged him with "who comes there."*

The reply came: "Friend."

*"Advance Friend and give the countersign."*

The man advanced. He advanced until his abdomen touched Alexander's bayonet point — that was the protocol, the physical intimacy of it as startling as a handshake from a stranger — and then leaned over and whispered the word.

Alexander passed him. Passed another. And another. Five men came through his post, each pressing belly to bayonet, each whispering the same word in the dark. Then he heard it — a voice, just outside the camp, in a half-whisper: *"Is it right?"*

The word was out.

He called for the officer of the guard. The Colonel came. The order was given: run any man through the legs who attempted to run the guard. No one did. The camp settled. The night grew old and cold and Alexander stood through all of it, his bayonet point catching starlight, his eyes adjusting to the dark until he could distinguish a man from a shadow at twenty yards.

He had been a civilian six days ago. Now he was a sentinel with the authority to bayonet a man in the thigh. The transition had been as smooth and natural as a river going over a falls — the water did not change, only the terrain beneath it.

---

The prayer meetings were a different kind of challenge.

Daily meetings were held in the camp, and Alexander attended — partly because his mother had made him promise, partly because he wanted to, and partly because the meetings were one of the few activities at Camp Wheeler that did not involve profanity, gambling, or the twelve-pound brass cannon. The services were led by the chaplain, a man of genuine conviction if uneven oratorical gifts, and attended by a shifting population of soldiers who came for reasons ranging from sincere devotion to desperate loneliness to simple curiosity about what a prayer meeting in a barracks looked like.

Alexander found out on his second evening.

A man stood up — middle-aged, thick-necked, with the look of someone who had been a deacon or a lay preacher in civilian life — and began to shout. He called on God to "come down now and bless us," his voice rising from speech into something that was neither speech nor song but a kind of vocal convulsion, raw and rhythmic, accompanied by the clapping of his hands and the swaying of his body. He shouted until he was exhausted, then began to draw his breath between his lips in sharp, hissing inhalations, muttering and ejaculating. When he recovered somewhat, he began again: "The Lord is with us! He is here! Glory to God!" The other men added their amens and ejaculations, a ragged chorus that filled the room with sound that was part worship, part mania, and part the particular desperation of men who had recently left their homes and did not know if they would ever return.

The Colonel was in the room below, drilling officers in the use of arms. The noise from above must have reached him like the sound of a riot.

Alexander was mortified.

*I felt ashamed that I was there,* he wrote to his mother. *Perhaps I feel wrong. What shall I do? I wish to go to meeting, but I do not wish to see such scenes as that.*

The question was genuine. Alexander's faith was deep and real — it had been planted by his mother and watered by the Reverend Mr. Copeland and rooted in the rocky soil of Clinton County Presbyterianism, which favored dignity, restraint, and a relationship with God that was more conversation than convulsion. The shouting man offended Alexander's sense of propriety, but it also frightened him — not because the man was insincere, but because he might be sincere, and if this was what sincere faith looked like under pressure, Alexander was not sure he had enough of it.

*Ask Mr. Copeland what I shall do.*

He could not ask the question of himself. He was seventeen. He needed his minister, his mother, the familiar voices of authority that had always told him what was right and what was wrong. In three months, he would be in Baltimore, a hundred miles from the nearest person who knew his name, and the question of what to do about God would be his alone to answer. But for now, in the hemlock-board barracks at Ogdensburgh, he still reached for home.

---

The other question — the one that occupied the second page of his September 27 letter — concerned the Brother Craftsmen.

It was a soldiers' mutual aid society, headquartered in Watertown, and it had offered Alexander the office of adjutant — the third-ranking officer in the organization. The society's purpose was simple and practical: if a member was sick, the others would care for him. If he was wounded in battle, they would see to his treatment. If he was attacked, they would defend him — unless he had started the fight, in which case he was on his own. If he was convicted of "licentious conduct," he was expelled.

Alexander described it all to his mother in careful detail, asking her approval, presenting the evidence as if laying out a legal case for her review. He had already accepted the position "with the privilege of withdrawing if it does not meet your approval."

This was Alexander at seventeen: a boy who would defy his older brother's advice and enlist against his wishes, but who would not join a benevolent society without his mother's permission. The contradiction was not hypocrisy. It was the particular architecture of a young man's moral life — the load-bearing walls were in different places than an older person might expect. He could decide to risk his life. He could not decide whether to accept a one-dollar-a-month salary from a fraternal organization.

*I tell you what it is, Mother. If I ever hated vice I hate it ten times worse now.*

The camp was teaching him what the farm had not — that virtue was not the default state of men, but a discipline that required constant maintenance. The drinking, the gambling, the profanity, the running of the guard — all of it was the natural entropy of seven hundred young men removed from their families, their churches, their communities, and deposited in a world where the old rules had been suspended and the new ones had not yet taken hold.

Alexander would not be swept along. He would be the sentinel. The man at post number eleven, bayonet fixed, challenging every shadow: *Who comes there?*

---

October arrived cold. The trees along the railroad turned amber and then bare, and the wind off Lake Ontario found every gap in the camp's improvised shelters. Alexander's company had drawn guard duty on the line, which meant standing in the open for hours at a stretch, watching the approaches to the barracks with the attention of a man who knew that the next person through the dark might be a drunken private heading for the village or might be something else entirely.

He wrote to his mother on October 3. The letter was short — routine, he called it — but it contained a sentence that Frances would have read with a specific kind of relief:

*Daily prayer meetings are held in the camp. I attended one last night. Have not spent so pleasant an evening since I have been here.*

He had found his way. Not the shouting meetings, but the quieter ones — the chaplain reading scripture, the singing, the prayers offered in measured voices by men who believed that God could be addressed without being screamed at. Alexander's faith would deepen and complicate over the next four years, strained by death and disease and the particular theological problem of a just God who allows good men to be torn apart by canister shot. But it would never break. It was, like his mother's, load-bearing.

On the same day, he wrote a separate letter — to an unnamed brother, probably Charles — that was all practicalities: the weather, the food, the drill schedule. No feelings, no questions about prayer meetings or fraternal societies. This was the other Alexander, the one who could compartmentalize his audience and give each person what they needed: spiritual earnestness for Mother, domestic details for the siblings, adventure stories for James.

He was already learning the central skill of the letter-writer in wartime: the management of truth. Not lying — Alexander never lied in his letters, as far as anyone could tell — but selecting. Choosing which truths to share with which recipient. Mother got the faith and the feelings. Fannie got the humor and the candor. James got the military details and the stories. And everyone got the same closing reassurance: *I am well. I am content. I love you.*

Whether the first two were always true was a question the letters could not answer. The third was never in doubt.

---

The regiment filled. Eight hundred men, then nine hundred, then the full complement. The Colonel drilled them harder — longer hours, faster movements, less patience for mistakes. The twelve-pound brass cannon was joined by a second, and the morning and evening salutes became a kind of punctuation, the camp's daily exclamation points.

Alexander watched the men around him and began to sort them into categories — a habit he would maintain throughout the war, the novelist's instinct for character that ran beneath his soldier's discipline like a vein of quartz through granite.

There was Corporal Luther, who would become his closest friend — steady, reliable, the kind of man you wanted beside you on guard duty and across from you at the mess table. There was Orderly Dickinson, who would later buy bread for Alexander in a hospital with his own money and earn the kind of gratitude that Alexander would carry for the rest of his life. There was Captain Ransom, whose unanimous election by the company was a testament to the quality of the man and the judgment of the men who chose him.

And there were the others — the drinkers, the gamblers, the men who ran the guard and spent their money on whiskey and had it stolen from them and borrowed from the men who'd saved. Alexander noted them all with the same clear, unsentimental eye.

*Several of our company have spent their money for drinking, some have had it stolen from them.*

That was Henry's observation, from his first letter home in July. Alexander had arrived at the same conclusion independently. The brothers' letters, read side by side, showed the same clear-eyed pragmatism about human nature — a family trait, perhaps, or a farm-country trait, the unsentimental realism of people who have watched animals be born and die and learned that nature does not soften its lessons for the tenderhearted.

---

By the middle of October, the orders began to solidify. The regiment would move south. The destination was not yet certain — there were rumors of Baltimore, of Washington, of Virginia, of points unknown — but the direction was clear. South, toward the war, toward whatever waited for them beyond the colored leaves and the cold wind and the hemlock-board berths that had been their world for a month.

Alexander felt it coming the way you feel weather changing — a shift in pressure, a quickening in the air. The drill grew more purposeful. The officers talked less about formations and more about logistics. New equipment arrived: knapsacks, blankets, rifles. The brass cannon were cleaned and oiled with a tenderness that suggested they were no longer decorative.

On an evening in late October, Alexander sat in the prayer meeting — the quiet one, the one without shouting — and listened to the chaplain read from the Book of Joshua: *Be strong and of a good courage; be not afraid, neither be thou dismayed: for the Lord thy God is with thee whithersoever thou goest.*

The words settled into him like rain into dry ground. He had heard them before, in the Champlain church, sitting beside his mother in the family pew. But they meant something different now. Whithersoever thou goest was no longer a generality. It was a specific promise for a specific journey that would begin in a matter of days.

He bowed his head. He prayed — silently, without shouting, in the manner his mother had taught him: a conversation with God, not a performance for men.

Then he went back to the barracks and packed his things.

---

The letter that arrived from Henry that week was characteristically practical.

*I have got so I don't mind it much.*

Henry was talking about standing guard in the rain, but the sentence carried a larger meaning that Alexander would not fully understand for months. *I have got so I don't mind it much.* The six most dangerous words in a soldier's vocabulary. Not the declaration of heroism, not the cry of battle, but the quiet admission that the extraordinary had become ordinary — that the things which should shock and disturb and terrify had been absorbed into the body's rhythms like the bugle at dawn.

Henry had got so he didn't mind it. Henry had been in the Army since June, four months ahead of Alexander, and in those four months he had crossed the threshold that every soldier crosses — the line between the man who flinches and the man who doesn't, between the civilian who is disturbed by violence and the soldier who accommodates it.

Alexander had not crossed it yet. He was still flinching. He was still disturbed by the profanity, bothered by the shouting prayer meetings, rattled by the crying man in the dark. He was still, in every way that mattered, a civilian in a uniform.

But the orders were coming. The train was waiting. And somewhere to the south, the war was waiting too, patient and enormous, ready to teach him what Henry already knew: that a man could get used to anything, and that getting used to it was both the salvation and the cost.

---

On the first of November, 1861, the 60th New York Infantry left Camp Wheeler.

They marched to the railroad in column, rifles on their shoulders, packs heavy with everything they owned. The brass cannon went with them. The Colonel rode ahead on a bay horse that had been presented to him in New York City, its coat brushed to a gleam that looked almost absurd against the mud of the Ogdensburgh road.

Alexander was in the left wing, which meant his company was among the last to board. He stood on the platform and watched the train take shape — car after car filling with men, the noise building like an orchestra tuning up, all the instruments playing different things but all of them playing.

The barracks were behind him. The hemlock berths, the straw, the parade ground where he had stood sentinel in the September dark. Four weeks of his life, compressed already into memory, shrinking in the way that places shrink when you leave them.

He climbed into the car. Found a seat by the window. Set his pack between his feet.

The train began to move.

Through the window, Alexander watched the last of Ogdensburgh slide past — the houses, the church spires, the edge of the lake. Then the countryside opened up: the farms of St. Lawrence County, the stone walls, the orchards stripped bare by autumn. The landscape of northern New York, which was the only landscape he had ever known, and which he was now leaving for the first time in his life.

He did not know that he would not see it again for nearly four years.

A man across the aisle produced a flask and offered it around. Alexander declined. Another man began singing, a song about a girl in Albany, and others joined in, the lyrics growing progressively less suitable for mixed company. Alexander noted this with the dutiful disapproval his mother would have expected, and then — because he was seventeen, and because the train was moving, and because the future was enormous and unknowable and the present was full of young men singing badly — he smiled.

*What a Bedlam,* he thought. And meant it with something that had not been there a month ago.

Affection.

---

The train carried them south to Rouses Point, where they would board a steamer for the journey down Lake Champlain to Whitehall. And at Rouses Point, in the last minutes before departure, Alexander saw two figures on the platform that stopped his heart.

Frances. And Charles.

They had come to see him off — his sister and his brother, standing in the November cold with their collars turned up and their faces carrying the particular expression of people who have traveled a considerable distance to say goodbye and are now trying very hard not to make the goodbye harder than it has to be.

Alexander broke ranks. He did not ask permission. He did not care. He went to them and they held each other — the three youngest Hubbells, minus James who was home, minus Henry who was already at the war, minus the mother who had sent them all into the world and now waited at the kitchen table for the letters that would tell her they were still in it.

*I was very much surprised and very much pleased to F and C there,* he wrote afterward. *I was feeling very lonely and seeing them cheered me up wonderfully.*

The steamer could only carry half the regiment. Alexander's company, being in the left wing, had to wait an hour for a second boat — the *United States,* a name that seemed almost too on-the-nose for a troopship carrying men to defend the republic. In that hour, he stood with Frances and Charles on the platform and said the things that families say when one of them is going somewhere dangerous and all of them know it.

He did not record what those things were. He never did. The letters preserved the facts — the times, the distances, the meals, the weather — but the moments of deepest feeling, the ones that mattered most, were always summarized in a single line or not mentioned at all. *I was feeling very lonely and seeing them cheered me up wonderfully.* That was all. The rest — the clutching, the tears, the words spoken too fast and too softly — belonged to the platform and the November air and the space between people who love each other and are about to be separated by war.

Frances gave him a case to pass to Captain Ransom. She had made it herself — some kind of useful contrivance, the letter didn't specify — and it was so well made that Ransom would ask Alexander to convey his thanks and say "he did not see how you managed to contrive so convenient a thing." This was Frances Hubbell's way: even in the act of saying goodbye, she was practical. She sent her son to war with something useful for his captain.

The steamer whistled. Alexander boarded.

He looked back once. Fannie and Charles were still on the platform, growing smaller. He raised his hand. They raised theirs.

Then the steamer moved, and the platform slid away, and Alexander Hubbell went to war.
