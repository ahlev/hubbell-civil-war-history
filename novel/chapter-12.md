## Chapter 12: Into the Mountains

---

The Cumberland Mountains in November were a world apart from anything Alexander had known.

The ridges rose sharp and forested, cut by narrow passes where a handful of men could hold off an army. The roads — if you could call them roads — were dirt tracks that turned to rivers in the rain and to ice in the cold. The rivers themselves were different: wider, faster, running through gorges that made the Champlain Valley's gentle waterways look like garden streams.

Alexander's regiment was holding a pass about fifteen miles below Chattanooga, one link in a chain of Union positions that stretched across the mountains from Tennessee into Georgia. The Confederates were everywhere — not in force, not yet, but in the form of cavalry raids and guerrilla attacks and the constant, nerve-shredding awareness that the next bend in the road might contain a gun.

*Our coming into this Gap prevented us from being in a fight,* Alexander wrote on November 1. *The rest of the brigade pushed on and camped about four miles from here.*

The fight he had missed was the battle of Wauhatchie, fought on the night of October 28-29. The rebels had attacked the Union camp at one or two in the morning, following so closely behind the retreating pickets that the camp was barely alarmed before they were inside it, pouring fire into the sleeping men.

*Reports are so conflicting that I will wait until I can get an authentic acct.*

Alexander had learned to distrust reports. In the fog of the Shenandoah Valley, in the chaos of Antietam, in the confusion of Gettysburg, reports had been wrong as often as they were right, and the smart soldier learned to wait for confirmation before committing anything to paper. It was a journalist's instinct, and Alexander had it.

The casualties from Wauhatchie were significant. General George S. Greene — Alexander's brigade commander, a man he respected — was "seriously if not mortally wounded." Lieutenant Geary, son of the division commander, was reported killed. General Geary himself was "slightly wounded in the toe."

The hierarchy of wounds was its own commentary on the war: the brigade commander seriously wounded, his subordinate's son killed, the division commander scratched. The bullets did not respect rank.

---

November deepened into the siege of Chattanooga.

Grant was in command now, consolidating Union forces for the campaign that would break the Confederate hold on eastern Tennessee and open the road to Atlanta. Alexander's regiment was one piece of a vast machine being assembled in the mountains — infantry, cavalry, artillery, supply trains — all converging on the ridges around Chattanooga where Bragg's Confederate army sat like a gargoyle on a parapet, looking down.

Alexander saw little of the grand strategy. He saw his pass, his pickets, his rations, his mail. He saw the rebels who shelled a wagon train ("their practice was bad and did no damage") and the rebel cavalry that charged an empty campfire ("our boys fired upon them when they wheeled about and skedaddled"). He saw the small war, the daily war, the war that consisted not of great battles but of skirmishes and alarms and the endless, wearing vigilance that drained men faster than combat.

He wrote about it with a dry humor that was new in his letters — a humor born not of high spirits but of exhaustion, the gallows wit of a man who has seen too much to be dramatic about it.

*Wonder what Aunt Wry would say if she knew my vandal footsteps had pressed the soil of Alabama.*

Aunt Wry was apparently a Southern sympathizer, or at least a woman whose opinions about the war differed from the Hubbell consensus. Alexander's reference to her was playful but pointed — a reminder that even within families, even within the tight-knit networks of upstate New York, the war created fissures.

*Do you suppose she pities us as much as she used to?*

---

His mother had asked about a discharge.

The question was natural — Alexander had been in the Army for more than two years, had fought in two of the war's major battles, had been hospitalized with disease, had lost his brother. Surely he had earned the right to come home. Other men were being discharged. Other mothers were getting their sons back. Could Alexander not be one of them?

Alexander shut the question down with a firmness that was respectful but absolute:

*You asked me to give you my views fully in regard to a discharge. It would be useless.*

He would not seek a discharge. The war was not over. His country needed him. He had sworn an oath — "three years or the war" — and he meant to keep it. The fact that Henry was dead and James was thin and Charles was enduring and Frances was alone on a farm she could barely manage — none of these changed the calculus. Alexander's duty was to his country, and his country's duty was unfinished.

It was not heartlessness. It was the particular moral architecture of a man who believed that honor and love existed on the same level but in different rooms, and that the door between them could not always be opened. He loved his mother. He served his country. When the two allegiances conflicted, he chose the one he had sworn to, not the one he was born to.

Frances would not have been surprised. She had raised him to be exactly this kind of man.

---

Late November brought the battles that broke the siege: Lookout Mountain on November 24, Missionary Ridge on November 25. The "Battle Above the Clouds" — as the newspapers would romanticize Lookout Mountain — was fought in fog so thick that the combatants could barely see each other, the firing rippling up the mountainside like lightning along a ridge.

Alexander was present but his letters did not describe the fighting in detail. The western theater was bigger, louder, more chaotic than the eastern theater he was accustomed to, and the battles were fought on a scale that dwarfed even Gettysburg — more men, more guns, more territory to cover.

What Alexander noted was not the battle but its aftermath: the breaking of the siege, the opening of the supply lines, the sudden abundance of food after weeks of near-starvation. Chattanooga had been besieged — the Union forces trapped in the city, their supply lines cut, the men eating half-rations and the horses dying for lack of forage. The breaking of the siege was, for the soldiers who had endured it, less a military victory than a rescue.

Alexander ate. He had not been eating well. The scurvy that would hospitalize him the following summer was already taking root in his body, the Vitamin C deficiency building silently in his blood and bones while he ate hardtack and salt pork and wrote letters home about rations that were adequate but not, in any meaningful sense, food.

---

December 1863. Hospital again.

Not the dire collapse of Harpers Ferry — not the five-dollar letter, not the bread bought with Dickinson's money — but a lesser crisis, a return of the chronic illness that had dogged him since Antietam. He was sent to the general hospital at Tullahoma, Tennessee, a facility that was larger and better equipped than the Harpers Ferry hospital but no more pleasant.

He wrote to his mother from Tullahoma on December 1. The letter was calm, measured, designed to reassure. He was being treated. He was improving. He would rejoin his regiment soon.

But the letters to his brother Charles, written around the same time, told a different story. Alexander was tired. Not the ordinary fatigue of marching and fighting but a deeper tiredness, a weariness of the soul that no amount of sleep could cure. He was nineteen years old and he had been at war for over two years and he had lost his brother and been hospitalized twice and the war showed no sign of ending.

*I feel quite sad tonight,* he would write a few weeks later, in January. *I suppose Frances would say that I have the blues.*

The blues. It was Alexander's word for the thing that had no name in 1864, the thing we would now call depression or combat fatigue or PTSD. He gave it a name from civilian life — the blues, as if it were a temporary mood rather than a condition, something that could be cured by a good night's sleep or a letter from home.

Frances would say he had the blues. Frances, sitting at the kitchen table in Champlain, reading her Bible, writing her letters, managing the farm, carrying the weight of one dead son and three surviving sons and a war that was eating her family alive — Frances would say he had the blues. And she would be wrong, but she would be the kind of wrong that comes from love: the underestimation of suffering that is itself a form of protection, the mother's instinct to minimize the wound because acknowledging its true depth would be unbearable.

Alexander had the blues. The blues had Alexander.

But he would not stay down. He never stayed down. The granite foundation that Frances had laid in him — the faith, the duty, the stubborn refusal to surrender — held. It cracked but it held. It would crack again and again over the next year and a half, and each time it would hold, because Frances had built it well and Alexander had inherited her will along with her faith.

He closed his eyes in the hospital at Tullahoma. He thought of home. He thought of the tea table.

He was nineteen years old, and he had sixteen months of war left.

He would survive them. But they would cost him everything except his life.
