# PART TWO: THE LONG DRILL

---

## Chapter 6: The Railroad War

---

The Relay House sat at the junction of the Baltimore & Ohio's main line and the Washington branch, a crossroads of iron that was as strategically important as any fortress. Control the Relay House and you controlled the supply lines to the capital. Lose it and Washington starved.

Alexander's regiment moved there in March 1862, establishing Camp Niles in the fields surrounding the junction. The work was the same work they had done at Camp Jackson — guard the railroad, walk the line, stand picket — but the scale was different. The Relay House was busier, louder, more vital. Trains came through at all hours, loaded with everything an army needed to fight: ammunition, hardtack, salt pork, boots, blankets, replacement rifle barrels, surgical instruments, coffin timber. The trains moved east and west in a rhythm that was the war's actual heartbeat, more regular and more important than any drum.

Alexander watched them and learned. He learned to read a train the way a farmer reads a sky — to know by the number of cars and the speed of the engine whether the cargo was routine or urgent, whether the front was hungry or well-supplied, whether something had happened that the newspapers hadn't reported yet. A train moving fast with few cars meant orders. A train moving slow with many cars meant supplies. A train with the windows covered meant wounded.

He did not write about the wounded trains. Some truths were not for his mother's breakfast table.

---

His letters from this period — January through April 1862 — showed a young man settling into the particular rhythm of military life that was neither peace nor war but something in between. The excitement of Camp Wheeler and the novelty of Camp Jackson had faded. What replaced them was not boredom exactly, but a kind of steady, low-level alertness that wore on the nerves like a dull headache. He was always on duty or about to be on duty or recovering from duty. The days were structured to the minute and empty of incident. The nights were long and cold and punctuated by the sound of trains.

He was promoted.

The details were not dramatic — a corporal's stripes, given to him sometime in the early months of 1862, in recognition of his reliability and his leadership on the guard line. Alexander did not dwell on it in his letters, which suggested either modesty or the recognition that a corporal's promotion was, in the context of a war that was killing men by the thousands, a small thing.

But it changed his responsibilities. As a corporal, he commanded a squad on guard duty, posting men to their positions and checking on them through the night. He was the man who walked the line at two in the morning, making sure the sentinels were awake, making sure the bridges were secure, making sure the railroad — the Army's circulatory system — continued to pulse.

*My responsibilities are much greater,* he would write later, with characteristic understatement. *I could hardly tell you on paper, that you would understand.*

The responsibility fit him. He wore it naturally, the way some men wear authority and others wear it like an ill-fitting coat. Alexander was not a shouter or a bully. He led the way Ransom led — by example, by competence, by the quiet assumption that the right thing to do was obvious and the only question was whether you had the courage to do it.

---

Luther was his constant companion through these months.

Corporal Luther — his first name lost to the letters, which referred to him only by surname — was Alexander's closest friend in the regiment. They had stood guard together at Camp Wheeler. They had marched south together. They would fight together at Antietam and Gettysburg and through the Atlanta Campaign. They would survive the war together and travel home together, and Luther would spend a few days at the Hubbell farm in Champlain before returning to wherever his own home was.

The friendship was the kind that war produces — forged not by shared interests or compatible personalities but by shared danger and shared hours and the particular intimacy of men who have stood together in the dark, trusting each other with their lives, night after night, until trust becomes as automatic as breathing.

Alexander mentioned Luther often in his letters but never described him. He was simply there — a presence at Alexander's side, as constant and unremarkable as his own shadow. When Alexander sent regards to his mother, Luther's name was always included. When Alexander told a story, Luther was usually in it. When Alexander went on guard, Luther went with him.

They were the kind of friends who did not need to explain their friendship, because the friendship was not a thing they had chosen but a thing that had happened to them, like weather. It was simply the condition of their shared existence, and it required no more explanation than the sunrise.

---

April brought the news from home that sent Alexander into his rage about old Rye and the sugar-making. But April also brought something else: orders.

The regiment was moving again. Not a routine redeployment from one camp to another, but a real movement — west, toward the Shenandoah Valley, toward the war that Alexander had been watching from a distance for seven months.

General Hooker was reorganizing the Army. Stonewall Jackson was rampaging through the Valley, threatening Washington from the flank. Union forces were being shuffled like cards, regiments pulled from garrison duty and thrown into the field, the comfortable routine of railroad guarding replaced overnight by the urgent chaos of active campaigning.

Alexander packed his kit. He had gotten good at this — reducing a life to what could be carried on his back, leaving behind everything that wasn't essential, making the hard calculations about weight and utility that separated the soldiers who could march from the ones who fell out. He packed his rifle, his ammunition, his rations, his blanket, his half-shelter tent, his tin cup, his mess kit, his Bible with Fannie's inscription, and the letters from home.

The letters were not essential by any military definition. They added weight. They took up space. They served no tactical purpose.

He carried them anyway. He would always carry them.

---

The march to Harpers Ferry took the regiment through country that was beautiful and hostile simultaneously — the rolling farmland of western Maryland, green with spring, dotted with the white and pink of fruit trees in bloom, and threaded with roads that had been ground to mud by the passage of armies.

They marched in column, the standard formation for movement: four abreast, rifles on shoulders, the column stretching for more than a mile. The pace was steady but not fast — about two and a half miles an hour, with ten-minute rests every hour — and the men fell into the rhythm of it the way men fall into the rhythm of any repetitive physical task, their bodies moving automatically while their minds wandered.

Alexander's mind wandered to Champlain. It usually did, on marches. The rhythmic monotony of walking freed his thoughts from the present, and they flew north like homing pigeons, settling on the kitchen table and the stone wall and the orchard and the faces he had not seen since November.

He thought about his mother, alone on the farm. He thought about Fannie, teaching at the academy. He thought about Charles and James, still in training, not yet shipped out. He thought about Henry, somewhere in Virginia with the 34th New York, closer now than he had been in months — perhaps close enough to find, if Alexander could get permission to leave his regiment for a few hours.

He did not know that the thought of finding Henry would become one of the most important events of his war.

He did not know that the finding would be the last.

---

They reached Harpers Ferry in late May.

The town sat at the confluence of the Potomac and Shenandoah rivers, pinched between hills that rose sharply on three sides — Maryland Heights to the north, Loudoun Heights to the south, Bolivar Heights to the west. It was a natural fortress or a natural trap, depending on who held the high ground. The Union held it, for now, but the Confederates wanted it badly, and the hills around the town had changed hands so many times that the locals had stopped keeping count.

Alexander arrived and looked up at the heights and understood, with the soldier's instinct he was developing, that this was a place where things happened. Not the slow things of railroad duty — not the trains and the bridges and the endless standing guard — but the fast things, the explosive things, the things that left marks on the ground and the men who fought over it.

He was right.

Within three days, the batteries opened.

*Our batteries opened on the rebels strongly yesterday and set them scampering in fine style from some heights they had the boldness to show themselves on.*

The shells arced over the town, over Alexander's head, over the camp where his company slept on their arms every night, and the sound of them — the shriek and howl that he would describe in his letter of May 31 — was like nothing he had ever heard. Not the thunder of the twelve-pound brass cannon at Camp Wheeler, which had been loud but contained, a sound you expected. This was wilder, more violent, a sound that tore the air open and left it ragged.

He loved it.

*It does me good to hear our shells shriek and howl over our heads.*

There it was — the sentence that set Alexander apart from the men who merely endured the war and placed him among the men who were, in some complicated and not entirely comfortable way, alive in it. The shells terrified him, certainly. He was not stupid, and a man would have to be stupid not to be terrified by artillery passing over his head. But the terror was shot through with something else — exhilaration, adrenaline, the fierce excitement of a young man discovering that he is not a coward.

He had wondered. Every soldier wondered. You could not know until the shells came, until the bullets sang, until the ground shook and the smoke rolled and the man beside you fell and you either stood or ran. You could not know in advance which man you would be.

Alexander stood.

He stood on the picket line while rebel shot struck within ten rods of him. He stood in the camp while they slept on their arms, ready to fight at a moment's notice. He stood on guard while the town was shelled to drive out rebel sharpshooters, the few remaining civilians warned to evacuate, the buildings shuddering as the shells struck.

*Such is war,* he wrote. And meant it without irony — this is what it is, this is what I signed up for, this is the thing Henry told me I was too young for, and here I am, and I am not too young, and I am not afraid.

He was eighteen years old. He had been a soldier for eight months. And the war — the real war, the one he had traveled six hundred miles to find — had finally found him.

*My life is in God's hands,* he wrote. And then, at the bottom of the letter, in a postscript that carried more pride than anything in the body of the letter:

*(Corporal Hubbell)*

He had earned the right to sign his rank. And the shells over Harpers Ferry had earned him the right to something else — the knowledge that he could stand under fire and not break, that the faith his mother had given him and the courage he had brought from Champlain were sufficient to the demands of the war.

He did not know that the demands would increase. He did not know that the shells over Harpers Ferry were a rehearsal, a warm-up, a gentle introduction to a violence that would build and build until it consumed everything — his brother, his health, his youth, and nearly his life.

But for now, in the May sunshine, with the shells arcing overhead and the rebels scampering and the batteries thundering, Alexander was alive and whole and proud, and the beautiful Enfield in his hands was no longer a ceremonial object but a working tool, and the war was no longer something he read about in letters and newspapers but something he stood inside, shoulder-deep, breathing its smoke.

He wrote to his mother. He told her about the shells. He told her about the shelling of the town. He told her about the rebel deserter who surrendered himself. He told her about the picket duty, the shot that struck within ten rods.

He did not tell her that he loved it. He did not need to. She could read between the lines — she had always been able to read between the lines — and what she read there, in the spring of 1862, was a son who was becoming a soldier in truth and not merely in name.

It frightened her more than the casualty lists.

A son in danger was terrible. A son who was thriving in danger was something else entirely.

She opened her Bible. She found the Psalm. She read it again.

*Thou shalt not be afraid for the terror by night; nor for the arrow that flieth by day.*

She prayed that it was true.

She prayed harder that he would never have cause to test it.

But the test was coming. It was four months away, and its name was Antietam, and it would change everything.
