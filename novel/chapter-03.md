## Chapter 3: The Beautiful Enfield

---

The Hudson River at three in the morning was black glass.

Alexander stood on deck in the cold, unable to sleep, watching the banks slide past like the walls of a great corridor. The regiment was aboard the steamer and two barges, more than eight hundred men packed into spaces designed for freight, and below decks the noise was impossible — snoring, arguing, singing, the particular animal sounds of men who had been drinking since Albany. But up here, in the open air, with the river stretching out on either side and the stars sharp as bayonet points overhead, there was a kind of peace that Alexander had never experienced.

They had passed West Point in the darkest hours. He had barely seen it — a scatter of lights on the bluff, the silhouette of buildings against the sky — but knowing it was there, the place where officers were made, where the science of war was taught in classrooms instead of on hemlock-board berths, had stirred something in him. Henry had wanted him to go to college. This was not college. But it was education.

By the time dawn came, the river had widened and the scenery had changed. The banks rose into cliffs and then fell away into rolling hills, and the light coming off the water was the pale gold of early November, thin and clean. Alexander watched it all with the particular attention of a young man seeing the world for the first time — which is what he was. He had never been south of Plattsburgh before this week. Every mile of the journey was new territory, and he recorded it with the hunger of someone who knows that what he is seeing matters and may not come again.

*The scenery on the river is the most beautiful I ever saw.*

---

New York City was overwhelming.

They arrived on a Sunday morning and lay anchored in the harbor until Monday evening, the skyline of Manhattan shimmering across the water like a fever dream. When they finally disembarked, they marched into the city proper — eight hundred men in column, rifles on their shoulders, drums beating — and found themselves in a square dominated by an equestrian statue of George Washington.

The ceremony was brief and grand. The regiment was drawn into a hollow square around the statue, and a star-spangled banner was presented — one of those immense garrison flags that looked, when unfurled, like a piece of the sky had torn loose and been caught by human hands. A "beautiful bay horse" was given to the Colonel. The crowd cheered. The men cheered back.

Then they were marched to another street for another presentation, another banner, more cheering. New York gave them something to look at, Alexander noted — flags and horses and the spectacle of martial display. It was impressive but ultimately insubstantial, like a meal made entirely of dessert.

And then — the rifles.

Each man received a weapon: "a beautiful Enfield rifle and sword bayonet." Alexander wrote the words with the kind of reverent precision that suggested the moment meant more to him than the flags or the horse or the cheering crowd. The Enfield was a serious piece of engineering — a .577 caliber rifled musket, effective at ranges that would have been fantasy with the smoothbores of his grandfather's era. It was heavy, well-balanced, and deadly accurate, and holding it changed something in the way a man stood. The recruits who had been shuffling through New York like tourists were suddenly soldiers. The weight in their hands was the weight of purpose.

Alexander did not describe his feelings about the rifle in the letter. He didn't need to. The fact that he called it "beautiful" — the same word he would later use, three years into the war, to describe a bullet whistling past his head — said everything.

---

They left New York Monday evening, crossing to Jersey City and taking the cars south. The train moved through the night, the landscape invisible beyond the windows, the car rocking with a rhythm that should have been soporific but was not — the men were too keyed up, too aware that every mile was a mile closer to the war.

Philadelphia arrived at two in the morning.

And here Alexander's letter took on a warmth that the New York paragraphs had lacked. An "excellent breakfast" was waiting for them — beef, sweet potatoes, coffee, cheese, sauerkraut, bread and butter. After weeks of Army rations, the food was almost shockingly good, the kind of substantial, home-cooked meal that reminded every man in the regiment of a kitchen he had recently left.

But it was what came after breakfast that lodged deepest in Alexander's memory.

*After breakfast we were taken to another room where there was every convenience for washing.*

Not weapons. Not flags. Not speeches. Soap and water and a place to clean themselves after three days on trains and boats and barges. The Quaker City understood what New York did not — that what soldiers need most is not spectacle but care.

*I shall always think with pleasure of the Quaker City. At New York they gave us something to look at, but at Philadelphia they gave us something better pleasing to hungry soldiers — something to eat.*

The distinction was characteristic. Alexander valued substance over show, the practical over the performative. It was a farm-country sensibility: you could admire a neighbor's painted fence, but what mattered was whether his cows were healthy and his hay was in. Philadelphia fed them and let them wash. New York gave them a parade. Alexander knew which one he'd remember at midnight on a picket line.

---

The last leg of the journey ran through Maryland.

The regiment had been warned about Baltimore. Three months earlier, on April 19, the 6th Massachusetts had marched through the city and been attacked by a pro-Confederate mob — four soldiers killed, twelve civilians dead, the first blood of the war shed not on a battlefield but on a city street. The bullet holes were still in the roof of the railroad depot. Some of the blood was still on the cobblestones, or so the rumor went.

Henry had written about his own passage through Baltimore in July — the loaded guns, the tension, the relief when the city "rather cheered" them instead of shooting. But that had been summer, when the Union's grip on Maryland was tightening. Now it was November, and the political situation had stabilized further, and yet the regiment took no chances. Alexander did not record whether guns were loaded for the transit, but the officers would have been fools not to order it, and the officers of the 60th New York were not fools.

They marched half a mile through the city — a short distance, but long enough to feel every pair of eyes on the sidewalk, to measure every window for the possible silhouette of a rifle barrel. Baltimore was Union-held but Southern-leaning, a city of divided loyalties where a man's politics could be read in his face if you knew the grammar. Alexander read the faces as he marched. He did not record what he saw.

Then they were through, and on the train again, and the landscape opened up into Maryland farmland — "very fine farms and some fine grain," as Henry had noted in July. Alexander saw it too, the professional eye of the farmer's son assessing the soil and the fences and the condition of the livestock. It was good country. It was also, in less than a year, going to be the site of the bloodiest single day in American history.

But that was September 1862, and this was November 1861, and Alexander had not yet heard of a creek called Antietam.

---

Camp Jackson was three miles from Baltimore, strung along the Baltimore & Ohio Railroad like a bead on a wire. The regiment's assignment was unglamorous but vital: guard the railroad. The B&O was the main supply line connecting Washington to the western states, a lifeline of iron and timber that the Confederates would have given anything to cut. Every bridge, every trestle, every switching yard needed men standing on it with rifles, twenty-four hours a day, in all weather.

Alexander's company took up its position and began the work that would occupy them for the next five months — the long, cold, tedious, essential work of guarding things. Standing on bridges at midnight. Walking the line in rain. Watching the approaches for saboteurs, deserters, spies, or ordinary travelers who had wandered too close to military property.

It was not the war Alexander had imagined.

The war he had imagined — the war of the newspapers and the recruiting posters and the equestrian statues — was a thing of charges and countercharges, of flags planted on hilltops, of decisive moments when brave men seized the day. The war he got was a thing of railroad timetables and password protocols, of standing in the dark counting the seconds until his relief arrived, of meals eaten cold because the cook fire would give away the position.

He did not complain. He was not the complaining kind. But the gap between expectation and reality produced its own kind of homesickness — not for a place, exactly, but for a version of the war that existed only in the imagination of people who had never been in one.

---

The weather turned cold, then warm, then cold again. Maryland's climate was disorienting for a boy from the Canadian border — November in Baltimore felt like September in Champlain, and December brought days so warm that Alexander was "really uncomfortable on account of the heat." The rhythms of the season were wrong. The light was wrong. The trees were wrong — they kept their leaves too long, and when the leaves finally fell, they fell all at once, as if embarrassed to have lingered.

Alexander adapted. He adapted to the weather, to the railroad work, to the food, to the noise, to the company of men who were not his family and did not share his values and yet, by the alchemy of shared experience, were becoming something like brothers. He adapted to the sound of trains in the night, which replaced the sound of wind in the orchard. He adapted to the smell of coal smoke, which replaced the smell of woodsmoke. He adapted to the absence of his mother's voice, which nothing replaced.

And he wrote.

The letters came steadily now — every few days, sometimes every day, pages of close-written script in a hand that was growing more confident with practice. He wrote to his mother, to Fannie, to James, to Charles, sometimes to all of them in a single letter, sometimes addressing them separately, always conscious of his audience, always selecting the truth that each person needed to hear.

To his mother, he wrote about faith and money and health: *I try to keep my promise to you "to do nothing of which you would not approve." I feel that God is very near me. I sometimes feel very lonely, but I think of Him and you fighting for me and I feel better.*

To Fannie, he wrote about the officers and the daily adventures: the boiler explosion that nearly killed Captain Ransom, the details of guard duty, the personalities of the men in his company.

To James and Charles, he wrote about military matters: the drill, the equipment, the tactical situation, the rumors that filtered through camp like water through gravel.

Each letter was a performance — not in the sense of being false, but in the sense of being shaped. Alexander was learning that a letter home was not a diary entry. It was an act of love conducted through the medium of ink and paper, and like all acts of love, it required attention to the needs of the beloved. His mother needed reassurance. His sister needed connection. His brothers needed information. He gave each of them what they needed, and in doing so, he became the family's switchboard — the node through which information flowed, the keeper of the network that held the Hubbells together across the widening distances of war.

---

Pay day came on a Friday in early December.

Alexander received seventeen dollars and thirty-three cents — his wages through the first of November. He kept two dollars and thirty-three cents. He sent the rest home.

Fifteen dollars.

It was not a fortune. It was not enough to pay the taxes, or to fix the barn roof, or to buy the seed for spring planting. But it was everything Alexander had, minus what he needed to survive, and he sent it with instructions that were as fierce as any order given on a battlefield.

*Now, my dear Mother, I give it all to you. This is the first time that I have been able to make my Mother a present and I want you to take it. If you do not I shall feel very, very bad. I want you to buy you a nice warm cloak for winter or else a warm woolen dress and a warm pair of gaiters. Now if you disobey my orders I shall feel very much like having you courtmartialed. It is rather a bad thing to fall into the hands of such a court. You had not better disobey. I give you fair warning.*

The passage was the most Alexander thing in the entire collection — playful and earnest simultaneously, the threat of court-martial delivered with a wink, the love underneath it absolute and unironic. He had been a soldier for ten weeks and was already fluent in the Army's language of command, and he deployed it here not to assert authority but to express devotion. The boy who could not join a fraternal society without his mother's permission was now ordering his mother to buy herself a warm cloak, and the reversal — the son commanding the parent, the child providing for the elder — was the first of many small revolutions the war would work on the Hubbell family's hierarchy.

Frances would receive the money. Whether she bought the cloak was not recorded. Knowing Frances, she more likely put it toward the taxes or the spring seed and wore the old cloak another year. She was not a woman who spent money on herself when there were obligations to meet.

But Alexander had made his gesture. He had converted his body's labor — the hours of standing guard, the drill, the cold, the discomfort, the distance from home — into fifteen dollars, and he had sent them home like a letter made of currency, a love note denominated in greenbacks. It would not be the last time. Over the next four years, he would send money whenever he could, tracking his pay with the precision of an accountant and his mother's needs with the anxiety of a son who knows that the farm depends on soldiers' wages to survive.

---

Two days after pay day, a man in Alexander's company nearly killed himself.

David Davaway was on picket duty at a bridge below the camp. Stepping off a wall, he stumbled and dropped his rifle. He grabbed for it with his right hand, but before he could catch it, the weapon struck a stone and discharged. The ball passed through his right thumb between the first and second joints and nearly grazed his head.

His thumb was taken off. He would be discharged.

Alexander recorded the incident with the matter-of-fact tone of someone reporting the weather, but he added a detail that revealed how deeply it had affected him: *When I go out with my reliefs, I am oblige to caution the men with "men be careful how you carry your rifles." They sometimes carry them very carelessly.*

He was a private — the lowest rank in the Army — and he was cautioning men about rifle safety. No one had asked him to do this. No one had authorized it. He did it because he had seen what carelessness could do, and because his instinct, always, was to prevent harm rather than merely witness it. It was the same instinct that would make him a corporal, then a sergeant, then the man his officers trusted to lead a skirmish line or rally a faltering squad.

Leadership, Alexander was discovering, was not a rank. It was a reflex.

---

The letter closed with a sentence that Frances would have read with more feeling than any passage about money or rifles or prayer meetings:

*Tell Frances I shall never forget the text she wrote in my bible. "God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble." He is such indeed to me.*

Fannie had inscribed the verse before Alexander left home — a small act, a pen moving across a flyleaf, a sister's prayer compressed into twelve words. Alexander carried it with him like an amulet, lighter than the Enfield but more powerful, a weapon against the particular enemy that no rifle could engage: the loneliness of a seventeen-year-old boy three hundred miles from home, standing guard on a railroad bridge in Maryland, watching for enemies who never came, waiting for a war that had not yet arrived.

He did not know that the war would arrive soon enough.

He did not know that within six months he would hear shells shriek over his head at Harpers Ferry and write home about it with a kind of terrified delight.

He did not know that within a year he would kneel on the field at Antietam, searching for his brother among the dead, and not find him.

He knew only what he knew: that God was his refuge, that his mother was fighting for him in prayer, that fifteen dollars was on its way to Champlain for a warm cloak, and that when he went out with his reliefs he cautioned the men to carry their rifles carefully, because carelessness killed as surely as the enemy, and often sooner.

He folded the letter. He addressed it. He gave it to the company clerk for the morning mail.

Then he picked up his beautiful Enfield and went out to stand his post.
