# PART FOUR: THE LONG ROAD
*January 1863 – November 1863*

---

## Chapter 11: Mud Season

---

The Army of the Potomac spent the winter of 1862-63 doing what armies do when they cannot fight: they sat in the mud and waited.

Alexander's regiment was camped near Fairfax Station, then near Stafford Court House, then near Aquia Creek Landing — each camp indistinguishable from the last, a collection of canvas tents arranged in rows on ground that had been churned to mud by the passage of a hundred thousand men and their horses and their wagons and their guns. The mud was everywhere. It was in the food, in the clothing, in the bedding, in the letters home. It was a presence as constant and as demoralizing as the cold, and the cold was constant, and the demoralization was building.

Alexander endured it. Endurance was his default mode now — not the eager enthusiasm of Camp Wheeler, not the exhilarated fear of Harpers Ferry, but a steady, low-grade persistence that would carry him through the next two and a half years of war. He had learned what Henry had learned before him: how not to mind.

*I have got so I don't mind it much.*

The words were Henry's, from a letter written a lifetime ago, and Alexander heard them in his head sometimes, not as a memory but as a lesson, the most important thing his brother had taught him.

---

The letters from this period were regular — one every week or two — but subdued. Alexander wrote about rations, weather, mail delays, troop movements. The language was cooler, more measured, less prone to the exclamation points and emotional outbursts that had characterized his earlier correspondence. He was learning the art of the soldier's letter: say enough to reassure, not so much that it alarms.

The mail problem continued to plague him. Letters from home went astray, delayed by the chaos of the Army's postal system or — Alexander suspected — stolen by thieves who rifled envelopes looking for cash.

*Mother, during the last two months I have received but two letters from home. I have felt so bad that I had but small heart to write.*

The sentence was the saddest kind of confession: he had stopped writing because he was not being written to. The circulatory system of letters — the network that kept the family alive across the distances of war — had developed a blockage, and the oxygen was not getting through, and Alexander was suffocating in silence.

His mother, receiving this letter, would have been stricken. She had been writing. She had been writing constantly. But the letters were not arriving, lost somewhere in the postal system's vast incompetence, and her son was sitting in a camp in Virginia believing that his mother had forgotten him.

*Mother, could you for a moment suppose I could ever forget my old home and my Mother? Never, never. I love my Mother better than life itself.*

The sentence exploded from the page like a shell — sudden, intense, carrying a force that seemed disproportionate to the question it answered. Of course Frances had not supposed he had forgotten her. She had supposed nothing of the kind. But the fear of being forgotten — the particular terror of a soldier whose connection to home depends entirely on the mail, and whose mail has stopped coming — had lodged in Alexander like shrapnel, and the removal of it required words of equal force.

*It is thoughts of you and my old home that have sustained me in our long marches. No, Mother, I can never forget you.*

He was nineteen years old. He had been a soldier for a year and a half. He had survived Antietam and the hospital and the loss of his brother, and the thing that sustained him — the thing that kept him marching, kept him fighting, kept him alive — was the thought of his mother at the kitchen table in Champlain.

It was both beautiful and terrible: beautiful because the love was real and powerful and life-sustaining; terrible because the distance that separated them was growing, and the war showed no sign of ending, and Alexander could not protect his mother from old Rye or the taxes or the loneliness any more than Frances could protect him from the bullets.

---

Spring 1863 brought new generals and new hope.

Hooker had taken command of the Army of the Potomac, replacing Burnside after the disaster at Fredericksburg. The army reorganized, re-equipped, and began to stir. Alexander felt the change.

*The "Army of the Potomac" was on the move day before yesterday. Gen'l. Hooker means to give the two years men an opportunity to pay a parting salute to the rebels. The Army is in fine condition and spirits.*

The optimism was real but qualified — Alexander had learned that optimism was a renewable resource that had to be managed carefully, like ammunition. You could not spend it all on one battle, because there was always another battle coming.

*Please, for my sake, box the ears of the first person you hear say they will not fight.*

The contempt for civilians who doubted the army's will — the "fireside patriots" of his earlier letter — had not diminished. If anything, it had hardened. Alexander was deep enough into the war now to have earned the right to an opinion, and his opinion was clear: the army would fight. The question was not whether the soldiers had the will but whether the generals had the competence.

Hooker, it turned out, did not. Chancellorsville, in early May, was another Union defeat — a battle where tactical advantage was squandered by indecision, where the rebels under Lee and Jackson outmaneuvered an army twice their size. Alexander's regiment was engaged, though the letters did not describe the fighting in detail. The defeat was demoralizing but not crushing — the army had been beaten before, and it would march again.

---

Then came Gettysburg.

The march north began in June, the army following Lee's invasion of Pennsylvania with the urgent, shuffling speed of men who know they are racing toward something decisive. Alexander marched through the familiar landscape of Virginia and Maryland, through the fields where he had searched for Henry less than a year ago, through the towns he had passed on his way south from Camp Wheeler, and then across the Pennsylvania line into country he had never seen.

They arrived near Gettysburg on July 1, with the battle already in progress.

July 2, 1863. The regiment went in.

*God has again in his great kindness preserved my life through another most terrific battle. I have not received a scratch.*

Again. The word carried the weight of precedent — Antietam first, now Gettysburg, the 60th New York walking into the fire for a second time, and Alexander walking out of it for a second time, unmarked, the ball that should have found him spending itself against a tree or a stone or the body of the man next to him.

*The reg't. lost fifty-two — eleven killed and forty-some wounded.*

Fifty-two men. A quarter of the regiment's remaining strength, or close to it. The numbers were becoming a kind of arithmetic of attrition, each battle subtracting a fraction of the whole, the regiment shrinking like a bar of soap with each use.

*We commenced fighting about 2 P.M. on the 2nd inst. and continued fighting until about midnight.*

Ten hours. Ten hours of combat, from the hot afternoon through the long July twilight and into the darkness, the fighting continuing by muzzle flash and the light of burning fences and the stars overhead. Alexander compressed ten hours into one sentence. He did not describe the fighting. He did not describe the fear, the noise, the dying. He wrote:

*If God spares my life, I will endeavor to write you again soon and give you particulars.*

The particulars never came. Like Antietam, Gettysburg was too large for letters. Alexander could describe a boiler explosion, a moonlit reunion, a prayer meeting with a shouting man. He could not describe a battle. The experience exceeded the medium.

*We are burying our and the rebel's dead today.*

July 4, 1863. Independence Day. The army buried the dead — Union and Confederate both, the blue and the grey together in the Pennsylvania ground — while the nation celebrated its birthday. The irony was either perfect or obscene, depending on your proximity to the graves.

*Love to all.*

*Your aff. Son,*

*Alex F. Hubbell*

*P.S. Our Captain deserves especial praise for his bravery.*

Even at Gettysburg, even standing over the graves, Alexander remembered to praise Captain Ransom. The loyalty was instinctive — a farm-country loyalty, the kind you give to the man who leads you well, the kind that once given is never withdrawn.

---

After Gettysburg, the war shifted.

The eastern theater — Virginia, Maryland, Pennsylvania — would continue to grind, but the decisive action was moving west. Tennessee, Georgia, Alabama — the Confederacy's heartland, where Sherman was gathering his armies and Grant was tightening his grip on the Mississippi. The Union high command was reorganizing, moving troops from east to west, feeding fresh units into the western campaigns.

The 60th New York received its orders in September 1863: transfer to the Army of the Cumberland. Tennessee. A different war, in a different landscape, under different generals.

Alexander packed his kit — the letters, the Bible, the Enfield — and prepared to move. He was leaving the theater where Henry had died, the fields where he had searched for his brother, the camps where he had written his most powerful letters. He was going west, into country he had never seen, to fight a war he did not yet understand.

But before he left, he had one more experience that the letters preserved — an experience so quiet and so human that it stood out from the thunder of Gettysburg like a wildflower growing from a shell crater.

---

Ellis Ford, on the Rapidan River, September 1863.

The river was about a hundred yards wide and very shallow. On one bank stood the Union pickets. On the other stood the Confederate pickets. They were close enough to talk, close enough to see each other's faces, close enough to trade newspapers if the officers allowed it — which they did not.

Alexander stood his post and looked across the water at the enemy. The enemy looked back.

*We sit and look at each other very composedly as we have both learned better manners than to fire at each other.*

The sentence was remarkable. "Better manners." As if the war were a social occasion and the soldiers had progressed, through months of shared misery, from rudeness to courtesy. The pickets had reached an understanding that their generals had not: that shooting at the man across the river served no purpose except to get shot back at, and that the two of them — the Union corporal from New York and the Confederate private from Virginia or Georgia or God-knew-where — had more in common with each other than either had with the politicians who had started the war.

*The rebels are very anxious to exchange papers with us, but we have strict orders not to do it.*

The officers forbade fraternization. The officers were right to forbid it, from a military standpoint — you could not ask a man to kill someone he had just traded newspapers with. But the instinct to connect — the same instinct that drove Alexander to write letters and Frances to demand them — was stronger than orders, and the pickets found ways around the prohibition. They shouted across the water. They tossed tobacco. They were, for a few days on the Rapidan, something like neighbors.

*If the settling of the war was left to the soldiers we would do it in short order.*

Alexander wrote that without irony and perhaps without fully understanding its implications. The soldiers would end the war because the soldiers understood its cost in a way the civilians and politicians did not. The soldiers had buried the dead at Gettysburg on the Fourth of July. The soldiers had stood in the hospital at Harpers Ferry and begged for bread. The soldiers had searched for their brothers on the Antietam field and not found them.

*Some of the Rebs are pretty nice fellows, much to my surprise.*

The surprise was genuine. Alexander had been raised to see the rebellion as treason, the rebels as traitors, the enemy as something less than human. The war — and specifically the quiet hours on the picket line at Ellis Ford, sitting and looking at the enemy and finding that the enemy was a man who wanted to trade newspapers and go home — was complicating that view.

It was not yet the political awakening that would come later, the transformation from abolitionist-hater to Lincoln voter. But it was the beginning of it. Alexander was learning, at Ellis Ford, that the enemy was not an abstraction. The enemy was a man on the other side of a shallow river, standing guard in the same heat, eating the same bad food, missing the same kind of home.

---

The orders came. The regiment moved.

Alexander left Virginia in October 1863, heading west by rail, through Washington and across the Appalachians and down into Tennessee. The landscape changed: the rolling hills of the Virginia Piedmont gave way to the sharp, forested ridges of the Cumberland Mountains, the rivers wider, the sky bigger, the distances vaster than anything Alexander had known.

*I have been in the state of Alabama,* he wrote to his mother in November, from a place called White Side, Georgia. *Wonder what Aunt Wry would say if she knew my vandal footsteps had pressed the soil of Alabama.*

The humor was light but the homesickness was heavy. Alexander was further from Champlain than he had ever been, in a landscape that bore no resemblance to the farms and orchards of Clinton County, fighting under generals he did not know in a campaign he did not yet understand.

But he was alive. He was healthy. He was writing letters.

And the war — the long, grinding, seemingly endless war — was about to enter its final and most terrible phase.

Sherman was coming. Atlanta was waiting. And Alexander's regiment was one of the instruments Sherman would use to break the Confederacy's spine.

Alexander did not know this yet. He knew only that he was in Georgia, that the mountains were different from any he had seen, and that his mother's letters — when they came, when the mail worked, when the postal system managed to forward a piece of paper from Champlain to White Side via Washington and Chattanooga — were the only things that made the distance bearable.

He opened his Bible. He read the Psalm.

He wrote home.
