# PART FIVE: THE FURNACE
*December 1863 – December 1864*

---

## Chapter 13: The Blues

---

*Jan. 8, 1864*
*Lolliecoffer House*
*Nashville, Tenn.*

The letter began with an apology, which was unusual. Alexander was not, as a rule, an apologetic man. He acknowledged faults when they existed — bad spelling, late responses, insufficient detail — but he did not apologize for them with the particular heaviness that opened this January letter.

*I know you think I am a very naughty boy not to write you oftener, but as I have said before, it is hard for me to write when I get no answers to my letters.*

The mail problem again. Months of silence from home, months of letters sent into a void. But this time the explanation carried an undertone that was new: Alexander was not just frustrated. He was defeated.

*I feel quite sad tonight. I suppose Frances would say that I have the blues.*

It was the only time in 120 letters — the only time in the entire collection of 274 — that Alexander admitted to something like despair. He had been homesick before, lonely before, frustrated and furious and frightened before. But sad — that simple, devastating word — was new. And the framing of it through Frances's imagined response ("Frances would say that I have the blues") was itself a kind of deflection, a way of acknowledging the feeling while keeping it at arm's length, as if naming it in his sister's voice rather than his own made it less real.

*I shall wait until I feel a little more cheerful to finish the letter.*

He set the pen down. He did not finish the letter that night.

---

The circumstances explained some of it. Alexander was in Nashville, separated from his regiment, trying to rejoin his unit through a military system that was vast, indifferent, and maddeningly bureaucratic. He had left Chattanooga "clandestinely" — an extraordinary word for an extraordinary act. Alexander, the model soldier, the man who had never broken a rule in his life, had gone AWOL.

*To accomplish this I was obliged to leave Chattanooga clandestinely. I suppose I am reported at my detachment as a deserter, but this will do me no harm as I shall be able to join the regt. before my report can reach there.*

He had smuggled himself onto a troop train, talked his way past the commanders at Nashville, and was now waiting for transportation to Savannah, Georgia, where his regiment was stationed. The casual confidence of it — "this will do me no harm" — masked what must have been a considerable anxiety. Desertion was a capital offense. Men were shot for it. Alexander had calculated the risk and decided that the dishonor of desertion was preferable to the dishonor of sitting in a detachment doing nothing while his regiment fought without him.

*I did not like the way things worked and determined to get to the regt. if it took a leg.*

"If it took a leg." The phrase was grim and literal in a war where legs were taken with regularity. Alexander was willing to risk a court-martial, risk the firing squad, risk the bureaucratic machinery of the Army coming down on him like a millstone — anything to get back to his company, his regiment, his war.

This was not bravado. It was need. Alexander needed his regiment the way he needed his mother's letters — as a fixed point, a home within the homelessness of war. Without the regiment he was just a soldier; with it, he was Sergeant Hubbell of Company H, 60th New York, and the identity that rank and unit provided was the only identity that worked in a world where everything else — home, family, certainty, safety — had been stripped away.

---

The months between January and April 1864 were the quietest of Alexander's war.

He made his way south to Stevenson, Alabama, where the regiment was refitting for the spring campaign. The weather improved. The mail resumed. Letters from home arrived — slowly, irregularly, but they arrived — and with them came the oxygen that Alexander's emotional life required.

He wrote to his mother with something approaching calm. The letters were shorter, more measured, less freighted with anxiety. He asked about James and Charles. He sent regards to neighbors. He mentioned the weather.

*I shall wait until I feel a little more cheerful to finish the letter.*

He had felt more cheerful. The blues had lifted, or at least retreated to the place where they waited between episodes, and Alexander was functional again — writing, drilling, preparing for whatever came next.

What came next was Sherman.

---

By April, the preparations were unmistakable. Troops were massing at Stevenson and Bridgeport and the other staging points along the railroad from Nashville to Chattanooga. Supply trains ran constantly. New regiments arrived from the east. The Army of the Cumberland, the Army of the Tennessee, and the Army of the Ohio — three armies, more than 100,000 men — were converging on the mountains of north Georgia.

Alexander felt it. He wrote about it with a mixture of anticipation and dread that was the emotional signature of an experienced soldier — a man who wanted the campaign to start because waiting was worse than fighting, but who knew, from Antietam and Gettysburg, exactly what fighting cost.

*Battles will probably be fought in a few weeks that will not be a whit behind, and perhaps throw completely in the shade the battles of Antietam and Gettysburg.*

He was right. The Atlanta Campaign, which began on May 7, 1864, and continued without pause until September, would be the longest sustained combat operation of the war — four months of daily fighting, nightly entrenchment, and relentless forward movement that would destroy the Confederate Army of Tennessee and break the Confederacy's industrial heartland.

Alexander would be in the middle of it.

*May God be with us and grant victory to our arms.*

The prayer was sincere. It was also practical. Alexander knew what was coming, and he knew that God's presence, while essential, was no guarantee of survival. The ninety-first Psalm promised protection. Antietam had proven that the promise came with exceptions.

*I hope, dear Mother, that you will not worry yourself about me. Our Father in Heaven is watching over us all and nothing will happen to us but what is for the best.*

"Nothing will happen to us but what is for the best." It was the theology of a man who had lost his brother at Antietam and emerged from the experience with his faith intact but his understanding of "best" permanently expanded to include outcomes that no human heart would choose.

*I hope I shall come safely out of the struggle — if not, I trust that we shall all meet in the better world.*

There it was. The sentence that soldiers write before major campaigns, the sentence that mothers read and cannot answer, the sentence that manages to be both resignation and hope simultaneously. If I die, we'll meet in heaven. If I don't, I'll see you at the tea table.

Alexander folded the letter. He addressed it. He gave it to the mail clerk.

Then he shouldered his rifle and marched south.

---

*It is the death struggle of this mighty rebellion.*

He wrote that on April 28, 1864 — the last letter from Stevenson, the last letter before the furnace. It was the most portentous sentence he ever wrote, and it was accurate. The Atlanta Campaign was the death struggle. Not of the rebellion alone, but of the men who fought it, on both sides, the armies grinding against each other day after day in the Georgia heat until the grinding itself became a kind of conclusion.

Alexander marched into it at twenty years old. He had been a soldier for two and a half years. He had survived two major battles, two hospitalizations, the loss of his brother, and the blues. He was tough and experienced and faithful and afraid, and the fear was the right kind — not the paralyzing kind that stopped a man in his tracks, but the galvanizing kind that sharpened every sense and quickened every reflex.

He was ready.

The furnace was ready too.
