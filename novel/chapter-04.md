## Chapter 4: The First Money

---

The Trent Affair broke in December like a crack in ice, sudden and widening.

A United States Navy captain named Charles Wilkes had boarded a British mail steamer, the *Trent,* in international waters and removed two Confederate diplomats — James Mason and John Slidell — who were traveling to Europe to seek recognition for the Confederacy. The act was legally dubious and diplomatically catastrophic. Britain, the greatest naval power in the world, was furious. War between the United States and Great Britain — a war the Union could not possibly fight while already fighting the Confederacy — seemed, for several weeks in December 1861, genuinely possible.

The news arrived at Camp Jackson the way news arrived everywhere that winter: in newspapers that were days old and rumors that were hours old and neither of which could be entirely trusted. The men debated it at meals, on guard duty, in the margins of letters home. The older soldiers, the ones who remembered the Mexican War and had opinions about international law, argued about the legality of Wilkes's action. The younger ones, who had opinions about nothing except wanting to fight someone, argued about whether they could beat the British.

Alexander, at seventeen, had no doubt.

*I am as ready to fight Britons as Southerners.*

He wrote it to his mother with the breezy confidence of a young man who had never fired his beautiful Enfield at anything that could fire back. The sentence was absurd in its way — a private in a New York infantry regiment declaring his readiness to take on the Royal Navy — but it was also revealing. Alexander's patriotism was not yet complicated. It was a simple, fierce, undifferentiated thing, like a bonfire before it has been shaped into a hearth. He loved his country the way he loved his mother: totally, reflexively, without analysis. Anyone who threatened either one would find him standing in the way with his bayonet fixed.

The crisis passed. Lincoln's government, guided by Secretary of State William Seward, released Mason and Slidell and allowed them to continue to Europe. The diplomatic language was exquisite — the United States acknowledged that Wilkes had acted without authorization while simultaneously avoiding any admission of wrongdoing — and the British, satisfied that their sovereignty had been respected, stood down. War was averted.

Alexander was disappointed. Not because he wanted more enemies — one was sufficient — but because the brief flare of the Trent Affair had been exciting in a way that guard duty on the Baltimore & Ohio Railroad was not. He wanted something to happen. He was seventeen and armed and thousands of miles from home, and the war — the real war, the war of battles and campaigns and the things he would tell his mother about at the tea table — remained stubbornly elsewhere.

It would come to him soon enough. But first: Christmas.

---

Christmas 1861 was the first Christmas Alexander had spent away from home, and the absence of home was not an abstraction but a physical sensation, a heaviness in the chest that could not be relieved by prayer or drill or letters.

He thought about the kitchen in Champlain. The stove would be burning hard against the December cold, and the windows would be fogged with the steam of cooking, and his mother would be making something — she was always making something, her hands never idle, the Scottish pragmatism that kept the farm running also keeping the household in constant motion. Fannie would be there, perhaps, or perhaps at the academy where she taught. James and Charles would be at the table, doing whatever James and Charles did in the evenings — James reading, probably, Charles whittling or mending something.

And the chair where Henry used to sit would be empty, as it had been since June.

Alexander wrote letters. He wrote to his mother, to James, to Charles, to Fannie, each letter a thread thrown across the distance, a cable in the suspension bridge that connected Camp Jackson to Champlain. The letters were his lifeline, and he understood this with a clarity that increased with each one he sent. Without letters, the distance would kill him — not his body, which was young and strong and fed adequately by the Army, but something inside him that needed to hear from home the way his lungs needed air.

*Send me papers as often as you can. We have nothing to read here.*

The request appeared in letter after letter, a refrain as persistent as a hymn. He wanted newspapers from Champlain, from Plattsburgh, from anywhere in Clinton County — not for the war news, which he could get from the Baltimore papers, but for the local intelligence: who had married, who had died, who had moved away, what the weather was doing to the roads. He wanted to know that the world he had left still existed, that the farm and the village and the people he loved were not dissolving in his absence like a dream that fades on waking.

Frances sent papers. She sent them faithfully, week after week, for the entire war. She also sent letters that were dense with instruction, interrogation, and the particular brand of love that expressed itself through demands.

*Write, write, write, write, write, write, write, WRITE.*

That command would come later — October 1862, after the worst had happened — but its spirit animated every letter Frances sent. Write to me. Tell me you are alive. Let me hear your voice, even if it is only ink on paper. The casualty lists are in the morning paper, and I will read them before breakfast, and if your name is not on them I will eat, and if it is — but it won't be. It can't be. God has promised. Read the ninety-first Psalm.

---

The new year arrived without ceremony. Alexander's letter of January 1, 1862, was addressed to "Family (general)" — the only time in the entire collection that he wrote to no one in particular, as if the occasion required a broadcast rather than a conversation. The letter was brief and unremarkable. Happy New Year. I am well. The weather is warm for January.

But the first weeks of 1862 brought a change in Alexander's correspondence that a careful reader — and Frances was the most careful reader in Clinton County — would have noticed immediately. The letters grew longer. More detailed. More confident.

He was finding his voice.

The boy who had written "What a Bedlam!" in September, with its breathless exclamation and knocked elbows and half-finished thoughts, was becoming a writer. His sentences lengthened. His paragraphs developed structure. His observations sharpened from the general ("very good, I think") to the specific ("the Capt. was going the Grand Rounds... one of the officers is obliged to go over the line every night between the hours of one and six"). He began to tell stories — real stories, with beginnings and middles and ends, with characters and dialogue and the kind of detail that puts a reader inside the scene.

The boiler explosion was the first of these. Captain Ransom had been riding a freight train on the Grand Rounds when the boiler burst, blowing the engineer "two or three hundred feet into the air." The engineer fell through a tree, breaking a limb three inches thick. Ransom, standing on the platform of the tender, was somehow uninjured — thrown clear, or shielded by the fireman's body, the letter was not specific. The Captain walked back to camp, shaken but whole, and resumed his duties as if nothing had happened.

Alexander told the story to Fannie in a letter that built suspense as deliberately as a novelist: *"I can almost hear you say, 'Out with it you provoking boy.' Well, 'here goes.'"* He described the mechanics of the locomotive, the position of the men, the physics of the explosion, the miraculous survival. He was showing off — for his sister, for himself, for the pleasure of turning an event into a narrative and watching it come alive on the page.

He was eighteen years old. He had been a soldier for four months. And he was becoming, without knowing it, the family's historian — the one who would record their war, letter by letter, in a voice that was equal parts journalist, poet, and devoted son.

---

January brought a flurry of letters — six in the month's first three weeks, an extraordinary rate even for Alexander. They went to his mother, to James, to Charles, to Fannie, each one a different facet of the same diamond.

To his mother on January 13: a long, careful letter about his duties and his health. The tone was reassuring but not dishonest — he acknowledged the cold, the discomfort, the boredom, but framed them as manageable challenges rather than sources of suffering. This was the art of the soldier's letter home: to tell enough truth to be credible without telling so much that the recipient cannot sleep.

To his mother on January 15: a second letter, written from Camden Station in Baltimore, about the regiment's movements. Something had changed — the regiment was shifting, reorganizing, the routine of Camp Jackson giving way to something more fluid and uncertain. Alexander described it calmly, but the frequency of his writing — two letters in two days — suggested an anxiety he would not name.

To James on January 17: a letter full of military detail, the kind of letter a brother writes to a brother who might himself be heading to the war. James was eighteen now, old enough to enlist, and Alexander's letters to him increasingly carried the weight of advance intelligence — here is what it's like, here is what to expect, here is what no one tells you.

To his mother on January 24: another long letter, this one more personal, more emotional. Alexander was homesick. The holiday season had sharpened the distance, and the January cold — even Maryland's mild January — had settled into his bones with a persistence that no fire could quite cure. He wrote about money, about clothing, about the health of the men in his company. He did not write about fear, because he did not yet have reason to fear anything more dangerous than a stumbled rifle on a bridge.

But the letters were accelerating. Six in three weeks. Something was building — whether in Alexander's circumstances or in his emotional state, the letters did not say. They said only that he was writing faster, writing more, throwing threads across the distance with a frequency that suggested the distance was growing harder to bear.

---

February and March passed in the grey monotony of railroad duty. The regiment moved from Baltimore to the Relay House, a junction point on the B&O, and established Camp Niles in the surrounding fields. The work was the same — guard the bridges, walk the line, stand picket in the cold — but the landscape changed, and with it the feeling of the war.

At the Relay House, Alexander could see the trains that carried supplies to the front. He watched them pass at all hours — long trains of boxcars and flatcars loaded with ammunition, food, blankets, boots, the material of war that flowed west like blood through an artery. And sometimes the trains came back carrying wounded men, their cars converted to ambulances, the moaning audible through the walls as the train slowed to take the junction curve.

He did not write about the wounded trains. There were things a son did not tell his mother.

But he wrote about the mail. The mail was his obsession, his joy, his torment. When letters came, he was transformed — "I hardly knew whether I was on my head or feet," he wrote after receiving four letters at once. When they didn't come, he sank into a silence that his mother read, correctly, as despair.

The mail was unreliable. Letters went astray, were delayed, were stolen, were destroyed. A letter mailed in Champlain might reach Camp Niles in four days or four weeks or never. A letter mailed from Camp Niles might reach Champlain promptly or might end up in the dead letter office in Washington, undelivered and unread, the words of a soldier to his mother dissolving slowly in a government warehouse.

Alexander endured it. He had no choice. But the frustration bled into his letters in ways that revealed the depth of his dependence on the connection.

*I have received one mailed Nov. 18, another Nov. 6th... another Dec. 3 and one from Henry mailed Dec. 4. I have not received any papers as yet.*

He tracked every letter, every newspaper, every scrap of mail from home with the precision of a bookkeeper. He noted the dates they were mailed, the dates they arrived, the gaps in the sequence. He was building a ledger of love — debits and credits, sent and received, the account always slightly overdrawn because the mail could never keep pace with the need.

---

March brought a letter from Champlain that sent Alexander into a fury.

His mother's sugar-maker — a man referred to only as "old Rye" — had been causing trouble. The details were unclear from Alexander's response, but the situation was plain enough: a man in the village was taking advantage of a soldier's widow, cheating her on the sugar-making arrangement that was one of the farm's few sources of cash income.

Alexander's reaction was volcanic.

*I was filled with great wrath when I read your acct. of your trouble with your sugar-maker. I fairly hopped up and down, I was so provoked. If there is such a thing as get satisfaction out of old Rye I will have it if ever I return. Wonder if he can find no better employment than persecuting a helpless woman.*

The passage was Alexander at his most raw — the elaborate courtesy and gentle humor of his usual letters stripped away, replaced by something hot and immediate. This was not the reflective Alexander who mused about prayer meetings or the storytelling Alexander who narrated Captain Ransom's boiler explosion. This was the protective Alexander, the son who had enlisted at least partly because he could not bear to stay home while his mother was alone, and who now learned, from three hundred miles away, that the very aloneness he had feared was being exploited.

He could do nothing. That was the particular cruelty of it. He could write a furious letter, and the letter would arrive in four days or ten days or never, and meanwhile old Rye would continue his persecutions, and Frances would continue to face them alone, and Alexander would stand on his railroad bridge with his beautiful Enfield and feel the rage cool into helplessness and the helplessness settle into guilt.

*Shame on him — though I very much doubt if he has either shame or soul. I cannot find words to express my indignation.*

He could not protect his mother. He had left home to protect his country, and in doing so he had left his mother unprotected, and the calculus of that exchange — national duty purchased with maternal vulnerability — was one that Alexander would never entirely resolve. It sat in his letters like a splinter, working its way deeper with every month of absence.

---

Spring came to Maryland early, the trees greening before the calendar said they should, and with the spring came movement. The regiment received orders. They were leaving the Relay House. They were heading west, toward the Shenandoah Valley, toward a place called Harpers Ferry that Alexander had read about in school — John Brown's Harpers Ferry, the place where the war had started before it officially started.

The orders came with the kind of urgency that meant something had changed. The Valley was heating up. Stonewall Jackson was moving through it like a brush fire, threatening Washington from the west, and the Union needed every available regiment to contain him. The 60th New York was available.

Alexander packed his kit. He cleaned his Enfield. He wrote one more letter home, shorter than usual, practical rather than emotional. He was learning the grammar of movement — how to compress a life into a knapsack, how to say goodbye to a place without ceremony, how to face a new direction without looking back.

He was eighteen now. His birthday had passed somewhere in the winter — the letters did not record it, which meant either that he had not mentioned it or that the letter mentioning it had been lost. He was eighteen, and he had been a soldier for six months, and he had not yet heard a shot fired in anger.

That was about to change.

He shouldered his pack and fell in with his company. The regiment formed on the road. The drums beat. The fifes played. The column moved.

Harpers Ferry was a hundred miles to the west, through country that was green and rolling and entirely at war.

Alexander marched toward it with his rifle on his shoulder and his mother's Psalm in his pocket, and he did not look back.
