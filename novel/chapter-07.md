## Chapter 7: Shells Over Harpers Ferry

---

The Shenandoah Valley in the summer of 1862 was a corridor of heat and movement. Armies passed through it like weather systems — Confederate forces under Jackson sweeping north, Union forces under Banks and Frémont chasing them or running from them, the valley's farms and villages caught between the two like grain between millstones.

Alexander's regiment moved with the tide. Harpers Ferry to Winchester, Winchester to Front Royal, Front Royal to Strasburg, Strasburg back again. The marches were long, the roads were bad, and the heat was merciless — a wet, pressing heat that was nothing like the dry summers of northern New York, a heat that sat on a man's shoulders like a pack and could not be put down.

*We went up Loudon Valley to Hillsborough, from there to Leesburg, thim to Fairfax Courthouse, from there to Dumfries, five miles from Aquila Creek and twenty-four from Fredericksburg.*

Alexander catalogued the movements with the precision of a surveyor. He was developing a habit that would serve him well for the rest of the war: the meticulous recording of distances, directions, and place names. His letters became maps you could follow with a finger — this town to that town, this river to that ridge, this many miles in this many days. It was the farmer's instinct translated into military terms: you always knew where you were, because knowing where you were was the first step in knowing what to do.

---

June brought a pause at Winchester, in the heart of the Valley.

Alexander stood on the heights above the town and looked south, down the length of the Shenandoah, and saw what the generals saw: a natural highway running 150 miles from Harpers Ferry to Lexington, flanked by the Blue Ridge to the east and the Alleghenies to the west, rich enough to feed an army and straight enough to move one. Whoever controlled the Valley controlled the war's western flank. The fighting for it would continue for two more years.

He wrote to his mother from Winchester, a letter that mentioned the shells and the marching and the heat but devoted most of its space to a more domestic concern: he needed clothing. The Army-issue uniform was wearing thin. The boots were developing holes. The knapsack straps had begun to fray.

*Can you send me an overcoat,* he had asked months ago. Now the requests were more specific, more urgent: boots, socks, shirts, the practical necessities that the Army provided in theory and the soldiers did without in practice. The supply system was a shambles — uniforms arrived in the wrong sizes, shoes in the wrong quantities, blankets that disintegrated after a month of use. The men who survived best were the men whose families sent them what the Army couldn't.

Frances sent packages when she could. The logistics of getting a parcel from Champlain to a moving regiment in the Shenandoah Valley were formidable — the package had to be wrapped, addressed, mailed, transported by rail to Washington, forwarded to the regiment's last known location, and then chased from camp to camp until it caught up with the intended recipient. Many packages never arrived. The boots Alexander requested in April did not reach him until — well, it was not clear they ever reached him at all. Some requests were repeated in letter after letter, a litany of need that marked the weeks: the boots, the boots, the boots.

---

July found the regiment camped on Pine Mountain, near Washington, Virginia — not the capital but a small town in the foothills of the Blue Ridge. The Valley campaign had settled into a pattern of advance and retreat, skirmish and counter-skirmish, the two armies circling each other like boxers between rounds.

Alexander wrote to Fannie. The letter was different from his letters to their mother — looser, more conversational, more willing to venture an opinion on matters that he would have kept from Frances for fear of worrying her.

He told Fannie about the men who stayed home. The "fireside patriots," he called them — men who were brave enough at a distance, who found fault with the Army's conduct from the safety of their parlors, who talked about what should be done without volunteering to do it.

*I have a very poor opinion of such men,* he wrote. *They are what I call fireside patriots. They are brave enough at home but let them come out here and you would see them whine.*

The contempt was sharp and uncharacteristic. Alexander was not, by nature, a contemptuous man. He was generous, empathetic, inclined to give others the benefit of the doubt. But the gap between the men who fought and the men who talked about fighting had become, for him, the central moral division of the war — more important than North and South, more important than slavery and freedom, more important than any political question. The question that mattered was the simple one: Were you here, or were you not?

*There is a great deal of talk now about drafting. I hope they will draft every man that is able to carry a musket, and I believe they will.*

He was eighteen years old, and he had the fury of a young man who has risked something real and cannot forgive those who have risked nothing.

---

Late July brought a letter that contained, buried in a paragraph of military news, a sentence that stopped the eye:

*Our First Lieutenant died last night at ten o'clock. It was a heavy blow to us all. He was a Christian Man and died like one.*

Lieutenant White. Loring E. White — the "pious young man" Alexander had mentioned in his first letter from Camp Wheeler, the man the company had elected as their first lieutenant. Dead. Not from a bullet or a shell but from disease — the invisible enemy that killed twice as many soldiers as the Confederates did.

Sergeant Sterns had been present at the death. He told Alexander he "never saw so happy a death," which meant that White had died as Christians in the nineteenth century aspired to die: conscious, reconciled, speaking words of faith in his final hours. The phrase "happy death" sounds strange to modern ears, but to Alexander and his comrades it was the highest compliment that could be paid to a dying man — not that he was brave, not that he was resigned, but that he was *happy,* that his faith had held, that the God he had worshipped in health had not abandoned him in extremity.

Alexander recorded it and moved on. He was learning the grammar of wartime grief — the brief notation, the respectful sentence, the pivot back to the living. You could not linger on each death. There were too many of them. And the rhythm of the war — the marching, the fighting, the writing, the waiting — would not pause for mourning any more than it would pause for weather.

*Luther is in the hospital, sick.*

The note was casual, almost offhand, but it carried a weight that Alexander's mother would have felt immediately. Luther was not just a name in a letter. He was Alexander's shadow, his companion, his best friend. Luther in the hospital meant Alexander alone on the guard line, alone in the camp, alone with the heat and the shells and the particular silence that fills the space where a friend used to stand.

*I am in excellent health and am one of the hardiest men in the company. "Little Doolittle."*

The nickname was new — a play on his small stature, probably given to him by the men in his company, worn with the amused pride of a man who knows his worth is not measured in inches. Alexander was small but durable. He would prove it, over and over, through four years of marching and fighting and illness and recovery. He was, as he said, one of the hardiest men in the company. The war would test that claim to its limits.

---

August 1862. The calendar turned toward the month that would end everything Alexander had known and begin everything he would become.

The armies were moving again. McClellan's Peninsula Campaign had failed. Pope's Army of Virginia was gathering in the fields of northern Virginia. Lee was about to launch his invasion of Maryland.

Alexander did not know this. He knew only what a corporal in a New York infantry regiment could know: that the mail was slow, that the heat was terrible, that his regiment was marching again, and that somewhere ahead of them, in a direction he could feel but not see, something was building.

He wrote to his mother on July 26, a letter that mixed military observations with family concerns in the way that all his letters did — as if the war and the farm, the battlefield and the kitchen table, occupied the same plane of reality, which for Alexander they did.

*Mother has no reason to blush at conduct of at least two of her sons.*

It was a proud sentence, and it was also a worried one. "At least two" — meaning himself and Henry, the two Hubbells currently in the field. The qualifier acknowledged what Alexander could not say directly: that the conduct of the other sons was yet to be tested, and that in a war where men deserted and drank and ran from battle and did all the things that Alexander's mother had forbidden, the Hubbell name was carried like a flag, vulnerable to any single member's failure.

He closed the letter with love and with the habitual reach toward home: *Remember me to Mrs. B.*

Mrs. Brinkerhoff. A neighbor, a family friend, a fixed point in the constellation of Champlain that Alexander navigated by even as the war carried him further and further from its light. He asked to be remembered to her in letter after letter, the way a sailor asks to be remembered to the harbor — not because the harbor can hear him, but because saying its name keeps him oriented in the dark.

---

The weeks narrowed toward September like a funnel.

Alexander marched. The regiment marched. The whole Army of the Potomac marched, a hundred thousand men moving north through Maryland, following Lee's army toward a confrontation that neither side fully understood was coming.

The countryside was beautiful — the orchards heavy with fruit, the corn standing tall, the stone walls running across the green hills like the seams of a garment. Alexander saw it through the sweat and dust of a forced march, through the particular tunnel vision of a soldier who has been walking for twelve hours and whose world has contracted to the pack on his back and the road under his feet and the man in front of him.

And then, somewhere in the marching, somewhere in the dust and the heat and the mechanical rhythm of boots on dirt, Alexander's regiment passed a camp. A large encampment. He hailed a passing soldier.

"What troops are encamped there?"

"Sedgwick's Division."

The name hit him like a physical blow. Sedgwick's Division. The 34th New York was in Sedgwick's Division.

Henry was here. Henry was three miles away, in a camp that Alexander could see from the road.

Alexander looked toward the tents. Somewhere among them, his brother was eating or sleeping or cleaning his rifle or writing a letter home. Somewhere among them was the "large bearded man" who had once been a clean-shaven boy at the kitchen table in Champlain, who had said *however* too often and couldn't spell *useful* and had advised his little brother to go to college instead of to war.

Alexander could not leave the column. Not now. Not during a march. But when they halted — two miles beyond — he went to the Colonel.

"Sir, I have a brother in one of the regiments we passed. I have not seen him in over a year. I request permission to go back and see him."

The Colonel looked at him. The Colonel was not an unkind man, but he was an officer, and officers could not grant leaves of absence to corporals who wanted to visit their brothers in other regiments while the army was on the march. It was against regulations. It could be construed as desertion.

"I have not the power to grant your request," the Colonel said.

Alexander felt the refusal settle into his chest. A year. He had not seen Henry in over a year.

The Colonel looked at him again. Something in the young man's face — the particular expression of a boy who is trying very hard to be a soldier and is succeeding at everything except hiding his feelings — must have moved him, because he added:

"If you are willing to run the risk, you can go."

Alexander said nothing.

He was willing to run it.

---

The moonlight was bright enough to see by.

Alexander left his camp after dark, moving quickly, passing the guards without difficulty — he knew the passwords, the protocols, the rhythm of the guard rotations, because he was a corporal who had been posting guards for months and understood the system from both sides. He was, for this one night, using his military expertise for a personal purpose, and the irony was not lost on him.

He found the encampment. He found the regiment. He found the company.

*"Is this B company?"*

*"Yes."*

*"Is there a man among you by the name of Hubbell?"*

*"Yes."*

*"Hubbell, there is somebody out here that wants to see you."*

Alexander stood in the moonlight and waited. His heart — that was his word for it, his heart "bumped" — was pounding in his chest with a force that no amount of military discipline could control. He was in a moment to see his long-absent brother. The last time they had stood face to face was September 1861, in Champlain, when the world was still intact and the war was still a thing that happened to other people.

A large bearded man stepped out of one of the tents.

It was Henry.

*The way our two hands flew together was a caution.*

Alexander laughed. He — well — he was so glad he did not know what to do. Henry's surprise was total. "Why Dick, is that you — really you. I can hardly believe my eyes."

Dick. Henry's nickname for Alexander, drawn from some childhood joke or family shorthand that the letters never explained. It was a name from another world — from the farm, from the kitchen table, from the time before the war turned them into Corporal Hubbell and Private Hubbell and set them marching on parallel roads that should never have converged but did, this once, in the Maryland moonlight.

They talked. They talked for hours, the way brothers talk when they have been apart too long — catching up, comparing experiences, trading the small news that letters could not carry. Henry was thinner. His health had been bad. His regiment had been through hard service on the Peninsula. But he was steady, pragmatic, the same Henry who had worried about money and misspelled *useful* and said *however* when he meant *but.*

Alexander would write about this meeting three months later, in a letter from Harpers Ferry, and the writing would be the most emotional passage in the entire collection — the place where Alexander's gift for observation met his capacity for feeling and produced something that transcended both.

*I laughed — I — well — I was so glad I did not know what to do.*

The dash after "I" was the most eloquent punctuation mark in 274 letters. It was the place where language failed, where the feeling outran the words, where a seventeen-year-old — no, eighteen now — stood in the moonlight and looked at his brother and could not say what he felt because what he felt was too large for the English language and too private for a letter home.

---

Dawn came.

Alexander had to go back. His regiment would be moving, and his absence would be noted, and the Colonel's tacit permission did not extend to daylight. He stood with Henry in the grey light and said goodbye.

Henry said something — the letters did not record what. Perhaps it was *take care of yourself.* Perhaps it was *write to Mother.* Perhaps it was *however,* because Henry always said *however,* and sometimes the most familiar word is the one that means the most.

Alexander left. He walked back through the camp, through the pickets, through the grey morning, to his own regiment. He fell in with his company. He shouldered his rifle.

He did not look back. Looking back was not something he did, then or ever. He was a forward-looking man, always facing the next march, the next camp, the next letter home. But as the column moved out, he carried with him the memory of his brother's face in the moonlight — the beard, the surprise, the hands that flew together — and that memory would sustain him through what was coming.

He did not know it was the last time he would see Henry alive.

Henry did not know it either.

In eleven days, they would both be on the field at Antietam — Henry with the 34th New York, Alexander with the 60th — and only one of them would walk off it.

But that was eleven days away. And for now, in the Maryland dawn, with the dew on the grass and the column moving south and the war waiting for them both, the brothers had said goodbye. And goodbye, in the grammar of the Civil War, could mean see you soon or see you never, and you did not know which until it was too late to take the word back.

Alexander marched south.

Behind him, Henry's campfire cooled to ash.
