## Chapter 15: Three Years

---

September 21, 1864.

Alexander sat in U.S. Hospital No. 2 at Chattanooga and did the arithmetic of his war.

Three years. Three years to the day since he had raised his right hand at Camp Wheeler and sworn to serve the United States of America "for the term of three years or the war." He had been seventeen then — a farm boy with clean hands and sharp eyes and a mother's Bible in his pocket. Now he was twenty, night-blind, scurvy-ridden, lying in a hospital bed a thousand miles from home, and the war was not over.

*Have you taken a realizing sense that I have now been in the U. S. Service as a soldier three years — three long — long — weary years!*

The dashes were Alexander's punctuation of emphasis — not exclamation points, which would have been too bright, but dashes, which were more like pauses, more like the intake of breath between blows. Three long. Long. Weary years.

*I can conscientiously say that I have with God's help done my best to bear that oath and do my duty as a soldier should do.*

The self-assessment was characteristic: honest, specific, delivered without false modesty. Alexander did not claim heroism. He claimed duty. He had done his best. He had borne the oath. The distinction between bearing and keeping was one that only a soldier would make — you kept a promise when it was easy; you bore one when it cost you everything.

*And now, dear Mother, I want you to join me, (and I know you will) in thanking God for his kind and watchful care over me in preserving me through so many dangers and temptations to which I have been exposed in the past terrible three years of war, and to ask a continuance of his kind care over me during the remainder of my term of service.*

The sentence was a prayer composed in a letter, a thanksgiving offered not in a church but in a hospital ward, the congregation consisting of one sick soldier and one distant mother. He asked Frances to join him — to pray with him across the distance, to synchronize their faith the way they had synchronized their letters. It was the most intimate request he ever made: not for money, not for clothing, not for food, but for prayer. Shared prayer. The last thing the war could not take from them.

*When I think of all the things which I have passed my heart is too full to speak.*

Too full to speak. Alexander, who had spoken eloquently about everything — the moonlight reunion, the shells over Harpers Ferry, the beautiful bullet at Dallas — found himself at the limits of language. Three years of war had filled his heart to the point where the words could not get out, could not squeeze through the narrow passage between feeling and expression. He had too much to say and could say none of it.

The silence was not weakness. It was the silence of a river behind a dam — enormous, pressurized, ready to break through if the dam ever gave way. But Alexander's dam held. It always held. He was a Hubbell, and Hubbells did not break.

*This is the first time since I have been in the service that I have been in a general hospital sick.*

The clarification mattered to him — he wanted his mother to understand that the hospitalization was unusual, that his record was clean, that the scurvy and the night blindness were aberrations rather than patterns. He was proud of his resilience, and the pride was justified. Three years of war and this was his first stay in a general hospital for illness. The Harpers Ferry hospital had been for the diarrhea, but Alexander categorized that differently, perhaps because it was smaller, perhaps because it was closer to the regiment, perhaps because the memory of begging for five dollars was too painful to reference even obliquely.

*There is one very singular thing about it — as soon as it is night comes I am as blind as a bat.*

Night blindness — nyctalopia — caused by severe Vitamin A deficiency, a common consequence of the wartime diet that consisted almost entirely of salt pork, hardtack, and coffee. The body, deprived of fresh food for months, began to shut down its non-essential functions, and night vision was one of the first to go. Alexander was literally losing his ability to see in the dark, and for a soldier whose duties included nighttime guard, nighttime entrenchment, and nighttime combat, the loss was catastrophic.

*However it may wear off in time. There are a number here troubled in the same way.*

He wrote it calmly, as if the condition were no more serious than a cold. The "however" — Henry's word, the word Alexander had mocked with quotation marks three years ago — appeared without irony, without quotation marks, just a word doing its work in a sentence. Alexander had absorbed his brother's verbal habits the way he had absorbed his brother's advice: unconsciously, gradually, the dead becoming part of the living through the accumulated weight of memory.

---

While Alexander lay in the hospital, the war went on without him.

Atlanta fell on September 2, 1864. Sherman's army marched into the city after four months of continuous fighting, and the fall of Atlanta was, more than any battle before it, the turning point of the war — not militarily, but politically. Lincoln's reelection, which had seemed doubtful in the summer of 1864, was secured by the capture of the Confederacy's most important city. The war would continue, but its outcome was no longer in doubt.

Alexander heard the news from his hospital bed. He did not describe his feelings about it, but they must have been complex — pride in the army's achievement, frustration at not being part of it, relief that the campaign was over, anxiety about what came next.

And beneath it all, the persistent, nagging worry about his brothers.

*The morning papers give an acct. of a heavy fight in the Shenandoah Valley. I am very anxious to hear from the boys.*

The boys. James and Charles. Fighting with the 153rd New York in the Shenandoah Valley, under Sheridan, in a campaign that was as brutal in its way as Sherman's march through Georgia. Alexander lay in Chattanooga and worried about them the way Frances lay in Champlain and worried about all three — the sick one in the hospital, the two in the Valley, the dead one in the ground.

The "heavy fight" was the Battle of Cedar Creek, fought on October 19, 1864. Alexander would not learn the details for weeks, but when they came, they would contain news both relieving and ominous: James had been wounded — a flesh wound, they said, not serious — and had walked five miles to avoid capture.

James was alive. James was wounded but alive.

James would be dead within a year.

---

October and November in the hospital. The scurvy retreating slowly, the night blindness improving with better food, the body healing itself with the grudging pace of a body that has been pushed too hard for too long.

Alexander wrote about the election. The 1864 presidential contest — Lincoln versus McClellan, the war's continuation versus a negotiated peace — was the most consequential election of Alexander's lifetime, and he had an opinion.

*Could I have voted I should have cast it "square and fair" for Abraham Lincoln.*

The transformation was complete. The boy who had written in December 1862 — *"we hate the abolitionists quite as badly as does the editor of that sheet, and we would much prefer fighting them to the Southerners"* — was now a Lincoln voter. The man who had despised abolitionists was voting for the president who had freed the slaves.

What had changed? Everything. Two years of war had changed it. The daily contact with the men who fought — including, increasingly, Black soldiers whose courage was impossible to deny — had changed it. The death of Henry had changed it. The hospital at Harpers Ferry and the hospital at Chattanooga had changed it. The friendly rebels at Ellis Ford had changed it. The whole accumulated weight of Alexander's war had pressed against his prewar convictions until those convictions cracked and were replaced by something harder and clearer.

*The majority of soldiers will vote that way.*

He was right. The soldier vote went overwhelmingly for Lincoln. The men who fought the war voted to continue it. The men who had buried their friends at Gettysburg on the Fourth of July and dug trenches every night in Georgia and written letters home with bullets overhead — those men voted for the president who said the war would not end until the rebellion was crushed and the Union was whole.

*How little the people at the North realize how much our country has cost us. It ought to be prized beyond rubies.*

He wrote that from the hospital, where the cost was measured in night blindness and scurvy and three long, long, weary years. The country had cost him his brother, his health, his youth, and nearly his life. It had cost him the farm at Champlain and the tea table and the mother he had not seen in three years. It had cost him everything except the faith that sustained him and the love that connected him to home.

It ought to be prized beyond rubies.

He meant it. The man who had enlisted at seventeen because he wanted to be present — because he could not bear to be the Hubbell who stayed home — now understood what his presence had purchased. Not glory. Not adventure. Not the beautiful Enfield and the shrieking shells. But something larger and harder and more permanent: a nation that would not break.

It had cost him everything.

He prized it beyond rubies.

---

December 1864. The hospital released him. He made his way to Nashville, where Hood's Confederate army was approaching the city.

The war was entering its final phase. Sherman had left Atlanta and was marching to the sea, cutting a swath of destruction across Georgia that would make the name "Sherman" a curse in the South for a century. Grant was besieging Lee at Petersburg. Sheridan had broken the Confederate army in the Shenandoah Valley.

Alexander, in Nashville, watched the preparations for the battle that would destroy Hood's army. He built breastworks. He heard Fort Negley's heavy guns open fire on the Confederate lines. He was a soldier again, functional if not fully recovered, doing the work that soldiers did.

*Hood is now in front of the city,* he wrote on December 5. *No heavy fighting has yet occurred here.*

Ten days later, Thomas attacked. The Battle of Nashville, fought December 15-16, 1864, was one of the most decisive Union victories of the war — Hood's army was broken, scattered, pursued south in a rout that ended the war in Tennessee.

Alexander may or may not have fought in it. His letters did not say. He was in Nashville, he was building breastworks, but whether he was in the battle line or in a support role, the letters did not specify.

It didn't matter. The battle was won. Hood was broken. The war was dying.

Alexander wrote home. He asked for letters. He sent regards to neighbors. He mentioned the officers who drank too much whiskey.

Christmas 1864. His fourth at war. His last.

He did not know it was his last. He knew only that the winter was cold and the mail was slow and the war was not yet over and his mother was in Champlain and his brothers were somewhere and Henry was in the ground and the country — the country he had enlisted to save, the country he prized beyond rubies — was almost whole again.

Almost.

Not yet.

But almost.
