# PART SIX: THE TEA TABLE
*January – July 1865*

---

## Chapter 16: The Dying of the Light

---

The Confederacy died slowly, the way a fire dies when it runs out of fuel — not all at once, but in stages, the flames shrinking, the embers dimming, the heat withdrawing from the edges toward the center until the center itself went dark.

Alexander watched it happen from a distance. He was back with his regiment, somewhere in the vicinity of Washington, watching the end unfold through the newspapers and the telegraph and the rumors that rippled through the army like wind through tall grass.

Sherman reached Savannah in December and turned north, marching through the Carolinas. Grant tightened his grip on Petersburg. Sheridan controlled the Shenandoah. The Confederate armies were shrinking — deserting, surrendering, melting away into the countryside — and the borders of the Confederacy were contracting like a puddle in the sun.

In Champlain, Frances read the newspapers and prayed. Her prayers had changed. Three years ago, she had prayed for preservation — *keep them, keep them all.* Now she prayed for ending. End it. End the war. Bring them home.

*Praise the Lord — Bless the Lord O my soul — all within me bless His holy, holy name for preserving the precious life of my darling child.*

She wrote that in January 1865, and the double *holy* and the ecstatic syntax — *all within me bless* — were signs of a faith that had been tested to its limits and had held. Three years of casualty lists, three years of waiting, one son dead, one son wounded, two sons still in the field. Frances had earned the right to praise.

---

April came. The dogwoods bloomed. The armies moved.

April 2: Petersburg fell.
April 3: Richmond fell.
April 9: Lee surrendered at Appomattox.

The news reached the army like an electrical current — a jolt that passed through every man simultaneously, transforming the army in an instant from a fighting force to a celebrating one. Men threw their hats in the air. They fired their rifles at the sky. They embraced each other — the same men who had dug trenches together and buried their friends together now holding each other in the Virginia spring, weeping, laughing, unable to believe that the thing they had been fighting for, the thing they had been dying for, was done.

Alexander did not describe this moment in his letters. The gap between his December 1864 letter from Nashville and his June 1865 letter from Washington was the longest silence in the collection — six months without a surviving letter. Whether he wrote letters that were lost, or simply did not write during this period, no one could say. The silence was its own kind of statement: some moments are too large for letters.

---

April 14.

Five days after Lee's surrender. The war was over. The nation was celebrating. Lincoln went to Ford's Theatre.

Alexander's letter did not record his reaction to the assassination. The letters recorded so much — the boiler explosion, the moonlit reunion, the beautiful bullet, the five-dollar request — but they did not record this. The death of the president he had voted for, the murder of the man who had held the nation together, the cruelest possible ending to the cruelest possible war — these events passed through Alexander's life and left no written trace.

Perhaps he could not write about it. Perhaps, like Antietam and Gettysburg, the event exceeded the medium. Or perhaps the letters from this period were among those lost to the postal system's incompetence, or to time, or to the ordinary accidents of preservation.

What we know is that Alexander was near Washington when it happened. He was a soldier in an army that had just won the war and was now mourning its commander-in-chief. He was a Lincoln voter watching Lincoln die. He was a man who had written *"How little the people at the North realize how much our country has cost us"* and who now watched the final cost extracted — the president's life, the nation's grief, the victory's taste turning to ash.

---

May and June passed in the bureaucratic limbo of demobilization. The army — which had been, for four years, the most urgent enterprise in the nation — was suddenly an administrative problem. Hundreds of thousands of men needed to be mustered out, paid, transported home. The machinery of war had to be converted to the machinery of peace, and the conversion was slow, tedious, and maddening for the men who had to wait through it.

Alexander waited. He waited near Washington, where the regiments were being consolidated and the paperwork was being processed and the generals were issuing farewell orders. He waited while the corps was broken up, while General Geary said goodbye, while General Greene — who had been wounded at Wauhatchie, who had once been colonel of Alexander's regiment — returned to take command of the brigade.

*I have been waiting to see if there would not soon be some news of our going home,* he wrote on June 16, *but I still see no immediate prospects.*

The waiting was its own kind of suffering. The war was over. His duty was done. His mother was in Champlain, his sister was in Champlain, the tea table was waiting, and he was stuck near Washington while the Army processed his paperwork with the speed and efficiency of a glacier.

But Alexander used the time. The regiment was paid. And Alexander, with the financial instinct he had inherited from Henry, made a decision that revealed how far he had come from the boy who had begged for five dollars in a hospital at Harpers Ferry.

*I expressed home yesterday three hundred and fifty dollars.*

Three hundred and fifty dollars. Four years of accumulated pay, sent home in fifty-dollar coupon bonds. It was a fortune by the standards of the time — more than a year's wages for a skilled laborer, enough to pay the taxes, fix the barn, buy seed, replace the equipment that had worn out during four years of war.

*Please say fifty to Frances.*

Fifty dollars for Fannie. His sister, who had never asked for anything, who had written the verse in his Bible and taught at the academy and held the family together from the home front. Alexander remembered her. In the midst of his own homecoming, in the rush of freedom and pay and the intoxication of having survived, he remembered to provide for his sister.

*Use as much of the money as you want — all of it if you want it.*

The sentence was the inverse of the five-dollar letter. Three years ago, he had asked his mother for money. Now he was giving it to her — all of it if she wanted it, the whole sum, the entire product of his four years of service converted into cash and sent north like a final love letter written in the language of currency.

The boy who had sent home fifteen dollars from Camp Jackson and threatened to court-martial his mother if she didn't buy a warm cloak was now a man who sent three hundred fifty dollars and said *use it all.* The trajectory was complete. The provider had been restored.

---

And then, on June 27, 1865, the order came.
