## Chapter 5: Christmas Far from Home

---

*A note on the chapter plan: The original plan called for this chapter to cover the Trent Affair and Christmas, but those events have been woven into Chapter 4. This chapter now serves as the closing of Part One, covering Frances's perspective at home and the departure of Charles and James, setting up the full family's involvement in the war.*

---

In Champlain, the winter settled in the way it always did — not gradually, the way it came to Maryland, but all at once, like a door slamming. One week the orchards still had color; the next, the world was white and grey and the wind off Lake Champlain cut through clothing as if it weren't there.

Frances rose before dawn. She had always risen before dawn — Frederick had been an early riser, and she had matched him, and after his death the habit had continued because there was no one to share the morning darkness with and therefore no reason to linger in bed. She dressed in the cold room, lit the lamp, and went downstairs to the kitchen. She built up the stove. She put water on for coffee.

Then she opened the newspaper.

This was the ritual. Every morning, before she ate, before she prayed, before she did anything else, she opened the paper and turned to the casualty lists. She ran her finger down the columns — the names printed in small type, regiment by regiment, company by company — searching for two names. Henry Hubbell, Company D, 34th New York. Alexander Hubbell, Company H, 60th New York.

When she did not find them, she closed the paper and ate her breakfast.

When the paper was late, or when the lists were long and the type was small and her eyes were tired, the search took longer, and the breakfast was cold by the time she reached it. When the paper did not come at all — when the roads were blocked by snow or the mail was delayed by weather — she did not eat breakfast. She sat at the table with her Bible open and her hands folded and waited for the next day's paper, and the waiting was a kind of fasting, a discipline imposed by fear rather than faith, though she would not have acknowledged the distinction.

She wrote letters.

She wrote to Henry, at his camp near Washington. She wrote to Alexander, at Camp Jackson near Baltimore. She wrote with the same ferocious energy she brought to everything — long letters, dense with instruction and interrogation, that demanded answers to specific questions and offered unsolicited advice on subjects ranging from personal hygiene to theology. Her handwriting was precise, almost architectural, each letter formed with the deliberation of a woman who believed that clarity of penmanship reflected clarity of thought.

*Read the 91st Psalm daily — and live up to its promises.*

She wrote that to Alexander in October, and she would write it again, in different words but with the same urgency, in virtually every letter she sent for the next four years. The ninety-first Psalm was her talisman, her shield, her substitute for the physical presence she could not provide. She could not stand beside her sons on the picket line. She could not nurse them when they were sick. She could not step between them and the bullets that would soon be flying. But she could give them the Psalm, and the Psalm was a promise from God, and God's promises did not fail.

*The Saviour is near, near you — and can hear the heart's faintest sigh. In the din of battle no artillery can drown that sigh if it is breathed in faith.*

She believed this absolutely. She believed it the way she believed in gravity — not as a theory that might be tested and found wanting, but as a fact of the universe, as immovable as the mountains. Her faith was not the shouting faith that had dismayed Alexander at the prayer meeting. It was quieter, harder, more Scottish — a granite faith, shaped by the Calvinist conviction that God was sovereign and his promises were binding and that a mother's prayer, offered in faith, could deflect a bullet as surely as a breastplate.

Whether she was right was a question the war would answer differently for each of her sons.

---

The farm went on.

This was the thing that people who had never run a farm alone did not understand: the war did not pause the seasons. The cows needed milking whether Richmond had fallen or not. The fences needed mending whether the Army of the Potomac had advanced or retreated. The taxes came due on schedule, indifferent to casualty lists, and the sugar-maker came to make his arrangements, and the neighbors came with their small crises and their large opinions, and through it all Frances managed, because managing was what she did.

She managed the finances with a precision that would have impressed a banker. She tracked every dollar that came in — Alexander's fifteen, Henry's twenty-five, the small income from the sugar-making, whatever the farm itself produced — and allocated it with the ruthlessness of a general distributing ammunition. Taxes first. Then seed. Then food. Then clothing, if there was anything left, which there usually wasn't.

She managed the correspondence like a campaign. Letters received were dated, cross-referenced, and filed. Letters sent were drafted in her head before she wrote them, the arguments organized, the questions sequenced, the emotional appeals placed strategically for maximum effect. She was, in her way, a general — commanding a dispersed force through written orders, maintaining morale through regular communication, gathering intelligence from multiple sources and synthesizing it into a coherent picture of the war as it affected her family.

And she managed her grief.

This was the hardest thing, harder than the finances or the farm or the correspondence, because grief could not be organized or scheduled or filed. It came when it came — at the breakfast table, at the post office, in the middle of the night when she woke to the sound of wind and thought, for a half-second, that it was the sound of cannon.

She did not speak of it. She did not have anyone to speak of it to. Fannie was at the academy, teaching. The neighbors had their own worries. The Reverend Mr. Copeland offered spiritual comfort, but spiritual comfort was a thin substitute for the warmth of a son's letter in your hand.

So Frances carried it alone, the way she carried everything alone, and if the weight bent her, no one saw it.

---

In January, the first rumors reached Champlain: Charles and James were going to enlist.

Frances had seen it coming. She had told Alexander in her letters — "I can see it in their faces" — and she was right. Charles was twenty-one, old enough and strong enough and quietly determined in the way that Charles was quietly determined about everything. James was eighteen, thin and bookish but possessed of a pride that would not let him be the only Hubbell brother who stayed home.

They would go together. The 153rd New York was forming, and Captain Reich — who had been Henry's commander in the 34th — was recruiting a new company. Charles and James enlisted in the same company, the same regiment, so that they could watch out for each other. This was a common arrangement in the Civil War, brothers serving together, and it was both a comfort and a particular kind of torture for the families who sent them — the comfort of knowing they were not alone, the torture of knowing that a single shell could take them both.

Frances packed their things. She wrote verses in their Bibles. She gave them the instructions she had given Alexander and Henry before them: no drinking, no gambling, no profane language. Read the ninety-first Psalm. Write often.

*Write, write, write, write, write, write, write, WRITE.*

She would write that in October, when the worst had happened and the letters were the only proof that her remaining sons were alive. But she might as well have written it now, in January, as Charles and James stood in the kitchen with their packs on the floor and their faces set in the particular expression of young men who have made a decision and will not be talked out of it and are trying very hard not to cry.

All four sons. Every one.

Frances watched them go. She did not try to stop them. She had not tried to stop Henry, or Alexander, and she would not try to stop Charles and James. She believed in the cause — she believed in it fiercely, with the conviction of a woman who had immigrated to this country and loved it with the particular passion of the chosen rather than the born. The Union was worth fighting for. The Constitution was worth defending. And if the cost of that defense was four sons marching away from the kitchen table, one by one, leaving their mother alone on a farm she could barely manage —

Well.

God had promised.

*No evil shall come nigh thee. Thou shalt only see it with thine eyes.*

She closed the door behind them.

She went back to the kitchen.

She sat down at the table — the table with five empty chairs now, Frederick's and Henry's and Alexander's and Charles's and James's — and opened her Bible to Psalm 91.

She read it twice.

Then she went to the post office to check for letters.

---

The 153rd New York shipped out in October 1862 — but that was ten months away, and in the winter and spring of 1862, Charles and James were still in training, still close enough to come home on occasional leave, still connected to Champlain by the short distances of upstate New York rather than the vast distances of Virginia and Tennessee.

For Frances, this meant a strange interregnum — two sons at war, two sons preparing for war, and herself at the center, the hub of a wheel with four spokes, each one stretching further from the center with every passing week.

She used the time. She organized. She planned. She prepared herself for the possibility that all four spokes might break simultaneously, that the newspaper might someday carry not one name but two or three or four, and that she would have to read them all before breakfast and somehow continue.

She did not prepare for what actually happened, because what actually happened was worse than anything she had imagined.

But that was September 1862. And this was still winter, still the time of preparation, still the time when hope was reasonable and the casualty lists were other people's sons.

---

Back at Camp Jackson, Alexander received his mother's letters and read them by lamplight in the barracks, and the reading was a kind of homecoming — the closest thing to it that the war allowed.

He could hear her voice in the words. He could see her hand moving across the paper, the pen dipping into the inkwell, the slight leftward lean of her script. He could picture the kitchen: the stove, the table, the window that looked south toward Frederick's stone wall. He could smell the coffee, the bread, the particular fragrance of a house that has been closed against winter and heated by wood until every surface carries the scent of smoke and warmth.

The letters made the distance both smaller and larger. Smaller, because for the minutes he was reading them, his mother was present — her voice in his ear, her counsel in his mind, her love wrapped around him as surely as the Army blanket on his berth. Larger, because when the letter ended and the voice stopped, the silence that followed was the silence of three hundred miles, of weeks of waiting, of all the things that had happened since the letter was written and that he would not learn about until the next one came.

He folded each letter carefully and kept it. He kept every letter he received during the war — from his mother, from Fannie, from his brothers, from friends and neighbors in Champlain. He carried them in his pack through every march, every camp, every hospital. He carried them to Antietam and Gettysburg and Kennesaw Mountain and through the siege of Atlanta. He carried them home.

Someone — Frances, or Fannie, or Alexander himself — later bundled them together with the letters he had sent, 274 pieces of paper that together constituted the most complete surviving record of one American family's experience of the Civil War. They were preserved, filed, stored. They survived a century and a half of attics and basements and moves and the ordinary entropy that destroys most things that people save.

They survived because someone understood that they were worth saving. That the words of a seventeen-year-old boy writing to his mother from a hemlock-board berth in Ogdensburgh, and the words of a fifty-three-year-old woman writing to her sons from a kitchen table in Champlain, were not just personal communications but pieces of history — evidence of what it cost, in human terms, to hold a nation together.

---

Alexander did not know any of this as he sat in the barracks at Camp Jackson, reading his mother's letter by lamplight. He knew only that she was alive, that she was well, that she was praying for him, and that she wanted him to read the ninety-first Psalm.

He opened his Bible. He found the page. The verse Fannie had written on the flyleaf caught his eye as he turned: *God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble.*

He read the Psalm.

*He that dwelleth in the secret place of the Most High shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty.*

Outside, the Maryland night was settling over Camp Jackson. The sentinels were walking their posts. The trains were running on the B&O, carrying supplies west, carrying wounded east. Somewhere to the south, in Virginia, armies were gathering in the dark. Somewhere to the north, in Champlain, his mother was sitting at her table, reading her Bible, praying the same Psalm.

*A thousand shall fall at thy side, and ten thousand at thy right hand; but it shall not come nigh thee.*

Alexander closed the Bible. He set it beside his berth. He pulled the blanket over himself and closed his eyes.

In four months, he would hear shells shriek over his head at Harpers Ferry and write home about it with the excitement of a boy and the precision of a soldier.

In six months, he would stand on the field at Antietam, searching for his brother, and not find him.

In a year, he would be in a hospital, sick and broke and alone, writing the most painful letter of his life, asking his widowed mother for five dollars.

But tonight — this night in the winter of 1862 — he was warm enough, and fed enough, and loved enough, and the Psalm was a blanket over the blanket, and the distance between Camp Jackson and Champlain was, for the duration of his mother's letter, exactly the width of a folded piece of paper.

He slept.

---

*In Champlain, Frances extinguished her lamp and climbed the stairs to bed. The house was quiet. The wind had died. The newspaper on the kitchen table lay folded to the casualty page, and beside it, arranged in order of receipt, were the latest letters from her sons — Henry's from Virginia, Alexander's from Maryland, and soon, the first letters from Charles and James.*

*She had four sons in the war. She had a farm to manage and a God to trust and a Psalm to pray and a morning paper to read before breakfast.*

*She lay in the dark and listened to the house settle around her — the creak of old wood, the tick of the clock in the hall, the faint, almost inaudible sound of wind in the orchard where Frederick's stone wall held the earth in place.*

*She thought of her boys. She thought of them as she always thought of them: not as soldiers, not as privates or corporals or the future dead, but as children. Her children. The ones she had carried and nursed and raised and released into a world that was trying to tear itself apart.*

*She prayed. Not the shouting prayer that Alexander had witnessed in the barracks, but the quiet prayer of a woman who has said the same words so many times they have worn smooth as river stones.*

*Keep them. Keep them all. Bring them home.*

*And if you cannot bring them all — she did not finish the thought. She could not. Some prayers are too terrible to complete.*

*She closed her eyes.*

*Morning would come, and with it the paper, and with it the lists, and with it the terrible daily question that every mother of soldiers must answer before she can eat her breakfast: Are they still alive?*

*Frances slept.*

*The answer, for now, was yes.*
