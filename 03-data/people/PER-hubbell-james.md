## Notes

James Hubbell is the family's most poignant arc: the bright youngest soldier-brother, the "one educated soldier in the family," appointed to West Point in 1862 with his mother's hopes and a patron's help. His own letters — headed "West Point Military Academy" — describe cadet life in detail and, decisively, name two men the external record confirms were really there: Cadet Lieutenant **Peter S. Michie** and Chaplain-Professor **John W. French**. He did not graduate; sometime in 1863 he left the Academy, and in March 1864 he enlisted as a private in Company I of the 153rd NY — his brother Charles's company. He drilled recruits, came through Third Winchester unhurt, and was wounded at Cedar Creek on October 19, 1864. He survived to the regiment's muster-out at Savannah in October 1865 — and then died at Albany on **October 12, 1865**, on his way home, "in the service of his country," and was buried beneath the four-brothers memorial his church raised at Glenwood Cemetery in Champlain. Of the four Hubbell soldiers, James is the one who came closest to coming home. The record confirms his identity, his wound, his two social worlds, and his grave; the formal West Point admission papers and the cause of his Albany death are the last threads, both behind archive walls.
