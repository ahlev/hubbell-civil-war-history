Born March 28, 1844 in Champlain, New York. Enlisted September 1861 at age seventeen — ignoring his older brother Henry's advice to stay home and go to college. Wounded at Antietam, hospitalized twice, survived scurvy during the Atlanta Campaign. Mustered out June 27, 1865. Died December 7, 1894 on his farm at Fonda, Iowa. The only brother to live to old age.

Wartime Footprint

"What a Bedlam!"

September 1861 — Ogdensburgh, New York

Alexander was seventeen when he enlisted, barely two weeks after Henry wrote home advising him to stay and go to college instead. His first letter from the barracks at Ogdensburgh captures a boy dropped into chaos — straw berths, armed sentinels, and a half-drunk fifer requesting music at all hours.

Here I am safe and sound in the barracks. How strange it does seem. Ah! what a bedlam! September 20, 1861 — Ogdensburgh

He reassured his mother that the gingerbread she packed "went well." He was the youngest of the four brothers, and the one who would write the most — 120 letters across nearly four years, more than the other three combined.

Baltimore and the Enfield Rifle

November – December 1861 — Camp Jackson, Maryland

Alexander's November 13 letter is the richest single document in the collection — a nine-day journal of the regiment's journey from Ogdensburgh to Camp Jackson, three miles outside Baltimore. He described the flag presentation in New York City, the breakfast served by women in Philadelphia at three in the morning, and the moment the train crossed into Maryland: five rounds of cartridges were distributed, but no caps. The regiment entered Baltimore armed but unable to fire.

At Camp Jackson, the men received Enfield rifles and sword bayonets. They named the place "Starvation Heights." Alexander, who had never been more than a few miles from Champlain, was finding his voice as an observer. He was also finding his faith.

I try to keep my promise to you "to do nothing of which you would not approve." I feel that God is very near me. I sometimes feel very lonely, but I think of Him and you fighting for me and I feel better. I think of all of you often, very, very, often. November 13, 1861 — Camp Jackson, Baltimore

By December, he had witnessed a boiler explosion that threw an engineer "two or three hundred feet into the air" and was commenting with heat on the Trent Affair: "I am as ready to fight Britons as Southerners."

Historical Context

The Trent Affair (November–December 1861) was the closest the United States and Great Britain came to war during the conflict. On November 8, Captain Charles Wilkes of the USS San Jacinto stopped the British mail steamer RMS Trent and forcibly removed two Confederate diplomats, James Mason and John Slidell, bound for London and Paris to seek foreign recognition of the Confederacy. Britain was outraged. The Royal Navy prepared a fleet, 11,000 troops were dispatched to Canada, and an ultimatum was drafted. War fever ran high on both sides — Alexander's hot declaration that he would fight Britons as readily as Southerners was typical of Northern sentiment. Lincoln's cabinet quietly released the prisoners in late December, defusing the crisis. Had Britain intervened, the Confederacy would have gained the world's largest navy as an ally, and the Hubbells' war would have become something far larger and far more dangerous.

First Combat

May – July 1862 — Harpers Ferry and Virginia

The 60th New York moved to Harpers Ferry in the spring of 1862, and Alexander's letters shifted. In late May he wrote to his mother that the regiment was sleeping on their arms. Rebel shells were falling within ten rods of their position. He was no longer a boy marveling at barracks bedlam. He signed his letter for the first time with his rank: "(Corporal Hubbell) Alexander."

It does me good to hear our shells shriek and howl over our heads. If the rebels do attack us, there will be an awful battle. My life is in God's hands. Such is war. May 31, 1862 — Harpers Ferry

By July, he was calling himself "one of the hardiest men in the company" and raging against men at home who paid for substitutes rather than enlist: "They are what I call fireside patriots. They are brave enough at home but let them come out here and you would see them whine." He was eighteen years old.

But the same month produced what may be the finest passage in the entire collection. Writing to his sister Frances from a hilltop near Warrenton, Alexander set aside the soldier and became, briefly, a painter.

“Way down South in Dixie.” That is where I am. And a beautiful country too if it were not cursed by the existence of slavery. I am encamped on the summit of a high hill or rather a mountain from which I can see a long distance and the view is very fine. Green fields, meadows, large farms, patches of woods, roads winding here and there, little villages in the distance, and all of this spread out before me like a beautiful painting. July 14, 1862 — Pine Mountain, near Warrenton, Va.
I was sitting on the hill just now watching the sun as it was setting behind the Blue Ridge. The soft notes of the bugle were floating through the air when I almost forgot I was a soldier. I seemed for a moment to be transported to the green fields and quiet scenes of old Clinton County. But then again I thought if the drum should beat to arms tomorrow morning, there will be a different scene. Troops will be forming in line of battle. The sun will be glistening on the bright steel of ten thousand breastplates. The artillery will be wheeled into position and the skirmishers will be thrown out, and then the earth will fairly rock. continued
Historical Context

Harpers Ferry changed hands eight times during the war — more than any other town. Situated at the confluence of the Potomac and Shenandoah Rivers where Virginia, Maryland, and West Virginia meet, it was a strategic chokepoint controlling access to the Shenandoah Valley. The 60th New York was stationed there as part of the garrison defending this perpetually contested crossroads. In September 1862, Stonewall Jackson would capture the garrison of 12,500 men in the largest Union surrender of the war — a disaster that occurred just two days before Antietam. Alexander's regiment narrowly avoided that fate by being reassigned before the siege.

Antietam

September 17–27, 1862

On September 17, 1862, Alexander fought at the Battle of Antietam — the bloodiest single day of the war. His letter two days later reported that he came through "safe and sound," with only a bruise below the right knee from a nearly spent ball. The 60th New York had gone into the fight about two hundred strong and lost between thirty and forty men.

But the letter carried worse news. Henry was missing.

I only wish I could report the same of Henry. I saw one of the 34th this morning and he said that he is missing, also that he thought he was quite sure that he was not killed. September 19, 1862 — Camp near Antietam

Two days later Alexander added a postscript: "I have not heard from Henry since writing the above. I hope and think he is yet all right. Do not worry." It was the last hopeful thing anyone would write about Henry.

The Hospital and the Five Dollars

September 27, 1862 — Hospital at Harpers Ferry

Within days of the battle, Alexander collapsed. Weeks of severe diarrhea compounded by the "severest military duty" had broken him. He was carried to the hospital at Harpers Ferry, where Orderly Dickinson bought him a loaf of bread with his own money. Alexander's letter to his mother from the hospital is the emotional center of the entire collection.

You know, Mother, what it is to be away from home, sick among strangers and to be without money. I will not try to describe my feelings when I ask for this... Do you suppose you could spare me five dollars, if not, less will do. I will leave you to judge how painful it is for me to ask this of you. If you have not got it to spare do not send it. I do not wish you to deny yourself. September 27, 1862 — Hospital at Harpers Ferry

In the same letter, he reported that Henry had been found — "slightly wounded in the hand, but is still with the company." This report, received secondhand, would prove wrong. By December, Alexander had learned the truth.

Thanksgiving and Fireside Patriots

November 1862 — Harpers Ferry

Alexander's Thanksgiving letter is his most introspective. He described what it felt like to stand in battle while thinking of his mother praying for him at home. He raged at men who avoided service by paying substitutes. And he catalogued his Thanksgiving meal — bean soup, boiled pork, and hardtack — while imagining what his grandmother was serving back in Champlain.

I felt when in the battle while the shot and shell were shrieking and whirring around me, as tho I had a Mother upon her knees praying for me and I took great comfort in the thought. It seemed as tho God's arm was wrapt around me. November 25, 1862 — Harpers Ferry

"They Saw Henry Grasp His Leg"

December 1862 — Fairfax Station, Virginia

In a December 21 letter, Alexander relayed the only eyewitness account of Henry's wounding: soldiers from the 34th New York had seen Henry grasp his leg, take a step or two, and throw himself down. It was a leg wound, not a hand wound — the earlier report had been wrong. Henry was not heard from again. No body was recovered.

In the same letter, Alexander's politics showed a sharp edge. He and his comrades, he wrote, "had the abolitionists quite as badly as does the editor of that sheet, and that we would much prefer fighting them to the Southerners." It was a view that would change completely by 1864.

Chancellorsville

May 1863 — Near Fredericksburg, Virginia

In May 1863, Alexander's regiment marched toward Chancellorsville. His letter of May 11 described the aftermath — seventy-three casualties in the regiment, three charges, the Eleventh Corps breaking and running. Alexander had been on guard detail and missed the battle itself. In the event, Alexander was detailed to guard the baggage train and missed the battle itself. It was one of the last times fortune would spare him anything.

Gettysburg

July 1–4, 1863 — Culp's Hill, Pennsylvania

On the afternoon of July 2, 1863, the 60th New York went into action on Culp's Hill, the rocky wooded height anchoring the right flank of the Union line at Gettysburg. The regiment had been under artillery fire for hours before the infantry fighting began at two o'clock. It did not stop until midnight. They fought again nearly all the next day. When it was over, the regiment had lost fifty-two men — eleven killed, the rest wounded. Alexander was not among them.

He wrote to his mother on July 4 — Independence Day, with the dead of both armies still lying in the fields around him.

God has again in his great kindness preserved my life through another most terrific battle. I have not received a scratch. The reg't. lost fifty-two — eleven killed and forty-some wounded. We are burying our and the rebel's dead today. July 4, 1863 — Gettysburg

Four days later, from Crampton's Gap in Maryland, he wrote again with the details a soldier saves for after the shock has passed. The 60th had captured two Confederate battle flags. They had stretched seven rebel officers out in a row in front of the company. "We beat the rebels gloriously in that battle," he wrote, then added, with the quiet fatalism that was becoming habitual: "My life is in God's hands. His will be done."

The fullest account came weeks later, tucked into a postscript dated July 30. The rebels had charged Culp's Hill with hoots and yells — the famous rebel yell that had broken other lines at other battles. It did not break Alexander's.

I had the satisfaction of firing about fifty or sixty shots into the very teeth of the rebels at Gettysburg. They charged on us with hoots and yells, but stopped their clack pretty quick. They did screech, but it was out of the other corner of their mouths. July 30, 1863 — Warrenton Junction

In the same letter, he noted that the regiment was down to about two hundred men. He was broke and asked his mother to send money, if she could — "be careful and send no other than U.S. money, as no other is good here." He was nineteen years old.

Historical Context

Culp's Hill was the scene of some of Gettysburg's most intense fighting on July 2–3, 1863. The 60th New York, part of the XII Corps, defended the wooded, boulder-strewn slope against repeated Confederate assaults. The position held, securing the Union right flank and contributing to the broader victory. Total Union casualties at Gettysburg exceeded 23,000; Confederate losses were comparable. Alexander's regiment suffered disproportionately — fifty-two casualties from a unit already thinned by two years of war. His letter of July 4, written while burying the dead on Independence Day, is among the most striking documents in the collection.

Henry's Ghost

July – August 1863 — Maryland and Virginia

What happened next was something Alexander had not expected. In the days after Gettysburg, the army pursued Lee's retreating Confederates south through Maryland. On July 8, from Crampton's Gap, Alexander realized where he was: seven or eight miles from the Antietam battlefield, where Henry had been killed the previous September. "If possible, I shall visit it and endeavor to find out something about Henry," he wrote.

He did not get the chance. The march continued. But weeks later, writing to his sister Frances on August 25, Alexander described what had happened during that march — one of the most quietly devastating passages in the entire collection.

During the march in Maryland, I passed over a part of the battle ground of Antietam, where we were at one time during the day and about half a mile from where we and the 34th fought. Oh! how I longed to leave the ranks and hunt for some trace of Henry, but it was impossible. August 25, 1863 — Ellis Ford

He had marched within half a mile of where his brother had fallen. He could not stop. The column moved on, and the ground where Henry had lain for ten months passed behind him.

The Rappahannock

August – September 1863 — Ellis Ford, Virginia

By late August, the 60th New York was on picket duty along the Rappahannock River, watching Confederate soldiers a hundred yards away on the opposite bank. The two sides had arrived at a weary equilibrium. Alexander described it with something close to bewilderment in a September 3 letter: "We sit and look at each other very composedly." The rebels wanted to trade newspapers; orders forbade it. "If the settling of the war was left to the soldiers," he told his brother Charles, "we would do it in short order."

He added, with genuine surprise, that "some of the Rebs are pretty nice fellows." It was not the kind of thing he would have written a year earlier, when he hated abolitionists and rebels in roughly equal measure. Something was shifting.

The same letter to his mother contained the most emotionally raw passage Alexander ever committed to paper.

Mother, could you for a moment suppose I could ever forget my old home and my Mother? Never, never. I love my Mother better than life itself. September 3, 1863 — Ellis Ford
Historical Context

Informal fraternization between enemy pickets was one of the war's strangest phenomena. Along rivers like the Rappahannock, where opposing lines were close enough to shout across, soldiers routinely traded coffee for tobacco and held conversations. Officers prohibited it but rarely enforced the ban. Alexander's surprise at finding Confederates "pretty nice" marks a turning point in his letters: the boy who had raged against fireside patriots was beginning to see the enemy as human. This shift would deepen through the Atlanta Campaign and culminate in his valedictory, where he spoke not of defeating the South but of standing as "a free and united nation."

Lookout Mountain

October – December 1863 — Tennessee

In the fall of 1863, the 60th New York was transferred west to the Army of the Cumberland, and Alexander entered a new theater of war entirely. By late October, the regiment was holding a pass in the Cumberland Mountains, fifteen miles below Chattanooga. The fighting came without warning. On the night of October 28, at Wauhatchie, five regiments of the division were attacked in a furious pre-dawn assault. The rebels followed so close behind the picket line that the camp was barely alarmed before volleys crashed into the tents. General George S. Greene, the brigade commander — who had held Culp's Hill at Gettysburg — was seriously wounded. Lieutenant Geary, son of the division commander, was killed.

Alexander survived Wauhatchie. One month later, his luck ran out.

On November 24, 1863, the division was chosen to storm Lookout Mountain — the "Battle Above the Clouds." Writing to his brother Charles on December 13, Alexander described the assault in the candid language soldiers reserve for each other, not for mothers.

I hope you may never be called on to climb such a hill as that. We were often required to halt ourselves on with our hands, which you know, in the face of the enemy is not over pleasant. December 13, 1863 — Wauhatchie

They climbed the mountain hand over hand, under fire, and charged with bayonets at the top. The rebels broke and ran. Eighteen hundred prisoners were taken. But Alexander did not walk down the mountain. A minié ball struck him in the left side.

His first letter from the hospital at Tullahoma, written December 1, delivered the news to his mother in the understated way that had become his habit.

I was wounded at the storming of Lookout Mountain. I am wounded in my left side by a minie ball. Rather a close call for my life, I think. December 1, 1863 — Hospital, Tullahoma

In the casualty list he sent home, he included himself in the third person: "Sergeants Hubbell and Long, Corporals Ish and Kelly, Private Sidney Rider, wounded." Private George Hays was killed. Private Thomas Small had his leg torn off. The wound kept Alexander in hospital through the winter, and he missed the fighting at Missionary Ridge and Ringgold that followed days later.

From the hospital, his mother had written asking about a discharge. Alexander's reply was measured but clear: "There is no one, I think, but the chief magistrate of the U.S. has power to grant it. I am anxious to pursue my studies — very anxious — but it will seem hard to leave the rest of the boys to fight it alone." He would not leave. He still had a year and a half of war ahead of him.

Historical Context

The Battle of Lookout Mountain (November 24, 1863) was part of the broader Chattanooga Campaign to break the Confederate siege of the city. Fought on the steep, fog-shrouded slopes of the 2,392-foot mountain, the assault became known as the "Battle Above the Clouds." The XX Corps attacked up a slope so steep that soldiers used trees and rocks to pull themselves upward. The Confederate position, though seemingly impregnable, was taken in an afternoon. The next day, Grant's forces stormed Missionary Ridge in one of the war's most dramatic charges. Together, the battles of Lookout Mountain and Missionary Ridge broke the Confederate hold on Chattanooga and opened the road to Atlanta. Alexander's wound — a minié ball in the left side that he called "rather a close call" — was his second serious injury of the war.

The Blues at Nashville

January 1864 — Nashville, Tennessee

In early 1864, Alexander did something he had never done before: he deserted his detachment — not to go home, but to rejoin his regiment. He had been left behind at Chattanooga and did not like "the way things worked," so he left clandestinely to find the 60th New York, knowing he would be reported as a deserter. "I determined to get to the regt. if it took a leg," he wrote.

The same letter contained his only recorded admission of despair: "I feel quite sad tonight. I suppose Frances would say that I have the blues." After two and a half years of war, one brother dead, and a body weakened by chronic diarrhea, the weight was showing.

The Atlanta Campaign

May – June 1864 — Georgia

In May 1864, Sherman's armies began their advance on Atlanta, and Alexander was in the middle of it. His letters from this period describe war as industrial grinding — fighting nearly every day, entrenching through the night, driving the enemy from one fortified line only to find another.

We have been fighting nearly every day since I last wrote you and working the greater portion of every night entrenching ourselves in positions gained the previous day. The list of killed and wounded in our regt. is beginning to pile up fearfully. June 20, 1864 — Near Marietta, Georgia

His May 8 letter noted that three brothers were now in combat simultaneously in two theaters — Alexander in Georgia, James and Charles in Virginia. He asked his mother: "Have you heard from James and was he in that terrible fighting in Va.?" — a reference to the Battle of the Wilderness.

The June 20 letter, written one week before the assault on Kennesaw Mountain, carried the tone of a farewell. "If God orders it otherwise may we all meet in heaven." He closed with what reads as a veteran's political testament, written by a man who was not sure he would survive the summer: "How little the people at the North realize how much our country has cost us. It ought to be prized beyond rubies."

Historical Context

Sherman's Atlanta Campaign (May–September 1864) was a 130-mile advance through northern Georgia, fought against Joseph Johnston's Confederate army in a continuous series of flanking movements, entrenchments, and assaults. Sherman commanded three armies totaling nearly 100,000 men. The fighting was relentless — Dalton, Resaca, New Hope Church, Kennesaw Mountain — with the armies rarely more than a few miles apart. The 60th New York, in the XX Corps, fought through this grinding campaign. Union casualties exceeded 31,000 over four months. The fall of Atlanta on September 2 transformed the 1864 presidential election and ensured Lincoln's re-election.

Scurvy, Lincoln, and a Vote

August 1864 — Hospital near Atlanta

The campaign broke Alexander's body for the second time. By August he was hospitalized with scurvy — the result of weeks without fresh vegetables during the advance. The surgeon told him he was very young to have it.

From the hospital, Alexander underwent a political transformation. In 1862, he had written that he hated abolitionists as much as rebels. Now, in 1864, he was ready to vote for Lincoln.

F. A. Cook is at Champlain, and he is hot for Lincoln. I will hoist my colors soon. If I live I shall cast a vote this fall. You know I will have been in the service over three years when the election comes off, and according to the laws of the U.S. I shall be of proper age to vote, as no one is received into the U.S. Service under 18. August 22, 1864 — Hospital near Atlanta
Historical Note

Alexander had enlisted at seventeen, below the minimum age. By the muster rolls, however, he would be twenty-one by election day — old enough to vote. His shift from anti-abolitionist to Lincoln supporter mirrors a broader transformation among Union soldiers in 1864, as the war's purpose evolved from restoring the Union to ending slavery.

"Three Hopeful Sons"

August 30, 1864 — Hospital, 20th Army Corps

In late August, Alexander learned that all three surviving brothers were hospitalized at the same time — himself with scurvy near Atlanta, James with exhaustion at Frederick City, and Charles in a convalescent department near Harper's Ferry. Their mother, who had already lost one son and spent two years fearing for the others, received this news. Alexander's response was characteristically his own.

Please excuse me, Mother, but I did have a hearty laugh when I took in the idea of your three hopeful sons being in the hospital at the one and same time. August 30, 1864 — Hospital near Atlanta

He warned her about chronic diarrhea — "It is a terrible disease" — and reported that Atlanta was about to fall. Sherman's army had swung south toward Macon. Three days later, the city was taken.

The Valedictory

June 27, 1865 — Near Alexandria, Virginia

The war ended in April 1865. Alexander waited two more months for the order to muster out. When it finally came on the night of June 26, he wrote to his sister Fannie with the finest passage he ever composed — a valedictory for the entire war, written by a twenty-one-year-old sergeant who had served since he was seventeen.

When I left home nearly four years since our country was racked from its base to its center by one of the most violent and gigantic rebellions recorded on the pages of history. We knew not friends from foes. Treason walked our legislative halls and had command of our armies. Now by the providence of God, after four years of unparalleled struggling we stand a free and united nation, than which in point of honor we have no superior and in point of freedom, no equal. June 27, 1865 — Near Alexandria, Virginia

He would be discharged at Ogdensburgh — the same place he had enlisted, the same barracks where he had marveled at the bedlam nearly four years before. "That's a happy day that will be, eh, Miss Fannie."

Historical Context

The Grand Review of the Armies in Washington (May 23–24, 1865) marked the symbolic end of the war. Over 200,000 soldiers marched down Pennsylvania Avenue past the White House in a two-day parade — the Army of the Potomac on the first day, Sherman's western armies on the second. Alexander's XX Corps marched with Sherman's men. After the review, regiments were sent to mustering-out camps near their home states. Many soldiers, like Alexander, waited weeks for the paymaster to settle their accounts before they could go home. The war had cost the Union over 360,000 dead and 275,000 wounded. Alexander had served for nearly the full duration — three years and ten months.

The Tea Table

July 27, 1865 — Camp Wheeler, Ogdensburgh

Alexander's last letter is the shortest he ever wrote. The regiment had arrived at Ogdensburgh and was waiting for the paymaster. He was bringing a friend named Luther home for a visit. There had been a grand reception. He did not describe it.

I am very anxious to see you all again. I will tell you all about it at the tea table. July 27, 1865 — Camp Wheeler, Ogdensburgh

After the war, Alexander settled on a farm in Fonda, Iowa. He married Lois Wood in 1867. They had four children. He lived quietly for nearly thirty years — the only Hubbell brother to reach old age. He died on December 7, 1894, at fifty. His daughter-in-law Gladys would one day open a trunk in a basement, find his letters, and begin transcribing them.
