The Hubbell Brothers

Four brothers. Four years of war. Two hundred and seventy-two letters across the distance between them.

This rare family collection has been brought to life by a descendant, using modern data science and interactive technology. Their words, their connections, and the humanity of experiencing the war, brought to life in ways the letters alone never could before…

The Civil War as experienced through their eyes and minds.

“‘Way down South in Dixie.’ That is where I am. And a beautiful country too if it were not cursed by the existence of slavery. I am encamped on the summit of a high hill and the view is very fine. Green fields, meadows, large farms, patches of woods, roads winding here and there… all of this spread out before me like a beautiful painting.

I was sitting on the hill just now watching the sun as it was setting behind the Blue Ridge. The soft notes of the bugle were floating through the air when I almost forgot I was a soldier…”

Alexander Hubbell · Co. H, 60th N.Y. Pine Mountain, VA · July 14, 1862 · (Age 18)

“…But if the drum should beat to arms tomorrow, there will be a different scene. Troops will be forming in line of battle… the skirmishers will be thrown out, and then the earth will fairly rock.”

Alexander Hubbell · Co. H, 60th N.Y. Pine Mountain, VA · July 14, 1862

The Letters

What they wrote home.

Their father, Frederick Augustus Hubbell, had died in 1853, when Alexander was nine. Frances McNeil Hubbell raised four sons and a daughter alone in Champlain, on the Canadian border of New York. When the call came for volunteers in 1861, she sent all four.

What followed was not the heroic correspondence of myth — young men writing to their mother about being sick among strangers, about money begged from home, about the ache of months without a letter, about the long grinding weeks between the battles.

What makes them extraordinary is not any single revelation but the accumulation of ordinary detail across four years. Read together, they become something larger — an unedited record of what the Civil War felt like from inside it.

The Preservation

A trunk in a basement. Five generations of care.

Bundled away after the war, water-stained and nearly lost — then transcribed, introduced, digitized, and finally analyzed, each by a different descendant, each in a different decade. The last of them was named at Gettysburg — his parents chose the family name on the field where the first Alexander fought.
