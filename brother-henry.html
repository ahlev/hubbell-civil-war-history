Born November 1839 in Champlain, New York. Enlisted June 1861 at age 21. Killed at the Battle of Antietam, September 17, 1862. His body was never recovered.

Wartime Footprint

Arrival

July 1861 — Washington, D.C.

Henry was the first Hubbell brother to enlist. In June 1861, two months after Fort Sumter, he joined the 34th New York Volunteers and traveled by boat and rail from Albany to Washington. He was twenty-one years old. His father had been dead for eight years. His mother was managing the farm in Champlain alone.

His first letter home captures a young man trying to make sense of what he has entered. The regiment was cheered all the way from Albany. In Baltimore — where soldiers of the 6th Massachusetts had been attacked by a secessionist mob just weeks earlier — Henry noted bullet holes in the depot roof but reported that no one molested them. What troubled him was not the enemy but his own side.

Historical Context

Henry's passage through Baltimore was freighted with recent violence. On April 19, 1861 — less than three months earlier — soldiers of the 6th Massachusetts had been attacked by a pro-Confederate mob while transferring between rail stations. Four soldiers and twelve civilians were killed in what became the first bloodshed of the Civil War. The incident made Baltimore the most dangerous transit point for Union troops moving south. Regiments that followed were routed through the city under armed guard, often at night, with loaded weapons but sometimes — as Henry's regiment experienced — without percussion caps, rendering their rifles useless. The bullet holes Henry saw in the depot roof were physical evidence of the April riot, still unrepaired when his train passed through in July.

It seems to me I never got into such a drunken set as I have here, however there are some that don't drink. July 6, 1861 — Washington, D.C.

From his first letter, Henry established what would become his constant concern: money. He had sent eight dollars home and wanted to know if it arrived. He apologized for bad spelling. He found the knapsack very heavy, but said he would get used to it. These small, practical observations — the weight of things, the cost of things — would define his correspondence for the next thirteen months.

The Cannons at Bull Run

July 21, 1861 — Washington, D.C.

On July 21, 1861 — the day of the first major battle of the Civil War — Henry sat in camp at Washington and wrote to his mother while listening to the cannons.

While I am writing there is a battle going on. Can here cannons firing not over twenty-eight miles from here. Am not certain when our Regt. will go. Wherever we go I am ready. Know I shall not be forsaken if I put my trust in the all seeing power. July 21, 1861 — Washington, D.C.

That same day, he wrote separately to his sister Frances. The letter was ordinary — drilling five hours a day, wishing for something to read, asking her to send a dollar so he could get a shirt washed. Then came the postscript, written later that evening, after the Union defeat at Bull Run had sent shockwaves back to Washington:

Later — We are ordered off. Shall probably leave in the morning. Ready tonight. May leave before morning. There has been a hard battle today. Have not heard much about it yet. You need not send any money now, till I write. July 21, 1861, later — Washington, D.C.

The whiplash between the two halves of this letter — shirt laundry, then mobilization — would become a recurring pattern in the Hubbell correspondence. The ordinary and the catastrophic occupied the same page.

Historical Context

The First Battle of Bull Run (July 21, 1861) shattered the North's expectation of a short war. Union forces under General McDowell advanced toward Manassas Junction expecting an easy victory; instead, Confederate reinforcements arrived by rail — the first strategic use of railroads in warfare — and routed the Federal army. Panicked soldiers and civilian spectators fled back toward Washington. The defeat stunned the nation and triggered a massive mobilization: Lincoln called for 500,000 three-year volunteers. Henry's regiment, the 34th New York, was part of the wave of troops rushed to defend the capital in the days that followed.

Camp Life, Money, and a Brother's Advice

September 1861 – January 1862

Henry's regiment was stationed as guards along the Potomac, watching for rebel crossings. His letters settled into a rhythm of camp routine, financial accounting, and concern for the family at home. He was meticulous about money — tracking every dollar sent home, every debt to the sutler, every contribution to the band fund and the cook. In September, he told his sister he had a five-dollar gold piece and intended to send nearly everything home, adding: "You must not hesitate, I put that down as a command. Times are hard and you will need it bad enough."

He slept on the ground, which he believed was making him ill. He watched one soldier die from another's carelessness with a loaded gun — the third accidental death in the regiment. He heard that his younger brother Alexander was eager to enlist, and in a September 11 letter to his mother, he counseled firmly against it: "My opinion and advise is he had better stay at home, and I think I am wright. If he can get a good chance to go to College, he had better go." Alexander did not take the advice. He enlisted two weeks later.

The camp was not safe even from itself. In October, a drunken soldier stabbed another man at midnight; the victim lived until morning. "Rum is not allowed in Camp," Henry wrote. "If any one is found with it he is severely punished. I don't know how that man came to get it, but the gallows will not be to hard for him." He was learning the violence of boredom — the gambling punishments, the loaded guns in careless hands, the moral rot that came with idleness. "A man cannot live in Camp and maintain good morals unless he is very strict and firm," he told his mother. "There is so much swearing and gambling that it is enough to ruin any young man."

And yet, running through the grim reports, there were flashes of something gentler. "There is not a day that passes but what I think of you all," he wrote. He dreamed of being at home twice, "but my dreams are not quite long enough for on wakening in the morning I find that I am not quite there yet." He told his sister he had been a little homesick but had gotten over it. He sometimes thought he had done wrong to leave, knowing his mother would need him that winter. "But when I do get home," he promised, "I shall stay."

By November, the regiment had moved to Camp McClellan near Poolesville, Maryland. Henry sent twenty dollars home by express. One night the regiment formed a line of battle after a report that rebels were crossing the river, but it was a false alarm. He asked his brother Charles to tell their mother that her preserves "went very well."

In January 1862, Henry reported that the barracks had been torn apart and burned for firewood — they were thought unhealthy — and the men would live in tents for the rest of winter. He sent fifteen dollars home, itemizing his expenses to the cent: $5.20 to the sutler, $4.50 for boots, fifty cents to the band, fifty cents to the cook, a dollar for a sword. He was optimistic. "I think this war will be all over before next July," he wrote. Then he added a postscript that would become the foundational act of the entire collection: "Please burn all my letters." The family did not comply.

Historical Context

Henry's meticulous accounting — dollars to the sutler, cents to the cook — reflects the peculiar economy of a Civil War camp. Sutlers were licensed civilian merchants who set up shop within each regiment, selling tobacco, canned fruit, writing paper, pocket knives, and other goods the Army did not provide. Prices were steep: a can of condensed milk might cost fifty cents when a private earned thirteen dollars a month. Soldiers could buy on credit, with debts deducted from their pay at muster, which meant many men saw only a fraction of their wages. For a family like the Hubbells — a widowed mother managing a farm with no sons at home — every dollar Henry sent back represented real survival. His obsessive tracking of expenses was not personality; it was necessity. The allotment system, formalized in 1861, allowed soldiers to direct a portion of their pay home by express or through regimental paymasters, but the system was unreliable. Henry's repeated question — "did the money arrive?" — echoed across hundreds of thousands of families dependent on soldier wages.

Harpers Ferry

February – March 1862 — Virginia

In late February, the 34th New York broke camp and marched south toward Harpers Ferry. The first day covered twenty miles. Henry described the march in a letter to his mother: "You may be sure that I felt tired at night and was thankful to get a hard cracker and cold water, salt junk, for that was all we could have." They slept on the ground that night with nothing but the sky for a covering. Sleep, he reported, was out of the question — it was very cold.

When they arrived at Harpers Ferry, the destruction startled him. "It really made me feel bad to see what distruction the rebles have done in this place. Several buildings burnt. Nothing but the brick walls remain." The town had changed hands multiple times since the war began, and each army had left its mark. Henry was beginning to see what the war actually looked like on the ground — not the parades and cheering crowds of his journey south, but charred walls and empty streets.

He wrote to his mother from the march: "Nothing does me more good than to have a letter from home." To his brother James, he recalled making maple sugar together the previous spring. "I thought then it was rather hard," he admitted, "but it is nothing to what I have been through, or may."

The Siege of Yorktown

April 1862 — Virginia Peninsula

In early April, the regiment shipped south to the Virginia Peninsula as part of McClellan's massive advance on Richmond. Henry wrote to his mother from Hampton: they had traveled nearly two hundred miles in two days, crammed together with hardly room to lie down, eating nothing but hard crackers. Hampton itself was worse than Harpers Ferry — "every building burnt to the ground."

As they approached the Confederate lines at Yorktown, the tone of his letters changed. The camp routine fell away. He wrote to his brother James on April 6 about going on a double quick for a mile, expecting a fight. To Charles on April 18, he described the siege with an excitement that reads, in hindsight, like innocence: "Nothing livens me up more than to hear the firing cannon and musketry. The rebels will ketch it before the war is over." At night they lay with rifles ready. The cannon was fired every half hour to let the enemy know they were there. The drums had been silenced — "for fear that the rebels will find us out and throw shells into our camp."

When Yorktown was finally evacuated in early May, Henry walked through the abandoned Confederate works and found something that enraged him: buried shells, designed to explode when stepped on. "The rebels left shells for us to step on. Some of our men were hurt by them. They are the most cowardly enemy I have ever heard of." These were among the first land mines used in warfare. Henry had no word for them. He only knew what they did.

Historical Context

The Siege of Yorktown (April 5 – May 4, 1862) was the opening phase of McClellan's Peninsula Campaign. Rather than assault the Confederate earthworks, McClellan chose a deliberate siege, bringing up heavy guns over the course of a month. The Confederates evacuated before the bombardment began. The buried shells Henry described — called "torpedoes" at the time — were an early form of land mine, ordered by Confederate General Gabriel Rains. Their use provoked outrage on both sides; even Confederate General Longstreet condemned them as "not proper" warfare. They were, however, a harbinger of the industrialized killing that would define the rest of the war.

Fair Oaks and the Slow Decline

May – June 1862 — Near Richmond, Virginia

On May 31, the Confederates attacked at Fair Oaks, just miles from Richmond. Henry's regiment was in the thick of it. His account to his mother was characteristically precise: 123 killed and wounded, not the 150 the newspapers claimed. His company lost two killed and ten wounded. "I cannot be too thankful coming out without being injured," he wrote. A rebel prisoner told them he did not understand how the Yankees could fire so fast and load.

But what the battle did not do, the Peninsula itself was doing. By June, the regiment that had mustered nearly a thousand men could put only five hundred on duty. Henry himself was breaking down. In his June 13 letter, he reported he had been sick for three days — could not keep anything down, the doctor gave quinine and pills, but they did not help. By June 22, the dysentery was very bad. He could not eat hard bread. He could not afford a ten-cent newspaper. He went to the doctor most every morning.

How I long to have one good meal at home, even if it wasn't anything but potatoes and salt, anything but hard crackers. I can not bear to look at them. I was just thinking what a good dinner I should have if I was at home today, but there are better days coming I hope. June 22, 1862 — Near Richmond

In the same letter, he wrote the most dejected words of his entire correspondence: "I should like nothing better than to have my discharge, for your sake if nothing more. I have had about enough of it and long to be at home." Then, catching himself: "I for one am about disgusted with soldiering, but I shall try to make the best of it." To his brother Charles, he went further — confessing the one thought a soldier was never supposed to admit: "I am almost tempted to desert sometimes. I feel so lonesome. If it was not for the name of it, besides Mother being all alone." He did not desert. He stayed.

He mentioned that Alexander's regiment, the 60th New York, was said to be on its way. "Alexander will get tired of it when he comes to march as much as I have," he wrote. "I shall be very glad to see him." The brothers never met in the field. Five days after this letter, Lee attacked, and the Seven Days began.

The Seven Days

Late June – July 1862 — Harrison's Landing, James River

On June 25, Lee launched the Seven Days Battles — seven consecutive days of combat that drove the Union army from the gates of Richmond to a defensive camp at Harrison's Landing on the James River. Henry's letters grew shorter and more direct. The tone shifted. In a letter dated July 12, he described the chaotic retreat, the bayonet charges, the men who shirked the fighting. He had been urged by a comrade named McDonald to go to the hospital when the sick were ordered away, but he refused to leave. The battle began at five in the afternoon, and his regiment charged into the woods on the double quick, throwing their knapsacks. Henry lost his cap.

Our regiment fired very low and did great execution. The Irish Brigade charged a rebel battery — it was a splendid sight, shouting at the top of their voices. We lost a great many men, and the battery, ammunition, and provisions. We marched all the next day in a hard rain without a blanket. July 12, 1862 — Harrison's Landing, James River

He noted that his diarrhea was "very bad" and that the head surgeon had left for Congress. He had now served thirteen months. If he completed his full term he would receive a hundred-dollar bounty and a land warrant. He asked his mother to write often.

Historical Context

The Seven Days Battles (June 25 – July 1, 1862) were Lee's first major offensive as commander of the Army of Northern Virginia. Seven consecutive days of combat drove the Union army from the gates of Richmond to a defensive camp at Harrison's Landing on the James. The 34th New York fought at Gaines' Mill and Malvern Hill. Union casualties across the Seven Days exceeded 15,000. Disease — particularly chronic diarrhea caused by contaminated water and poor sanitation — was already killing more soldiers than combat; the Army of the Potomac reported over 25,000 men sick in July alone. Henry, weakened by weeks of dysentery, fought through it all rather than report to the hospital. He had two months left to live.

The Last Letter

July 28, 1862 — Harrison's Landing

Henry's final surviving letter was written to his sister Frances on July 28, 1862 — fifty-one days before the battle that would kill him. It is the most relaxed letter in his collection. He wrote about the grand review of the Army of the Potomac near the James River, where they were marched in line of battle and the battery fired blank cartridges. He thought an artist had sketched them. "You may perhaps see the sketch in the Harper's Weekly," he told Frances.

He teased her about teaching French. He teased a friend named Sid about farming and marriage. He offered a small fantasy of peace:

I should like it very well if peace should be proclaimed just before Sugar time, or in the fore part of next winter, so that I could make arrangements for farming next spring, but there is no such luck for me, as matters and things look now. I shall make the best of it. July 28, 1862 — Harrison's Landing

He closed by asking Frances to give his regards to all friends. His last written instruction to his family was this: "Burn this scribble — hope to do better next time."

They did not burn it.

Forty-Three Days of Silence

No letters from Henry survive between July 28 and September 17, 1862. The 34th New York moved north from the Peninsula to join Pope's army, then marched into Maryland as Lee's forces crossed the Potomac. On September 17, at the Battle of Antietam — the bloodiest single day of the war — Henry was wounded. He did not survive. His body was never recovered.

The Family Learns

September 21–28, 1862

What survives instead is the anguish of those who waited for news. On September 21 — four days after the battle — Henry's mother sat in Champlain and wrote to him, not yet knowing he was wounded. She had been reading the published casualty lists "with trembling," scanning for his name. The 34th New York had been "terribly cut up." But his name had not appeared.

Mother's Voice

Language is too weak, to express my anxiety about you since the commencement of the terrible battle. Than God your name has not yet appeared on the sad list, which has caused morning and lamentation to so many homes.

If I was a man I am sure I should enlist, but as it is I have sent all I had to send and hope they will do nobly.

Frances Hubbell to Henry — September 21, 1862, Champlain

She was writing to a son who was already dying, or dead. The dramatic irony is total. She asked him to describe the battle "in the most minute detail." She told him she was "almost tempted at times to go down to the hospitals." She reported that Charles had been made first corporal. She praised McClellan. The remainder of the letter is missing.

Six days later, Alexander — himself hospitalized with severe diarrhea at Harpers Ferry — wrote to their mother with what he had heard: Henry was "slightly wounded in the hand, but is still with the company." This report, passed along second-hand, would prove either wrong or tragically incomplete.

The next day, September 28, Mother saw Henry's name appear on the published wounded list. She wrote to Alexander in anguish, begging him to find Henry, to administer to his wants, to get him a furlough home.

Mother's Voice

I was shocked this morning by seeing Henry's name on the list of wounded. I cannot tell you of the agony of mind I am in. Want of means alone prevents my going starting for Washington.

My heart is aching to bursting, and I can write no more.

Frances Hubbell to Alexander — September 28, 1862, Champlain

None of these letters reached their destinations in time. They crossed in the mail, each written in ignorance of what the others said. Henry never learned of his mother's desperate attempts to reach him. She never reached him. In time, the family pieced together the truth. Charles would later write, plainly: "I think there is not the least doubt that Henry was killed."

Historical Note

The Battle of Antietam on September 17, 1862 produced 22,717 casualties in a single day — the bloodiest day in American military history. The 34th New York fought in Sumner's Corps near the West Woods and the Sunken Road. Henry Hubbell was among the approximately 2,100 Union soldiers killed outright. His body was never recovered and his grave, if marked at all, has never been identified.

What Remains

Henry Hubbell served thirteen months. He wrote about knapsacks and money, about preserves and French lessons, about cannons heard from twenty-eight miles away and a lost cap in a bayonet charge. He dreamed of making sugar in the spring and farming when the war was over. He tried to keep his younger brother from enlisting. He sent nearly every dollar home. He marched through the burnt ruins of Harpers Ferry and the mined earthworks at Yorktown. He watched his regiment shrink from a thousand men to five hundred. He refused to go to the hospital when his body was failing. He longed for one good meal at home, even if it was nothing but potatoes and salt.

His final request — "burn this scribble" — was ignored. Five generations later, every letter survives. They are the only record of his voice.
